The group that trained the brain improved its endurance by 24%, versus 12% for the group that trained only the body. That figure, published by Neil Dallaway and colleagues in the *European Journal of Sport Science* (2023), captures an idea that exercise physiology has been debating for a decade: the limit of endurance performance lies not only in the muscles or the heart, but also in the perception of effort the brain generates. If that perception can be trained, performance should follow it. Brain endurance training (BET) is the most direct attempt to put that hypothesis to the test.

The premise stems from the psychobiological model of endurance popularized by Samuele Marcora. According to this framework, we stop pedaling when perceived effort reaches a tolerable maximum and the task seems to demand more than we are willing to give, not necessarily when the muscles fail. Prior mental fatigue—answering demanding emails, a day of intense concentration—raises that perceived effort and brings forward the moment of giving up. The logical question is the inverse: if the brain gets used to operating fatigued, would it tolerate more before calling for the brakes?

## The Limit Is (Also) in Your Head

Perceived effort, not lactate, is what the model places at the center of the decision to stop. This does not deny peripheral physiology: acidosis, glycogen depletion, and temperature still matter. What the psychobiological model proposes is that those signals reach the brain and are integrated into a single conscious variable—how hard it is to keep going—which is what truly determines the end of an open-ended effort. Reducing that perception at the same intensity amounts, in practice, to being able to sustain more watts for longer.

Mental fatigue behaves like a measurable weight on that perception. In endurance tasks, arriving mentally tired does not change heart rate or oxygen uptake at a given power, but it does raise perceived effort and worsen performance. BET turns that phenomenon around: instead of avoiding mental fatigue, it induces it on purpose and repeatedly over weeks, seeking an adaptation that cushions its effect, just as muscle adapts to a progressive load.

## What the Cyclists Did

The road cyclists who tried BET improved their 20-minute time trial. Walter Staiano, Samuele Marcora, and colleagues published in the *Journal of Science and Medicine in Sport* (2023) two randomized controlled trials in which participants trained five times a week for six weeks. The Post-BET group completed cognitive response-inhibition tasks after each physical session, when already tired, while the control group listened to neutral sounds for the same amount of time. The idea was to stack mental load on top of physical load, not to substitute one for the other.

The results pointed in the same direction in both studies. In the first, time to exhaustion improved significantly more in the Post-BET group both at 80% of peak power (p = 0.032) and at 65% (p = 0.011), with lower perceived effort. In the second, the Post-BET group improved its 20-minute time trial versus control (p = 0.031) and reported lower perceived effort during a 60-minute submaximal test (p = 0.034). The pattern is consistent: performing more while feeling less, which is exactly what the model predicts.

## Doing It "Before" Also Works

The protocol of Dallaway et al. (2023) reversed the order and obtained the 24% improvement. Instead of adding the cognitive task at the end, this *European Journal of Sport Science* trial placed it before each session: for five weeks, four days a week, the BET group did 20 minutes of cognitive training—a 2-back working-memory task and a Stroop test with color-word interference—right before exercise. Both groups did the same physical work. The only difference was the prior mental load.

The oxygenation of the prefrontal cortex offered a clue to the mechanism. Dallaway et al. (2023) measured higher prefrontal oxygenation during the post-test exercise tasks in the BET group versus control (p < 0.05), suggesting that the cortex involved in effort control was working differently after training. An honest caveat is in order: this trial used a handgrip endurance task, not cycling, so it demonstrates the principle but does not guarantee that the transfer to the bike is identical. It is the combination with the Staiano et al. (2023) study, done directly on cyclists, that makes the idea relevant to those who pedal.

## Why It Would Work

The leading hypothesis is adaptation to mental fatigue. Repeatedly exposing the brain to exhausting cognitive tasks appears to reduce perceived effort at a given physical intensity, so the cyclist reaches the ceiling of tolerance later. It is not about generating more aerobic power—oxygen uptake and heart rate barely change—but about shifting the psychological limit that decides when you ease off the gas. In a race, that translates into holding a wheel for a few more seconds at the moment that defines the result.

The fine mechanism, however, is not yet settled. The signal of higher prefrontal oxygenation is compatible with a neural adaptation in the regions that regulate effort, but it could also reflect changes in attention or in task strategy. The available studies do not yet allow these explanations to be fully separated, and the authors themselves frame it as an open avenue rather than a closed case.

## How to Apply It Without Going Overboard

The simplest way to try it is to add 15 to 20 minutes of a cognitive task to sessions you already do. Stroop-type tests or n-back working-memory exercises, available in free apps, reproduce the stimulus used in the studies. Placing them after a quality session mimics the Staiano et al. (2023) protocol; doing them before mimics that of Dallaway et al. (2023). Starting with two or three exposures a week and ramping up slowly avoids accumulating a fatigue that ends up harming sleep or the physical training itself, which remains the foundation.

Managing total load is the key warning. BET adds stress, it does not subtract it, so it makes more sense in specific blocks before a goal than as a permanent routine without rest. For a cyclist who already sleeps poorly or runs short on energy, piling on exhausting mental homework can be counterproductive. Like any training load, it needs progression, periodization, and recovery weeks.

## What We Still Don't Know

The samples in these studies are small, a limitation worth not hiding. The trials of Staiano et al. (2023) and Dallaway et al. (2023) worked with small groups and five-to-six-week protocols, enough to show an effect but not to pin down the optimal dose or the durability of the adaptation. Larger-scale replication is missing, in women, across different performance levels, and with tasks more like the real demands of a race, where tactical attention competes with fatigue.

The reasonable conclusion is that BET is a promising, low-cost lever, not a magic bullet. The available evidence consistently points to training tolerance for mental fatigue reducing perceived effort and improving endurance, with gains that in the lab doubled those of physical training alone. For a cyclist who already optimizes watts, sleep, and nutrition, devoting a few minutes to tiring out the head on purpose may be one of the few avenues left to gain meters where races are decided: in the head, just before the legs say enough.
