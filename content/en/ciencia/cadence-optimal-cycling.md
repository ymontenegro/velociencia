## The 90 RPM paradox

On flat Tour de France stages, professional cyclists maintain an average cadence of 89 to 92 RPM. Lucía et al. (2001) monitored the cadences of elite riders across three Grand Tours — the Tour, the Giro, and the Vuelta — and recorded those values on flat roads and individual time trials, compared to just 71 RPM on the major mountain climbs. The finding is puzzling when contrasted with metabolic evidence: oxygen consumption studies consistently show that pedaling at 60–65 RPM at submaximal power outputs requires less oxygen per watt produced than doing so at 90 RPM. If professionals know their craft better than anyone, why do they choose a cadence that is, on paper, less efficient?

The answer is not simple and has occupied exercise physiology for four decades. Behind cadence selection, at least three systems compete to impose their own particular optimum: the metabolic, the neuromuscular, and the biomechanical. The point where these three systems reach a compromise is neither fixed nor universal — it depends on power output, accumulated fatigue, individual muscle fiber distribution, and years of experience on the bike. Understanding how these factors interact allows for more evidence-based decisions about training and race strategy.

## The first map: optimal cadence and power output

Coast and Welch (1985) published the study that gave cadence research its first mathematical framework. Five trained cyclists completed progressive tests to exhaustion at five different cadences: 40, 60, 80, 100, and 120 RPM. Analysis of the data showed that the cadence yielding the lowest metabolic rate for a given power output was not constant — it increased linearly with work rate. At 100 W, the most economical cadence was around 50–55 RPM; at 250 W, it had risen to 75–80 RPM. The relationship was linear and statistically robust.

Foss and Hallén (2004) confirmed and extended that finding with six elite cyclists using a protocol combining submaximal and maximal tests at 60, 80, 100, and 120 RPM. The most economical cadence — defined as the one producing the lowest oxygen uptake for a given power — shifted from 60 RPM near zero load to 80 RPM at 350 W. Published in the *European Journal of Applied Physiology*, the study added an important nuance: at maximal power outputs, a higher cadence allowed cyclists to develop greater peak power, even when the metabolic cost per watt was marginally higher.

The practical implication is clear: there is no single optimal cadence. Any statement like "90 RPM is the correct value" ignores the fact that that number only makes sense in relation to the power being produced. At 150 W the most economical cadence differs substantially from the one corresponding to 300 W for the same cyclist.

## What professionals do versus what metabolism suggests

The study by Lucía et al. (2001) with professional cyclists in competition revealed a systematic gap between the metabolically optimal cadence and the freely chosen one. On flat stages, riders averaged 89.3 RPM; in time trials, 92.4 RPM; on major mountain passes, 71.0 RPM. The work was published in *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise* and analyzed thousands of kilometers of real racing, with 7 elite cyclists distributed across the three Grand Tours.

At the power outputs professional cyclists produce on flat stages — between 300 and 380 W for elite riders — the most economical cadence according to Foss and Hallén's data hovers around 77–80 RPM, not the 89–92 that Lucía observed. The gap of 10–15 RPM cannot be explained by energy metabolism. Marsh and Martin (1997), publishing in *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise* with 32 subjects divided into groups by experience, aerobic power, and work rate, found that more trained cyclists consistently preferred higher cadences than recreational riders under identical power conditions — and that this preference did not coincide with their most economical cadence. Experience shifted the system toward higher RPM progressively and independently of metabolism.

## The role of muscle fiber type

Muscle fiber distribution acts as a second determinant of preferred cadence. Ahlquist et al. (1992) had eight subjects pedal for 30 minutes at 85% of their VO₂max at two cadences: 50 RPM and 100 RPM. Biopsy analysis of the vastus lateralis showed that high cadence (100 RPM) selectively depleted glycogen from type I fibers — the slow, oxidative ones — while low cadence (50 RPM) forced greater recruitment of type II fibers, the fast and glycolytic ones. Published in *European Journal of Applied Physiology*, the study demonstrated that cadence is not physiologically neutral with respect to the fiber type being recruited.

Hansen et al. (2002) measured the freely chosen cadence in a group of cyclists whose fiber composition had been determined by muscle biopsy. Freely chosen cadence varied between 56 and 88 RPM across subjects, and that variability correlated significantly with the percentage of myosin heavy chain type I (%MHC I) in the vastus lateralis. A higher proportion of slow fibers was associated with a higher preferred cadence. The physiological mechanism is coherent: type I fibers reach their peak mechanical efficiency at higher contraction velocities than type II fibers. A muscle dominated by slow fibers extracts more useful power per cycle at elevated cadences, pushing the preference toward higher RPM even when the metabolic cost increases.

The implication is direct for individual variability: two cyclists with identical VO₂max and FTP may have very different optimal cadences if they differ in their proportion of type I fibers. The cyclist with a more aerobic muscle profile — more common among pure climbers — will tend to perform better at high cadences even at a slightly higher metabolic cost. The profile with more fast-twitch fibers — more typical of time trialists and sprinters — will achieve greater muscle efficiency at more moderate cadences.

## The neuromuscular hypothesis: why professionals choose high cadences

The explanation that best resolves the 90 RPM professional paradox comes not from metabolism but from neuromuscular fatigue. Lepers, Millet, and Maffiuletti (2001) evaluated eight well-trained triathletes who completed 30 minutes at 80% of maximal aerobic power at three different cadences: their freely chosen cadence, that cadence minus 20%, and that cadence plus 20%. Before and after each trial, they measured the contractile and neural properties of the quadriceps via electrical stimulation of the femoral nerve. The publication in *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise* showed that the higher cadence produced less fatigue in quadriceps contractile force parameters at the end of the effort, despite a marginally higher metabolic cost.

The underlying mechanism is mechanical: at higher cadences, the force the muscle must exert on each pedal stroke to maintain the same power is lower. Power is the product of force and velocity — if the angular velocity of the pedal increases, the required force decreases proportionally. Pedaling at 90 RPM instead of 60 RPM at the same power reduces force per cycle by one third. That reduction in force decreases mechanical stress on muscle fibers, attenuates antagonist co-activation, and preserves contractile capacity for the final phases of a prolonged effort. In a five-hour race with attacks in the final 40 kilometers, the muscle that has accumulated less mechanical stress responds better at the decisive moment.

This hypothesis also explains why professionals drop to 71 RPM on the major mountain passes. Steep gradients force lower absolute power outputs — even elite cyclists produce between 350 and 450 W on a climb, compared to more variable powers on flat stages — and in that range the most economical cadence descends toward the 70–75 RPM threshold according to the Coast and Welch models. Furthermore, the inclined position on the bike on a steep gradient alters muscle geometry and reduces the ability to generate high angular velocity efficiently. Mountains push cadence downward through both metabolic and biomechanical factors.

## Cadence is not universal: what it means for the average cyclist

Marsh and Martin (1997) detected a mean difference of 8–10 RPM between inexperienced cyclists and those with several years of structured training, under identical power and aerobic fitness conditions. Novice cyclists tended to choose cadences closer to the metabolically optimal value for a given power. Experienced cyclists opted for higher cadences, implicitly prioritizing neuromuscular economy over metabolic economy. The study included 32 subjects across three groups and suggested that the adaptation toward higher cadences occurs progressively with training, possibly as a result of optimization of motor patterns and muscle recruitment strategies.

For a recreational cyclist producing 150–200 W during typical rides, the metabolically optimal cadence according to Foss and Hallén's data falls between 60 and 70 RPM. However, maintaining 80–85 RPM in that power range — even though it costs 3–5% more oxygen — can be a reasonable metabolic investment if the goal is to preserve muscle capacity for the second half of a long ride or the final climb of the route. The additional metabolic cost of pedaling at a higher cadence at low power outputs is small; the benefit in accumulated muscle fatigue can be significant in efforts exceeding 90 minutes.

Conversely, cyclists doing short, intense efforts on flat terrain — criteriums or short time trials — have different incentives. At 350–400 W, the metabolically optimal cadence already hovers around 80–85 RPM and the gap between metabolic and neuromuscular efficiency narrows. Under those conditions, differences between 85 and 95 RPM in terms of oxygen cost are small, and the choice can be guided primarily by individual biomechanics and motor comfort.

## Limitations of current knowledge

The majority of foundational studies on optimal cadence used small samples — between five and thirty subjects — in laboratory settings with electromagnetic brake ergometers, conditions that do not perfectly reproduce the variability of real road cycling. Foss and Hallén worked with six cyclists; Ahlquist with eight; Lepers with eight triathletes. The effects of irregular terrain, group riding dynamics, and tactical intensity variation on preferred cadence are poorly studied under ecological conditions. Lucía et al. contributed the largest dataset from real competition, but their analysis was observational and could not experimentally control the interaction between cadence, power, and gradient.

Muscle fiber composition is largely genetic in origin and difficult to modify substantially through training, which limits how far the individual cadence optimum can shift with practice. The most robust practical recommendation emerging from the literature is that preferred cadence should adapt to the work rate, that cyclists with more years of training will naturally gravitate toward higher cadences, and that no single value applies to all intensity and duration contexts.

## What the science says for better pedaling

Optimal cadence in cycling is not a number but a function. It depends on power output, effort duration, individual muscle profile, and the type of competition or training. Coast and Welch established in 1985 that the most economical cadence rises linearly with power, and Foss and Hallén confirmed nearly two decades later that this shift is consistent even in elite cyclists. Lucía demonstrated that the best cyclists in the world choose cadences 10–15 RPM higher than the metabolically optimal values for their racing power outputs — most likely because preserving the muscle is more valuable than saving a few milliliters of oxygen per minute.

For a cyclist training with a power meter and recording cadence, the most direct recommendation from the available evidence is to calibrate RPM to the power being produced and the terrain. Cadences of 75–85 RPM make sense in zone 3 and zone 4 efforts; moving to 85–95 RPM for above-threshold efforts on flat terrain can reduce accumulated muscle fatigue without a prohibitive metabolic cost. Steep climbs will naturally push cadence downward — fighting that descent with an overly short gear is as unproductive as pedaling unnecessarily slowly on flat ground.

Two cyclists with identical FTP values can differ by 10–15 RPM in their optimal cadence if their fiber composition is opposite. A cyclist with predominantly slow-twitch fibers should not force themselves to pedal at 70 RPM for metabolic efficiency reasons if their neuromuscular system performs better at 90. Another with a faster-twitch profile may find that 75–80 RPM is more sustainable during long stages. Power meter data, heart rate tracking, and the perception of muscle fatigue at the end of long rides are the best instruments for finding that individual equilibrium point.
