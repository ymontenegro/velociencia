## The natural laboratory of the mountains

Egan Bernal prepared for his 2019 Tour de France victory with a training block at 2,600 meters in his native Colombia. Tadej Pogačar regularly attends training camps in Livigno at 1,816 meters, combining them with high-intensity sessions at lower elevations. Jonas Vingegaard trained in Tignes at 2,100 meters before his Grand Tour campaigns. The pattern repeats across virtually every WorldTour team: altitude remains a central tool in professional cycling preparation, and science has spent decades trying to explain why it works and how to optimize it.

The fundamental question is simple: can the human body produce more red blood cells when exposed to an oxygen-depleted environment, and thereby carry more oxygen to the muscles upon return to sea level? The short answer is yes, but the full answer involves complex physiological mechanisms, surprising individual variability, and an ongoing debate about protocols, timing, and optimal doses.

## The hypoxia cascade: from kidneys to erythrocytes

When a cyclist ascends to 2,500 meters, the partial pressure of oxygen in inspired air drops from the usual 159 mmHg at sea level to approximately 130 mmHg. Oxygen sensors in the kidneys detect this decrease and activate the hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) pathway, a family of proteins that orchestrates the body's transcriptional response to oxygen scarcity. The best-known result of this cascade is increased production of erythropoietin (EPO), the hormone that stimulates bone marrow to manufacture more red blood cells.

Garvican et al. (2012) documented this response in eight elite cyclists exposed to 2,760 meters of natural altitude (with five sea-level controls). EPO levels surged 64% after just two nights at altitude. However, that initial spike did not persist: EPO concentration returned to baseline values after 12 nights, a phenomenon physiology explains as stabilization of the new equilibrium between oxygen production and consumption. What did persist was the cumulative effect on hemoglobin mass, which grew by 2.9% during the first 11 days and reached a 3.5% increase after 19 days of exposure. That gain translates to more circulating hemoglobin, greater oxygen uptake capacity in the lungs, and more substrate available for working muscles.

The equation governing this process can be expressed simply: maximal oxygen consumption ($\dot{V}O_{2max}$) equals cardiac output multiplied by the arterio-venous oxygen difference. By increasing hemoglobin mass, the second term of that equation is amplified, raising the aerobic ceiling without needing to modify cardiac function.

## The experiment that changed the rules: Levine and Stray-Gundersen

Benjamin Levine and James Stray-Gundersen published in 1997 the study that established the dominant paradigm of altitude training. Their design was elegant in its simplicity: 39 competitive runners (27 men, 12 women) were randomly assigned to three groups after a two-week preparation period at sea level. The "high-low" group lived at 2,500 meters and trained at 1,250 meters. The "high-high" group lived and trained at 2,500 meters. The "low-low" group lived and trained at 150 meters as the control.

The results were unequivocal. Both altitude groups increased their $\dot{V}O_{2max}$ by 5%, in direct proportion to a 9% increase in red cell mass. But only the high-low group improved their actual performance in a 5,000-meter trial: 13.4 seconds faster than before the camp. The high-high group, despite identical hematological adaptations, showed no improvement in race time. The explanation was physiologically logical: training at 2,500 meters forced a reduction in absolute work intensity, because the thin air limits the ability to generate maximal power. Living high provided the hypoxic stimulus; training low preserved training quality. The "Live High-Train Low" (LHTL) model was born.

Four years later, Stray-Gundersen, Chapman, and Levine (2001) replicated the protocol with 22 elite runners—athletes with a mean $\dot{V}O_{2max}$ of 72.1 ml/kg/min, a level that leaves little room for improvement. Yet after 27 days living at 2,500 meters and performing high-intensity training at 1,250 meters, the group improved their $\dot{V}O_{2max}$ by 3% (to 74.4 ml/kg/min), their hemoglobin rose by 1 g/dl, and their 3,000-meter performance improved by 1.1%. One-third of participants achieved personal bests. EPO levels nearly doubled within the first 20 hours of altitude exposure, confirming the speed of the endocrine response.

## The dose matters: neither too low nor too high

Chapman et al. (2014) addressed a question that previous studies had left unresolved: what is the optimal altitude for living during an LHTL camp? They distributed 48 collegiate runners into four groups based on their living altitude: 1,780, 2,085, 2,454, and 2,800 meters. All trained together at varying altitudes between 1,250 and 3,000 meters over four weeks.

The results defined an optimal window with precision. Only the groups living at 2,085 and 2,454 meters significantly improved their 3,000-meter time trial upon returning to sea level. The 1,780-meter group did not accumulate sufficient hypoxic stimulus to trigger relevant erythropoietic adaptations. The 2,800-meter group, paradoxically, also failed to improve: excessive altitude compromised sleep quality, increased physiological stress, and caused nocturnal oxygen desaturation that interfered with recovery. The science was clear: the 2,000 to 2,500-meter range maximizes the ratio between adaptive stimulus and recovery capacity.

Camp duration also has an established minimum. Studies agree that at least three weeks of continuous exposure are needed to produce a significant increase in hemoglobin mass. Garvican et al. (2012) demonstrated that after 300 hours of exposure at 2,760 meters, elite cyclists had consolidated a measurable increase. Shorter camps—of one or two weeks—may generate ventilatory benefits and improvements in muscle efficiency, but they do not complete the erythropoietic cycle. The evidence-based recommendation is a minimum of four weeks at 2,000–2,500 meters, with at least 20 hours of daily exposure to the hypoxic environment.

## The myth of the permanent responder

One of the most relevant findings of the past decade challenges a deeply held belief in the training world: the idea that some athletes are consistently "responders" and others are permanent "non-responders" to altitude. Nummela et al. (2021) analyzed data from 59 elite endurance athletes across 82 altitude training camps conducted between 1,350 and 2,500 meters. Of athletes who participated in at least two camps, only 27% showed consistently positive hemoglobin mass responses. Thirteen percent had consistently negative responses. But the most revealing figure is that 60% showed both positive and negative responses across different camps.

Intra-individual variability proved nearly as large as inter-individual variability. The same athlete could increase hemoglobin mass by 5% in one camp and show no change in the next, even with similar protocols. Factors modulating the response included actual camp altitude (positive responses rose to 65% when altitude exceeded 2,000 meters), the athlete's iron status, prior training load, sleep quality, and accumulated physiological stress. Male athletes showed greater hemoglobin mass increases than female athletes, likely due to baseline differences in iron stores and erythropoiesis.

Płoszczyca, Langfort, and Czuba (2018), in a narrative review published in *Frontiers in Physiology*, confirmed this inconsistency. Despite the theoretical benefits of altitude training on hematological variables, actual effectiveness varies according to hypoxic dose, training regimen, athlete conditioning level, and natural variability in EPO production. The authors concluded that permanent classification of an athlete as a responder or non-responder lacks scientific support.

## The performance window: when to race after descending

Perhaps the most practical question for a professional cyclist is not whether altitude training works, but when to descend for competition. Chapman, Stickford, Lundby, and Levine (2014) published a specific review on return timing, identifying that the optimal response depends on the interaction of three factors that deacclimatize at different rates: hematological, ventilatory, and biomechanical.

Hemoglobin mass gained at altitude is maintained for two to three weeks after descent, offering a relatively wide hematological window. However, during the first days at sea level, altitude ventilatory adaptations persist—such as residual hyperventilation and respiratory alkalosis—which can interfere with performance. Additionally, fluid retention and plasma volume expansion upon descent temporarily dilute hemoglobin concentration, a phenomenon known as hemodilution that masks actual erythrocyte gains during the first 48 to 72 hours.

Current practice among professional cycling teams points to two main strategies. Some teams prefer to compete within the first three days after descent, taking advantage of the fact that ventilatory adaptations still maintain elevated tone. Others wait seven to fourteen days, allowing hemodilution to resolve so that hemoglobin values faithfully reflect the erythrocyte mass gained. Mujika, Sharma, and Stellingwerff (2019), in their review on altitude training periodization, noted that this decision remains more art than science and that future research must establish with greater precision the deacclimatization kinetics of non-hematological factors.

## Simulated altitude: tents and hypoxic chambers

Not every cyclist can relocate to the mountains for a month. Hypoxic tents and simulated altitude chambers offer an alternative to partially reproduce altitude conditions at the athlete's home. These devices reduce the fraction of inspired oxygen through nitrogen generators, simulating altitudes between 2,000 and 4,000 meters without changing ambient barometric pressure.

The evidence for their effectiveness is less consistent than for natural altitude training. Several studies have compared intermittent hypoxic training with equivalent normoxic training, and results do not always favor hypoxia. The main limitation is exposure time: sleeping in a hypoxic tent provides seven to ten hours of daily stimulus, compared to 20 or more hours of exposure from permanent residence at altitude. Saunders et al. (2009) documented that the contribution of hemoglobin mass to performance improvements induced by simulated LHTL was real but modest, and that the magnitude depended critically on total accumulated hours in hypoxia.

The difference between normobaric hypoxia (tents) and hypobaric hypoxia (actual altitude) also generates debate. At natural altitude, beyond the reduction in oxygen fraction, total barometric pressure is lower, which affects gas diffusion in the lungs. Some researchers argue that natural altitude produces a more complete stimulus. Others maintain that the differences are marginal if the total hypoxic dose is comparable. What science accepts is that tents can serve as a useful complement—especially for accumulating exposure hours between natural altitude camps—but are not an equivalent substitute.

## Risks and practical limitations

Altitude training is not without risks. Dehydration is more pronounced at altitude due to increased respiratory water loss and diminished thirst stimulus. Sleep quality deteriorates above 2,500 meters, with increased nighttime awakenings and reduced deep sleep—factors that directly compromise recovery. The risk of overtraining increases because the same wattage produced at sea level generates a greater relative physiological load in a hypoxic environment.

Iron deficiency is a recurring and frequently underestimated problem. Accelerated erythropoiesis consumes iron at a rate above normal, and if stores are insufficient before ascending, the body cannot manufacture the additional hemoglobin that EPO commands it to produce. Iron supplementation before and during the altitude camp is standard practice among professional teams, and Constantini, Wilhite, and Chapman (2017) included it as an explicit recommendation in their clinical guide for altitude training.

## What science knows today

Three decades of research have consolidated a robust body of evidence on altitude training. The LHTL model produces verifiable hematological adaptations that translate into sea-level performance improvements. Optimal altitude sits between 2,000 and 2,500 meters, minimum duration is three to four weeks, and daily exposure should exceed 14 hours to generate a relevant erythropoietic stimulus. But individual response is neither predictable nor consistent: the same cyclist may respond differently in successive camps, and the factors modulating that variability—iron, sleep, training load, genetics—interact in ways science has not yet fully unraveled.

For the cyclist planning their season, altitude remains a high-return investment that requires attention to detail: monitoring serum iron before ascending, adjusting training intensities to the new environment, prioritizing sleep, and choosing the moment of descent with care. The mountains offer what no legal supplement can replicate—a genuine increase in oxygen transport capacity—but only when the conditions that physiology demands are respected.
