## The third pillar nobody watches

When Edward Coyle and Michael Joyner published their 2008 review "Endurance exercise performance: the physiology of champions" in *The Journal of Physiology* (PMID 17901124), the message was precise: endurance performance is determined by three interrelated physiological factors. The first is VO₂max, which sets the ceiling for oxygen uptake. The second is the lactate threshold, which determines what fraction of that ceiling can be sustained. The third is efficiency — how much mechanical power the muscle generates per liter of oxygen consumed. The first two have been on coaches' and cyclists' radar for decades. The third remains, largely, the great overlooked variable in everyday performance analysis.

The reason for this neglect is not that efficiency matters less. It is that efficiency does not appear on any cycling computer screen, is not directly reflected in FTP, and requires laboratory calorimetry to measure precisely. But its impact is concrete: a cyclist with a VO₂max of 75 ml/kg/min and a gross efficiency (GE) of 24% produces approximately 16 watts per kilogram at threshold conditions. Another cyclist with the same VO₂max but a GE of 21% produces around 14 watts per kilogram. That 3-percentage-point gap in efficiency represents a power-to-weight difference that no altitude training block can easily bridge.

## What it is and how to calculate it

Gross efficiency expresses the percentage of total metabolic energy consumed during exercise that is converted into mechanical work. Its formula is:

$$GE(\%) = \frac{W_{mec} \times 60}{\dot{V}O_2 \times EE_{O_2} \times 1000} \times 100$$

where $W_{mec}$ is mechanical power in watts, $\dot{V}O_2$ is oxygen consumption in L/min, and $EE_{O_2}$ is the energy equivalent of oxygen in kJ/L (approximately 20.1–21.1 kJ/L depending on substrate mix). In practice, metabolic energy is calculated using the Brouwer (1957) equation, which uses respiratory flows directly:

$$\dot{E}_{kJ/min} = 16.18 \cdot \dot{V}O_2 + 5.02 \cdot \dot{V}CO_2$$

Substituting into the GE expression yields:

$$GE(\%) = \frac{6 \cdot W_{mec}}{16.18 \cdot \dot{V}O_2 + 5.02 \cdot \dot{V}CO_2}$$

with respiratory flows expressed in L/min. This equation implicitly incorporates the respiratory exchange ratio through the differential coefficients of VO₂ and VCO₂, making it valid for any substrate mix between pure fat (RER = 0.70) and pure carbohydrates (RER = 1.00).

GE is not the only efficiency index used in cycling. *Net efficiency* subtracts resting metabolism from the denominator — yielding higher values, typically 22–30% — to isolate the energetic cost of exercise proper. *Delta efficiency* calculates the slope of the relationship between mechanical power and metabolic power across a range of intensities, capturing the marginal efficiency of producing additional power. Exercise physiologists prefer GE in applied studies because it does not require a separate measurement of basal metabolism and because its between-session reproducibility is higher. When texts say professionals "are more efficient," they almost always mean GE.

## The measurement protocol

Measuring GE requires indirect calorimetry: the cyclist pedals on an ergometer while breathing through a mask connected to a gas analyzer that records VO₂ and VCO₂ in real time. The most common protocol involves constant-power stages of 4 to 6 minutes — sufficient to reach steady state at submaximal intensities — with measurements at 2 or 3 different power outputs within the 100–300 W range, depending on the cyclist's level. GE is calculated for each stage as the ratio of mechanical power to metabolic power derived from respiratory gases. To guarantee reproducibility, tests are performed in a fasted state or with standardized nutrition, at controlled ambient temperature, and always at the same reference cadence, since cadence significantly affects the result.

Nutritional status matters because the RER and therefore the substrate mix change the $EE_{O_2}$. The same cyclist pedaling after a carbohydrate load shows a higher RER (closer to 1.0) and consequently a slightly higher $EE_{O_2}$, which modestly elevates the calculated GE. The difference between fasted and fed-state measurements can reach 0.5–1.0 percentage points of GE — enough to distort between-session comparisons when not controlled. This methodological detail explains why studies with different designs have reached apparently contradictory conclusions about training's ability to alter GE.

## Real-world ranges across performance levels

The literature converges on consistent ranges by cycling level. Sallet et al. published in 2006 in *Journal of Sports Medicine and Physical Fitness* (PMID 16998438) an analysis of 71 cyclists grouped as professionals and elite, finding a mean GE of 25.6 ± 2.6% in professionals and 24.4 ± 2.0% in elite riders. Lucía et al. (PMID 12471319) reported a mean GE of 24.5 ± 0.7% in 11 world-class cyclists. In studies of well-trained but non-professional cyclists, typical values range from 20 to 23%. Recreational and untrained individuals fall below 20%.

The spread within each category is large. Coyle et al. in their 1992 study (PMID 1501563) measured delta efficiency — a related index — in 19 highly trained cyclists and found a range of 18.3 to 25.6%, with all subjects belonging to the same training level. This variability was not explained by training volume or VO₂max, but primarily by muscle fiber composition. The underlying data point is that two cyclists with virtually identical training loads can end a season separated by more than 7 percentage points of efficiency.

## The role of muscle fiber type

The relationship between muscle composition and efficiency is one of the most robust findings in exercise physiology applied to cycling. Coyle et al. (PMID 1501563) demonstrated in 19 highly trained cyclists that the percentage of type I fibers in the vastus lateralis correlated with GE at r = 0.75 and with delta efficiency at r = 0.85 (p < 0.001 in both cases). Subjects with a higher proportion of slow-twitch fibers — which ranged from 32 to 76% in the sample — were systematically more efficient at converting chemical energy into mechanical work. The biochemical explanation lies in myosin heavy chain ATPase kinetics: the slow isoform (MHC-I), expressed in type I fibers, hydrolyzes ATP at a lower rate and generates more work per mole of ATP than the fast isoform (MHC-II). Put plainly: slow muscle produces more mechanical power per unit of chemical energy consumed.

Mitochondrial density and the degree of coupling in oxidative phosphorylation also contribute to GE, though more complexly. Well-trained mitochondria exhibit less proton leak across the inner membrane — that is, less uncoupling between the proton gradient and ATP synthesis — which translates into greater efficiency of the oxidative phosphorylation process. However, the mitochondrial component is more trainable than the muscle fiber phenotype: PGC-1α–mediated mitochondrial biogenesis responds well to endurance training, while type II to type I fiber conversion is much slower and more limited in adults.

## The inverse relationship with VO₂max

Lucía et al. published in 2002 in *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise* (PMID 12471319) a finding that appeared contradictory: in 11 world-class cyclists, the relationship between VO₂max and GE was inverse and significant (r = -0.72, p < 0.05). Cyclists with greater aerobic capacity tended to have lower gross efficiency, and vice versa. The group had a mean VO₂max of 72 ml/kg/min and a mean GE of 24.5%. The authors concluded that "a high CE/GE seems to compensate for a relatively low VO₂max in professional cyclists," suggesting that efficiency functions as a compensatory variable: a cyclist with moderate VO₂max but exceptional GE can compete with another who has higher VO₂max but lower efficiency. In 2012, Hopker et al. (PMID 22562732) confirmed this inverse relationship in longitudinal studies: when training raises VO₂max, GE tends to drop slightly, and vice versa.

This inverse relationship has a plausible physiological explanation. Cyclists with high VO₂max tend to have a greater proportion of type IIa fibers — recruited during intense aerobic demands — which, while more oxidative than type IIx, remain less efficient than type I. Additionally, high-aerobic-capacity muscle may operate with a degree of mitochondrial uncoupling that, while reducing energetic efficiency, acts as a thermostat against excessive heat accumulation. The result is that the cyclist with the largest engine does not always use fuel most economically.

## Cadence and efficiency: the concrete effect

Lucía et al. (PMID 15179176) tested eight professional cyclists in 2004 pedaling at 366 W — their habitual competition power — at three different cadences. The results were unambiguous: GE improved systematically with higher cadences. At 60 rpm, mean GE was 22.4 ± 1.7%; at 80 rpm it rose to 23.6 ± 1.8%; at 100 rpm it reached 24.2 ± 2.0%. The difference between 60 and 100 rpm was statistically significant. The same mechanical power is produced at lower metabolic cost when cadence is high, because torque per pedal stroke decreases and with it the recruitment of low-efficiency fast-twitch fibers. This physiologically explains the preference of great climbers — Pantani, Contador, Froome — for 90–100 rpm cadences on steep gradients where required power is high but travel speed is low.

## Can GE be trained?

This is the most debated question in applied cycling physiology. Hopker et al. published in 2009 in *International Journal of Sports Medicine* (PMID 19941249) a review of available evidence and concluded that "training has a positive influence on gross efficiency," though with important methodological caveats. Cross-sectional studies comparing trained with untrained cyclists showed no significant differences — a result the authors attribute to design limitations — while well-controlled longitudinal studies did detect improvements. Proposed mechanisms include type IIa fiber transformation toward a more type I–like phenotype, changes in muscle fiber shortening velocities, and mitochondrial adaptations that reduce proton leak.

The same group showed in a longitudinal study published in *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise* (PMID 19276841) that GE fluctuates throughout the competitive season in experienced cyclists: it improved from 19.6% to 20.6% during the pre-competition phase, remained stable during the main competition phase at approximately 20.3–20.6%, and declined to 19.4% by the end of season. One percentage point of variation may seem small, but at 300 W of mechanical power it represents approximately 12–15 W of difference in metabolic cost. The most extreme documented case was Coyle's (PMID 15774697) with a Tour de France champion: an 8% improvement in muscular efficiency over 7 years, from age 21 to 28, alongside relative VO₂max stability. The study was subsequently subject to methodological controversy, but no researcher disputed that the cyclist in question was exceptionally efficient.

The conclusion emerging from the literature is that GE responds to training, but modestly and with large inter-individual variability in response. Two cyclists with the same training volume and intensity distribution can finish the season with 2–3 percentage points of GE difference explained primarily by their baseline muscle composition. That is what makes GE the "least trainable" indicator in the physiological profile: not that it is completely fixed, but that its ceiling is largely determined by genetic factors that training can approach but rarely reach.

## Gross efficiency and durability: the connection

GE does not decline linearly during prolonged effort. Evidence suggests that work accumulation — measured in kilojoules per kilogram — deteriorates efficiency before it deteriorates threshold power. Spragg et al. demonstrated in their study with professional cyclists (*Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise*, PMID 35977108) that gross efficiency at 200 W measured fresh correlated with durability at r = 0.792: cyclists who were more efficient at rest showed better resistance to power deterioration after accumulating intense work. The mechanism likely involves glycogen sparing produced by higher GE — efficient muscle consumes less glycogen per watt produced — preserving the substrate needed for high-intensity efforts at the end of a race.

This connection between GE and durability adds an important practical dimension: it is not enough to know a cyclist's resting efficiency. The relevant question for competition is how that efficiency changes with accumulated fatigue, and that data is even scarcer in the literature than baseline GE. What is documented is that training intensity distribution influences durability (greater sub-threshold volume correlates with better durability), and that same distribution produces the most consistent improvements in seasonal GE. The parallel suggests shared mechanisms: the mitochondrial base built through zone 2 volume simultaneously improves efficiency and resistance to its deterioration under fatigue.

## What the power meter does not show

The most direct practical implication of GE is this: the watt appearing on the cycling computer screen is the same regardless of whether it is produced by a cyclist with 24% or 21% GE. But to produce that watt, the efficient cyclist consumes 12–15% less metabolic energy. Over a five-hour stage, that difference accumulates into hundreds of kilocalories. Nutritional strategy, glycogen management, the ability to sustain intensity on the final climb — all of this is conditioned by GE, but none of it is directly revealed by cycling computer data. Two cyclists with identical normalized power throughout a stage can arrive at the final climb with radically different levels of metabolic fatigue if their GE differs by 3 percentage points.

For an amateur cyclist without access to laboratory calorimetry, there are partial approximations. The caloric expenditure estimated by cycling computer algorithms assumes a reference efficiency — generally around 22–24% — that may not correspond to the individual's reality. Cyclists with high VO₂max but moderate GE tend to consume more than the device estimates; cyclists with high GE may arrive at the end of long rides with more reserves than the algorithm predicts. The only way to know one's own GE is a calorimetry test in a sports physiology laboratory — something only a few centers offer routinely, but which provides data that no power test can substitute.
