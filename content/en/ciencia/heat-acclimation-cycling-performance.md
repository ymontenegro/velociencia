## Heat as a training stimulus

A Tour de France stage at 38 °C on the tarmac of southern France separates riders who prepared their bodies for the heat from those who did not. Ambient temperature does more than cause discomfort: it directly compromises aerobic performance by forcing the cardiovascular system to split cardiac output between working muscles and the skin, which demands more blood flow to dissipate heat. Périard, Eijsvogels, and Daanen (2021) described in *Physiological Reviews* how this competition between thermoregulation and muscular work reduces sustainable power output, elevates heart rate, and accelerates fatigue. An unacclimated cyclist loses between 10% and 20% of their performance compared to temperate conditions, a margin that can translate to minutes in a 40-kilometer time trial.

The good news is that the human body has a remarkable capacity to adapt to thermal stress. The process is called *heat acclimation* when it occurs in a controlled laboratory environment, and *heat acclimatization* when it happens through natural exposure to hot weather. Both terms describe the same physiological phenomenon: a series of adaptations that reduce cardiovascular strain, improve sweating, and allow sustained power output in hot conditions. What makes this field particularly compelling is that some of these adaptations improve performance even when the thermometer stays below 15 °C.

## The adaptations heat triggers

Périard, Racinais, and Sawka (2015) published a comprehensive review in *Scandinavian Journal of Medicine & Science in Sports* of the adaptations induced by heat acclimation in competitive athletes. The catalog of physiological changes is extensive and unfolds in a predictable temporal sequence. The first three to five days produce the most evident cardiovascular adaptations: plasma volume expansion, reduction in resting and submaximal exercise heart rate, and improved stroke volume. Between days five and ten, sudomotor adaptations consolidate: sweating begins earlier, is more copious, and contains less sodium, preserving the body's electrolyte stores.

Plasma volume expansion is the central mechanism linking heat acclimation to performance. More plasma means greater total blood volume, which allows the heart to fill its ventricles more completely with each beat and eject more blood per contraction. Périard, Travers, Racinais, and Sawka (2016) detailed these cardiovascular adaptations in *Autonomic Neuroscience*, explaining how the increase in plasma volume raises maximal cardiac output, improves muscle perfusion, and enables simultaneous maintenance of skin blood flow without sacrificing oxygen delivery to the quadriceps. The net result is a more efficient heart that pumps more blood with less effort — precisely what a cyclist needs to sustain high power output for hours.

The reduction in core temperature is another key adaptation. An acclimated cyclist begins exercise at a lower body temperature and raises it more slowly than an unadapted rider. This widens the thermal margin before reaching temperatures that compromise central nervous system function, a critical factor in prolonged efforts where hyperthermia contributes to central fatigue as described in models such as Noakes' central governor hypothesis.

## Faster even in the cold: the Lorenzo study

Lorenzo, Halliwill, Sawka, and Minson (2010) published a study in the *Journal of Applied Physiology* that changed how the cycling world views heat acclimation. Twelve trained cyclists completed a 10-day protocol of exercise in a controlled environment at 40 °C and 30% relative humidity. Before and after the protocol, they performed VO₂max tests, time trials, and lactate threshold assessments in both cool (13 °C) and hot (38 °C) conditions. A control group trained at the same volume and intensity but in cool conditions.

The results were striking. Heat acclimation expanded plasma volume by 6.5 ± 1.5%, increased maximal cardiac output by 9.1 ± 3.4% in cool and 4.5 ± 4.6% in hot conditions, raised VO₂max by 5% in cool and 8% in hot conditions, improved power at lactate threshold by 5% in both environments, and accelerated time-trial performance by 6% in cool and 8% in hot conditions. The control group showed no changes in any parameter. The physiological equation is straightforward: more plasma volume → more stroke volume → more cardiac output → more oxygen to muscles → more sustainable power. That mechanism operates regardless of ambient temperature, making heat acclimation a legitimate ergogenic tool even for spring races in temperate climates.

## 14 days to erase the heat penalty

Racinais, Périard, Karlsen, and Nybo (2015) quantified the temporal progression of acclimation in a study published in *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise*. Nine trained cyclists performed 43.4-kilometer outdoor time trials in the heat (approximately 37 °C) on days one, six, and fourteen of a training period in hot weather. They also completed time trials in cool conditions (8 °C) before and after acclimation.

The first time trial in the heat took 77 ± 6 minutes. After six days of exposure, the time dropped to 69 ± 5 minutes. After 14 days, the cyclists completed the effort in 66 ± 4 minutes, a time statistically indistinguishable from their cool-condition performance (66 ± 3 minutes). In two weeks, the thermal penalty had been completely erased. Cyclists began all time trials at similar power output during the first 20% of the course, but in the initial heat trial power dropped sharply in the second half — a conservative pacing pattern that progressively attenuated with acclimation.

Karlsen, Nybo, Nørgaard, Jensen, Bonne, and Racinais (2015) complemented that data in *Scandinavian Journal of Medicine & Science in Sports* with a field study of nine well-trained cyclists during a two-week training camp in natural heat (34 ± 3 °C). Sweat sodium concentration dropped from 75 to 52 mmol/L in the first week. Sweat rate increased by 20%. Resting hematocrit fell by 5.6%, an indirect marker of plasma volume expansion. Time-trial power output improved 11 ± 8% between the first and second week, with an additional 5 ± 4% gain through the end of the camp.

## How long it lasts and how fast it fades

A practical question every cyclist asks: if I acclimate before a race, how long do the adaptations last? Daanen, Racinais, and Périard (2018) answered with a meta-analysis published in *Sports Medicine* that compiled data from multiple studies on acclimation decay kinetics. Heart rate adaptations are lost at a rate of 2.3% per day without heat exposure. Core temperature follows a similar pattern, with a daily decay of 2.6%. That means after one week without thermal stimulus, a cyclist has lost roughly 15% to 18% of their cardiovascular adaptations.

Sweat rate decays more slowly, suggesting that sudomotor adaptations are more robust than cardiovascular ones. Longer acclimation protocols produce more durable adaptations, a relevant finding for season planning. The most useful result from the meta-analysis is that re-acclimation is 8 to 12 times faster than original acclimation. A cyclist who acclimated over 10 days and then lost some adaptations can recover them with just two or three heat exposure sessions. This allows maintenance strategies: an initial acclimation block followed by periodic booster sessions before key summer competitions.

## Practical protocols: from the lab to the home trainer

Heat acclimation does not require a trip to the desert or access to a climate chamber. Périard, Racinais, and Sawka (2015) described how controlled hyperthermia protocols — where the goal is to reach and maintain a core temperature of 38.5 °C during the session — produce faster and more complete adaptations than fixed work-rate protocols. The practical cyclist can replicate this stimulus in several ways.

The most accessible option is training on a home trainer with windows closed and no fan, adding clothing layers to raise body temperature. Sessions of 60 to 90 minutes at moderate intensity (zone 2 to low tempo) under those conditions generate sufficient thermal load to trigger adaptations, provided they are repeated for at least five consecutive days. The risk of this approach is excessive dehydration, making fluid intake during the session mandatory.

Post-exercise sauna is another alternative with scientific support. Scoon, Hopkins, Mayhew, and Cotter (2007) published a study in the *Journal of Science and Medicine in Sport* with middle-distance runners who sat in a sauna at 90 °C for approximately 31 minutes after training, repeating the protocol roughly 13 times over three weeks. Plasma volume increased by 7.1%, red cell volume rose by 3.5%, and time to exhaustion improved by 32%, equivalent to a 1.9% improvement in time-trial performance. Although the study used runners, the mechanism of plasma volume expansion is transferable to cycling. Post-training hot water immersion produces comparable adaptations according to subsequent studies.

## The debate on transfer to cool conditions

Lorenzo's study generated excitement but also caution in the scientific community. Karlsen and colleagues, in a separate study from 2015, observed that natural acclimatization during a two-week training camp did not improve VO₂max or time-trial performance when cyclists returned to cool conditions. The discrepancy with Lorenzo's data can be explained by differences in experimental design: Lorenzo used a controlled laboratory protocol with matched exercise volumes between groups, while Karlsen studied natural acclimatization during a training camp where total training load varied.

The current position in the literature is that heat acclimation produces cardiovascular adaptations that can benefit performance at any temperature, but the magnitude of that benefit in cool conditions depends on the protocol used, the cyclist's training level, and whether plasma volume expansion reaches a sufficient threshold to raise maximal cardiac output. For cyclists already near their physiological ceiling, the margins are narrower. For recreational or intermediate-level cyclists, the potential for improvement is proportionally greater.

## Planning for the European summer

The grand tours in July and August represent the competitive scenario where heat acclimation has the greatest impact. Tour de France stages in southern France and Vuelta a España stages in Andalusia and Murcia expose cyclists to temperatures that can exceed 40 °C on the road surface. Professional teams plan altitude camps that combine hypoxic training with natural heat exposure, taking advantage of the fact that many altitude destinations in southern Europe offer both conditions.

A realistic protocol for an amateur cyclist preparing for a summer gran fondo could be structured as follows. Four to six weeks before the event, begin a block of five to ten indoor trainer sessions with added thermal load, optionally supplemented with post-exercise sauna or hot bath. Maintain one to two weekly booster sessions until race week. This strategy is sufficient to induce the main cardiovascular and sudomotor adaptations, reduce heart rate in heat, and improve subjective tolerance to thermal stress. Hydration and sodium replacement during sessions are not optional: chronic dehydration from heat training without adequate replenishment negates the adaptations and increases the risk of heat illness.

## Heat as an ally, not an enemy

Heat acclimation is one of the few physiological stimuli capable of simultaneously improving thermoregulation, plasma volume, cardiac output, and cardiovascular efficiency. Lorenzo demonstrated that 10 days of controlled heat exposure improve time-trial performance by 6% in cool conditions, a margin that in competitive cycling can mean the difference between the peloton and the podium. Racinais and Périard showed that two weeks of acclimation completely eliminate the heat penalty in time trials. Daanen confirmed that adaptations are quickly recovered with booster sessions.

The human body evolved in warm environments long before the invention of desk fans and air conditioning. The physiological machinery for adapting to heat is ancient, robust, and remarkably efficient. The cyclist who learns to use it as a training tool does not merely survive August stages better: they arrive at them with a heart that pumps more blood, better-perfused muscles, and a biological cooling system refined by millions of years of natural selection and ten days of riding the trainer with no fan.
