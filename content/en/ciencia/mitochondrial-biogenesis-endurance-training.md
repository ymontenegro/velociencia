## The picture that changed exercise physiology

Under the electron microscope, a cross-section of a muscle fiber from the vastus lateralis of a professional cyclist looks nothing like that of a sedentary person. Mitochondria, the oval organelles scattered through the cytoplasm between myofibrils, appear more numerous, larger and packed with denser internal cristae. In sedentary muscle they occupy around 3-4% of the fiber's volume. In endurance-trained muscle the figure exceeds 7-8%, and in elite cyclists type I fibers can approach 10-12%. That difference is not cosmetic: each additional mitochondrion is a factory burning fatty acids and pyruvate to produce ATP, the direct fuel of muscle contraction.

Mitochondrial biogenesis is the process by which the muscle cell builds new mitochondria or expands existing ones. For decades it was considered an adaptation exclusive to long-duration, low-intensity training. Research over the past two decades has complicated that picture: there are multiple molecular routes to a better mitochondrion, and they depend on the type of stimulus. Understanding what volume builds and what intensity builds is the difference between a random training plan and one designed with actual knowledge.

## Holloszy and the foundational discovery

John O. Holloszy published in 1967, in *The Journal of Biological Chemistry*, the experiment that founded the mitochondrial physiology of exercise. He subjected rats to a treadmill running program for twelve weeks and then measured the enzymatic activity of their muscles. The finding was unambiguous: the capacity of the gastrocnemius to oxidize pyruvate had doubled, and key respiratory chain enzymes such as succinate dehydrogenase, cytochrome c reductase and cytochrome oxidase showed increases of roughly 100% relative to sedentary animals. For the first time there was direct evidence that exercise not only enlarges the heart, it also restructures the energy factories inside each muscle fiber.

That experiment established a principle still in force today: the peripheral adaptations to endurance training happen largely inside the muscle, at the cellular level. The oxygen the heart pumps is only useful if the muscle can use it, and the capacity to use it depends on the number and quality of mitochondria available. What Joyner and Coyle identified decades later as cardiac output being the limiting factor for VO2max in elite athletes rests on the assumption that the muscular periphery is mitochondrially hypertrophied. Without that peripheral oxidative capacity, the extra oxygen would go to waste.

## PGC-1α, the master switch

When a muscle fiber contracts repeatedly, a cascade of intracellular signals activates a transcriptional coactivator called PGC-1α (peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha). Hood (2009), in a review in *Applied Physiology, Nutrition, and Metabolism*, described PGC-1α as the master regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis in skeletal muscle. It is not an exaggeration: this protein simultaneously coordinates the transcription of hundreds of mitochondrial genes encoded in the nuclear DNA and triggers the replication of mitochondrial DNA itself via the mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM).

PGC-1α responds to multiple intracellular signals triggered by exercise: the rise in cytosolic calcium during contraction activates CaMK, the fall in the ATP/AMP ratio activates AMPK, and moderate oxidative stress modulates additional transcription factors. All those signals converge on PGC-1α, which migrates to the cell nucleus and to the mitochondria to orchestrate the construction of new material. Memme et al. (2021), in *The Journal of Physiology*, described the process as a choreography between the nuclear and mitochondrial genomes: a complete mitochondrion requires more than a thousand distinct proteins, and only thirteen are encoded in the mitochondrial DNA itself. The rest travel from the cytoplasmic ribosomes with import signals directed to the organelle.

## Content versus function: two distinct adaptations

Granata, Jamnick and Bishop (2018), in *Sports Medicine*, cleared up persistent confusion in the literature: "better mitochondrial capacity" breaks into two variables. Content is the total amount of mitochondrial material per unit of muscle, measurable through markers such as citrate synthase activity or mitochondrial volume estimated by electron microscopy. Function is how much ATP each milligram of mitochondria can produce — the intrinsic respiratory efficiency of the organelle.

The conclusion reorganized training prescription. Total training volume is the main determinant of mitochondrial content, while relative exercise intensity is the critical factor for improving function per unit of mitochondria. Accumulating hours in zone 2 multiplies the number of mitochondria; high-intensity stimuli make each one work better. Both adaptations are cumulative and partly independent, which explains why elite cyclists combine high weekly volumes with specific blocks of intensity.

## Why zone 2 multiplies mitochondrial density

Zone 2, understood as continuous aerobic effort below the first ventilatory threshold, produces a sustained stimulus of AMPK activation and cytosolic calcium elevation without generating the metabolic stress that limits exercise duration. A session of three or four hours in that zone keeps the biogenesis signal turned on throughout the entire working period and during several hours of recovery. That time under molecular tension is what builds mitochondrial volume.

Type I fibers, slow and oxidative, are the most recruited during continuous submaximal efforts, and therefore are the ones that benefit most from volume accumulation. Bishop, Botella and colleagues (2019), in *Physiology*, documented that the increase in mitochondrial content induced by volume training occurs preferentially in oxidative fibers, while type II fibers barely modify their mitochondrial density with that stimulus. For a cyclist looking to sustain aerobic power for hours, that selective mitochondrial hypertrophy in slow fibers is precisely the desired adaptation: more mitochondria in the fibers that carry prolonged effort.

The practical threshold for volume to produce meaningful adaptations usually sits between eight and twelve hours per week of structured training, with at least three long zone 2 sessions. Below that volume, the stimulus is too fragmented to sustain the biogenesis signal. Above it, gains are not linear: doubling the volume does not double mitochondrial content, but it does postpone the point at which accumulation flattens out.

## Why HIIT improves mitochondrial function

High-intensity intervals generate an acute metabolic perturbation that continuous exercise does not reach. ATP falls quickly, AMP accumulates, lactate rises and momentary oxidative stress activates AMPK more forcefully than any long zone 2 session. MacInnis and Gibala (2017), in a review published in *The Journal of Physiology*, compiled evidence that HIIT induces increases in mitochondrial enzyme activity equivalent to or greater than continuous training, with a fraction of the total working time.

The most illuminating finding came from MacInnis et al. (2017), also in *The Journal of Physiology*: ten men performed single-leg cycling for two weeks, one leg with high-intensity intervals and the other with continuous cycling matched for total work. That design eliminated the genetic and individual differences that contaminate traditional studies. The HIIT leg showed larger increases in maximal mitochondrial respiration, particularly in complex I activity and in combined complex I+II capacity, even though absolute mitochondrial protein content increased similarly in both legs.

The study suggested that intensity carries qualitative information that volume cannot replicate: not simply "more mitochondria" but organelles with more developed internal cristae and greater density of respiratory complexes per unit of membrane. That qualitative adaptation increases maximal oxidative power, a critical factor for sustaining efforts in the VO2max range.

## The controversy over intensity

Bishop et al. (2019), in *Physiology*, opened a debate that remains active. The relationship between intensity and biogenesis is non-linear: very low intensity produces a weak signal, moderate-to-high intensity produces an optimal signal, but extreme sustained intensity can delay mitochondrial protein synthesis through excessive activation of catabolic pathways. The authors warned that very-high-intensity training repeated frequently, without adequate recovery blocks or aerobic volume, can paradoxically reduce mitochondrial content rather than increase it.

That nuance explains why successful polarized models concentrate intense stimuli in 10-20% of total volume, leaving the bulk of aerobic work in zone 2. Inverting that ratio — too much HIIT and too little aerobic base — produces a cyclist who is fast in the short term but stalled in mitochondrial volume in the medium term.

## How fast the whole thing reverses

Granata and colleagues (2018) documented that reducing training volume for just two weeks produces 15-25% drops in mitochondrial enzymatic markers. The same mitochondrion that took months to build starts being reabsorbed in days if the stimulus disappears, a phenomenon called mitophagy when it acts selectively on damaged organelles. Recovery after a break does not start from scratch: the cellular memory preserved in epigenetic factors allows an experienced cyclist to recover most of their oxidative capacity in four to six weeks, compared to the months a novice would need to reach similar levels.

## What builds what: the practical application

Memme et al. (2021) summarized the practical implication: different stimuli generate different adaptations, and complete training needs to cover both fronts. Extended volume at low-to-moderate intensity builds mitochondrial tissue and selectively hypertrophies type I fibers. Short, repeated high-intensity intervals improve the functional quality of each mitochondrion and extend the benefit to type IIa fibers, which acquire oxidative characteristics with that stimulus.

For a cyclist structuring an endurance season, no zone substitutes for the other. Three-to-five-hour rides in zone 2 are the raw material of mitochondrial content, the chassis on which function is later layered. Blocks of intervals at VO2max are the polish that turns that broad base into competitive capacity. Each hour in zone 2 adds volume to the cellular factories; each intense interval refines their internal machinery. The calibrated combination of both stimuli, sustained over years, is what separates an amateur capable of three comfortable hours at 200 watts from a professional capable of five hours at 300.
