## The mistake that lasted a century

In 1907, a physiologist at the University of Cambridge published a finding that would shape the narrative of exercise for a hundred years. Walter Fletcher and Frederick Gowland Hopkins observed that isolated frog muscle stimulated to fatigue accumulated lactic acid, and that the accumulation disappeared when the muscle was exposed to oxygen. The conclusion seemed straightforward: lactic acid was a waste product generated when the muscle lacked sufficient oxygen, and its accumulation caused fatigue. The elegance of the experiment, paired with the simplicity of the explanation, cemented an idea that migrated into textbooks, training plans, and the vocabulary of every cyclist who has ever felt the burn in their legs on a steep climb. The problem is that idea was fundamentally wrong.

Lactate is not lactic acid. It is not a metabolic waste product. It is not the direct cause of muscular fatigue. And far from being a toxic residue of effort, it is one of the most versatile energy molecules in the human body. Brooks (2018) summarized four decades of research by stating that lactate fulfills at least three simultaneous functions during exercise: it is a preferred energy source for multiple tissues, the main precursor for new glucose synthesis in the liver, and a signaling molecule that regulates gene expression in muscle cells. The review, published in *Cell Metabolism*, marked the consolidation of a paradigm shift that had begun decades earlier in a Berkeley laboratory.

## George Brooks and the birth of the lactate shuttle

George A. Brooks, an exercise physiologist at the University of California, Berkeley, published a review in *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise* in 1985 that challenged the very concept of the "anaerobic threshold." Brooks (1985) argued that lactate accumulation in blood did not simply reflect muscular production but rather the balance between the rate of production and the rate of clearance across the entire organism. Blood lactate was not an indicator of muscular hypoxia but a metabolite in transit, constantly produced and consumed even under full oxygen availability. A year later, Brooks (1986) formalized the lactate shuttle hypothesis in a seminal paper published in the same journal, presenting evidence that more than 75% of lactate formed during sustained steady-state exercise was cleared through direct oxidation, and only a smaller fraction, roughly 20%, was reconverted to glucose via the Cori cycle in the liver.

The hypothesis proposed something that contradicted the canonical physiology of the time: type II glycolytic muscle fibers, which produce lactate at high rates during intense effort, do not release it as waste but as an energy substrate that other cells can take up and oxidize. Type I oxidative fibers in the same muscle, fibers in less active muscles at that moment, the heart, the brain, the liver, and the kidneys are all lactate consumers. The molecule does not accumulate because the body is failing but because the rate of production temporarily exceeds the uptake capacity of receiving tissues. That conceptual distinction transformed our understanding of exercise metabolism.

## How lactate travels between cells

The molecular mechanism of the lactate shuttle depends on a family of membrane proteins called monocarboxylate transporters, known as MCTs. Two isoforms are central in skeletal muscle. MCT4, predominantly expressed in type II glycolytic fibers, exports lactate from cells that produce it at high speed. MCT1, expressed in type I oxidative fibers and on mitochondrial membranes, imports lactate into the cells and organelles that will oxidize it. Pilegaard et al. (1999) demonstrated in the *American Journal of Physiology* that lactate transport capacity in trained muscle was 12% greater than in untrained muscle, with MCT1 protein content 76% higher and MCT4 content 32% higher in subjects with a history of endurance training.

This transport infrastructure allows lactate to flow in a coordinated manner between cellular compartments and between distant organs. Van Hall (2010) reviewed lactate kinetics in human tissues at rest and during exercise in *Acta Physiologica* and documented that virtually all organs participate in lactate metabolism. At rest, the human body continuously produces between 1.0 and 1.5 mmol/kg/h of lactate, mainly in the skin, red blood cells, the brain, and skeletal muscle. During intense exercise, muscular production multiplies, but uptake mechanisms simultaneously activate in tissues that use lactate as their preferred fuel. The result is an energy distribution system that operates in parallel with blood glucose and competes with it for mitochondrial oxidation.

Brooks (2018) expanded the shuttle concept to the intracellular compartment. Within the muscle cell itself, lactate produced in the cytoplasm can be transported directly to the mitochondria through MCT1 located on the inner mitochondrial membrane, where the mitochondrial isoform of lactate dehydrogenase converts it to pyruvate to feed the Krebs cycle. This intracellular shuttle means that lactate does not need to leave the cell to be oxidized: it can be produced and consumed within the same myocyte, further challenging the notion that cytoplasmic accumulation reflects a metabolic crisis.

## The heart's preferred fuel

Gertz et al. (1988) conducted an experiment that defined the relationship between the heart and lactate during exercise. Using carbon isotope tracers in healthy human subjects, they measured the uptake and oxidation of energy substrates by the myocardium at rest and during moderate-intensity exercise. The results, published in the *Journal of Clinical Investigation*, showed that myocardial lactate uptake increased from 34.9 micromoles per minute at rest to 120.4 micromoles per minute within five minutes of exercise. When arterial lactate concentration rose during effort, the heart preferentially took it up over glucose, and virtually all absorbed lactate was immediately oxidized to generate ATP.

That preference is no accident. The myocardium is the tissue with the highest mitochondrial density in the body and operates almost exclusively through aerobic metabolism. At normal physiological lactate concentrations, around 0.5 mmol/L at rest, lactate supplies about 25% of the total substrate oxidized by the heart. When arterial concentration rises to 4.5 mmol/L, a common value during a high-intensity interval on the bike, lactate can represent 60-90% of the substrate oxidized by cardiomyocytes, displacing both fatty acids and glucose. The heart of the cyclist, that engine beating at 180 beats per minute on a mountain climb, preferentially runs on the lactate that the quadriceps are producing. What the cyclist interprets as waste is, in fact, the fuel keeping the heart running under maximal effort.

## The brain also feeds on lactate

Schurr (2006) posed a direct question in the title of his review published in the *Journal of Cerebral Blood Flow & Metabolism*: "Lactate: the ultimate cerebral oxidative energy substrate?" The evidence he compiled pointed to yes. Neurons can oxidize lactate efficiently, and during high-intensity exercise, when blood lactate concentration rises, the brain increases its lactate uptake proportionally. At rest, lactate represents approximately 8% of cerebral fuel. During intense exercise, that figure can climb to 25-33%, while glucose uptake decreases in parallel, a phenomenon suggesting the brain actively switches fuel when lactate becomes available.

The mechanism makes sense from a bioenergetic standpoint. Lactate oxidation produces between 14 and 17 ATP molecules per lactate molecule, and its conversion to pyruvate generates NADH directly in the cytoplasm, which can feed the mitochondrial electron transport chain. Gladden (2004) noted in his review published in *The Journal of Physiology* that lactate represents a "new paradigm for the third millennium": a molecule continuously produced under aerobic conditions in a variety of cells, tissues, and organs, serving simultaneously as an energy substrate, a gluconeogenic precursor, and a signaling molecule. For the cyclist who feels the mind clouding in the final kilometers of a mountain stage, understanding that the brain depends partly on the lactate produced by the legs adds a dimension that classical fatigue models simply ignored.

## What actually causes fatigue: it is not lactate

If lactate is not the villain, then what causes the burning sensation and the muscular failure every cyclist knows? Robergs, Ghiasvand, and Parker (2004) addressed this question with a detailed biochemical analysis published in the *American Journal of Physiology*. Their conclusion was categorical: there is no biochemical support for the idea that lactate production causes acidosis. Muscular acidosis during intense exercise originates from ATP hydrolysis, which releases protons ($H^+$ ions), and from the accumulation of inorganic phosphate ($P_i$). The key reaction is:

$$ATP \rightarrow ADP + P_i + H^+$$

When ATP demand exceeds the capacity of oxidative metabolism to regenerate it, anaerobic glycolysis accelerates. But lactate production, far from contributing to acidosis, actually attenuates it. The reaction catalyzed by lactate dehydrogenase consumes one proton for every pyruvate molecule converted to lactate:

$$Pyruvate + NADH + H^+ \rightarrow Lactate + NAD^+$$

Lactate acts as a metabolic buffer. If the muscle did not produce lactate, acidosis and fatigue would occur faster and more severely, because the protons generated by ATP hydrolysis would lack that transient sink. The correlation between elevated lactate and fatigue that 20th-century physiologists observed was real, but the causality was reversed: lactate rises because metabolism accelerates under effort, not because lactate causes metabolic collapse. It is a marker of metabolic state, not the agent of damage.

Gladden (2004) synthesized it in a sentence that defined the paradigm shift: "For much of the 20th century, lactate was considered a waste product of glycolysis due to hypoxia. Since the 1970s, a lactate revolution has occurred." That revolution meant recognizing that $H^+$ accumulation, $Ca^{2+}$ depletion in the sarcoplasmic reticulum, $P_i$ accumulation interfering with actin-myosin cross-bridges, and central fatigue mediated by the nervous system are the true mechanisms behind muscular failure. Lactate is a witness, not a criminal.

## From the test tube to the tarmac: what changes for the cyclist

The reinterpretation of lactate has direct consequences for cycling training. The first is conceptual: the "lactate threshold" does not mark the point where the body begins producing a toxic waste product but the point where the production rate of a valuable fuel exceeds the rate at which the body can redistribute and oxidize it. Brooks (1985) had already noted that blood lactate concentration provides minimal information about the rate of lactate production in the muscle, because what we measure in blood is the net result of production and clearance across the whole body. Two cyclists with the same blood lactate concentration of 4 mmol/L may have radically different production and clearance rates.

The second consequence is practical. Zone 2 training, so widely discussed in recent years, has a direct effect on the lactate transport and oxidation machinery. Prolonged sessions at intensity below the first lactate threshold stimulate mitochondrial biogenesis in oxidative fibers, increase MCT1 expression on cellular and mitochondrial membranes, and improve mitochondrial lactate dehydrogenase activity. The result is that trained muscle takes up and oxidizes lactate at a higher rate, shifting the blood lactate accumulation curve to the right. The trained cyclist does not produce less lactate; they consume it faster. Pilegaard et al. (1999) documented that MCT1 content in the muscle of trained subjects was 76% higher than in untrained ones, direct evidence that training widens the highways through which lactate travels.

The third consequence concerns high-intensity training. Intervals above threshold generate transient peaks in blood lactate that expose the entire organism to elevated concentrations of this molecule. Repeated exposure stimulates transport and oxidation adaptations not only in active muscle but in distant tissues. The heart, which depends on lactate as fuel during effort, benefits from a greater substrate flow. The liver increases its gluconeogenic capacity from lactate, regenerating glucose that returns to the circulation to sustain blood sugar during prolonged efforts. Even the brain adapts its lactate uptake capacity with chronic exercise exposure.

## The lactate shuttle as an energy distribution system

Brooks et al. (2022) published an update in *The Journal of Physiology* titled "Lactate in contemporary biology: a phoenix risen," consolidating four decades of evidence on lactate's role as a central molecule in energy metabolism. The review included data from isotope tracer studies, muscle biopsies, spectroscopic magnetic resonance imaging, and genetic models in MCT-knockout mice. The picture that emerges is one of an inter-organ energy distribution system in which lactate functions as a currency of metabolic exchange, comparable in versatility to glucose but with its own transport and signaling properties.

In a cyclist pedaling at threshold intensity, lactate flow organizes as follows. The quadriceps, hamstrings, and glutes produce lactate at high rates in their type II fibers recruited to sustain the required power output. A fraction of that lactate is oxidized within the same myocyte via the intracellular shuttle described earlier. Another fraction is exported into the blood via MCT4 and travels to type I fibers in the same muscle or in less active muscles, such as the upper body, where MCT1 imports it for oxidation. A third fraction reaches the heart, which oxidizes it preferentially. A fourth fraction arrives at the liver, where it is reconverted to glucose via gluconeogenesis. And a fifth fraction crosses the blood-brain barrier and fuels the neurons. All simultaneously. All coordinated by concentration gradients and transporter density.

Van Hall (2010) documented a finding that illustrates this coordination with data from elite cross-country skiers performing roller-skiing at race intensity. The arms were producing and releasing lactate into the blood while the legs were simultaneously achieving net lactate uptake from the circulation. The legs, which were producing massive amounts of lactate in their type II fibers, were at the same time recapturing the lactate that the arms and other tissues had released into the blood, oxidizing it in their type I fibers. Simultaneous production and consumption within the same muscle group. That duality is the essence of the lactate shuttle and the reason why measuring blood lactate offers only a partial snapshot of what is happening at the tissue level.

## Signaling: lactate as a regulatory molecule

Lactate does not merely serve as fuel and metabolic precursor. Brooks (2018) identified a third function that has opened a growing line of research: cellular signaling. Lactate regulates gene expression through an epigenetic mechanism known as histone lactylation, where lactyl groups bind to lysine residues on histones and modify DNA accessibility to the transcription machinery. This discovery, which came after Brooks' review but is consistent with his theoretical framework, implies that repeated exposure to elevated lactate concentrations during training not only provides energy but modifies the gene expression program of exposed cells.

For the cyclist, the implication is that every interval session pushing lactate above 6-8 mmol/L is not only training the energy metabolism but also sending molecular signals to the genes encoding mitochondrial proteins, monocarboxylate transporters, and oxidative metabolism enzymes. Brooks et al. (2022) proposed that lactate functions as a "lactormone," a term coined to describe its role as an autocrine, paracrine, and endocrine signal. The molecule that cycling considered toxic waste is, according to the accumulated evidence, one of the body's most active messengers during exercise.

## How to train with lactate, not against it

The lactate shuttle framework suggests that effective training does not seek to "eliminate" lactate but to optimize its production, transport, and oxidation as an integrated system. A cycling program that views lactate as fuel and signaling molecule should include three distinct stimuli. The first is volume at low intensity, below the first lactate threshold, which increases mitochondrial density and MCT1 expression in oxidative fibers, expanding the body's capacity to consume lactate. The second is work at threshold, in the zone where lactate production and clearance are in dynamic equilibrium, training the coordination between the production and clearance systems. The third is supra-threshold efforts, short intense intervals that generate lactate peaks and stimulate both glycolytic capacity and lactate-mediated signaling adaptations.

Brooks (1986) demonstrated that during sustained steady-state exercise, 75% of produced lactate is cleared through direct oxidation. That figure increases with training. A cyclist with years of aerobic base has a lactate oxidation machinery so efficient that they can sustain high power outputs with moderate blood lactate concentrations, not because they produce less but because they consume more. That clearance capacity is what separates the cyclist who collapses at kilometer 80 from the one who holds pace until kilometer 160. It is not about avoiding lactate but about building a metabolic system that processes it for what it always was: energy in transit.

## The phoenix of metabolism

Brooks et al. (2022) titled their most recent review "Lactate in contemporary biology: a phoenix risen," a reference to the molecule that rose from the ashes of a century of misunderstanding. Lactate is not waste. It is not an indicator of metabolic failure. It is not the reason legs burn on a climb. It is a fuel the heart prefers, the brain consumes, muscles exchange among themselves, and that regulates the expression of genes determining performance capacity. The next time a power meter registers a drop and the cyclist feels the familiar burn in the quadriceps, they can remember that what is happening inside their muscles is not an accumulation of metabolic garbage but an energy distribution system working at the limit of its capacity. The difference between those two interpretations is not semantic. It changes how one trains, how effort is dosed, and how the relationship between the body and intensity is understood.
