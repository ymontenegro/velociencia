The average effect of the menstrual cycle on physical performance is −0.06 on the effect-size scale, a value statistics classifies as trivial. That figure comes from the most cited meta-analysis on the topic, signed by Kelly McNulty and colleagues in *Sports Medicine* (2020), which pooled 51 studies and 709 women with natural cycles. The conclusion unsettles both sides of the debate: the period neither ruins performance, as folklore claims, nor are the phases irrelevant, as the opposite oversimplification suggests. The reality is finer, and understanding it changes how a rider organizes her weeks.

It is worth clarifying up front what this literature measures and what it does not. Almost all the evidence refers to eumenorrheic women—with regular cycles and no hormonal contraception—and to acute performance measured in the lab, not to the subjective sense of a bad day. Nor does it address relative energy deficiency in sport, a distinct and more serious problem that deserves its own space. Here we deal with only one narrow question: does the phase of the cycle, on its own, move the performance needle?

## What "Trivial" Means in the Best Evidence

The credible interval for the overall effect runs from −0.16 to 0.04, which includes zero. In practical terms, McNulty et al. (2020) found that performance might be trivially reduced in the early follicular phase—the days of menstruation—compared with the rest of the cycle. The largest difference appeared when comparing the early follicular phase with the late follicular phase, with an effect size of −0.14 (interval of −0.26 to −0.03). These are small magnitudes: the kind of difference that dissolves into the noise of sleep, stress, or nutrition in any given week.

The probabilistic ranking from the same analysis orders the phases coherently. The early follicular phase obtained the lowest SUCRA value, 30%, while the remaining phases clustered between 53% and 55%. Translated: if you had to bet on the worst average moment to perform, the first days of the cycle would be the candidate, though by a narrow margin. The authors themselves warn that much of the evidence is of low methodological quality, which forces us to take even this modest conclusion with caution.

A more recent and more demanding review reaches the same destination. Jennifer Schlie and colleagues published in the *Journal of Applied Physiology* (2025) a synthesis that admitted only studies that verified the phases with hormonal measurements of 17β-estradiol, progesterone, and luteinizing hormone, discarding mere calendar counting. Across 19 high-standard studies, the pattern held: on average, performance might be trivially reduced in the early follicular phase versus the other phases. That two reviews with different criteria converge reinforces the idea that the average effect is real but small.

### The Four Phases, in a Table

The cycle lasts about 28 days on average, although the variation between women and between months is wide. The table summarizes the hormonal profile of each phase and what the evidence says about performance, using a 28-day cycle as the reference.

| Phase | Approx. days | Dominant hormones | What the evidence says |
|-------|--------------|-------------------|------------------------|
| Early follicular (menstruation) | 1–5 | Low estrogen and progesterone | Slightly lower average performance (SUCRA 30%) |
| Late follicular (pre-ovulation) | 6–13 | High estrogen, low progesterone | Slightly better than early follicular |
| Ovulation | ~14 | LH peak, elevated estrogen | No consistent difference |
| Mid-luteal | 18–24 | High progesterone, moderate estrogen | Higher basal temperature; main impact on thermoregulation |

The luteal phase deserves a separate note because of temperature. Elevated progesterone raises basal body temperature by 0.3 to 0.5 °C, which does not change the power you can produce in cool conditions but does narrow the thermal margin on a hot ride. For a rider training in summer, that matters more for planning hydration and cooling than for calculating power zones.

## From the Lab to the Road: Data from Real Cyclists

The leap from the lab to the peloton came from a French study that analyzed 5,190 training sessions. Hugo Carlin and colleagues published in *Frontiers in Sports and Active Living* (2024) the tracking of 13 elite cyclists—seven with natural cycles and six on oral contraceptives—across 81 complete cycles recorded over 30 months. Rather than measuring performance in an isolated test, they grouped the sessions by external load into four types: intensive, long, medium, and light. The question was whether the phase of the cycle was reflected in how they actually trained, day to day.

The central finding was 8% more intensity at mid-cycle. In the intensive sessions, the intensity index peaked toward the middle of the cycle and was 8% higher than that recorded during menstruation (p = 0.032), with no differences in the gentler session types. Put another way, when these cyclists felt capable, they pushed harder spontaneously, and that moment tended to fall far from the days of their period. It was not that menstruation kept them from training; it was that the body chose its highest-intensity days elsewhere in the cycle.

The group using oral contraceptives erased that pattern. Carlin et al. (2024) found no significant differences in the intensity index between the phases of pill use in any of the four session types. It has a physiological logic: monophasic contraception flattens the natural hormonal swings, so the "signal" of the phase disappears. For a rider on the pill, periodizing by cycle loses its point; for one with a natural cycle, it can be a useful tool.

## Why the Average Misleads

The variability between women is greater than the average effect of the phase. An effect size of −0.06 describes the center of a wide distribution: there will be cyclists who perform markedly worse on their period because of dysmenorrhea or anemia, others who notice nothing, and some who feel better in the middle of menstruation. Averaging everyone into a single number hides precisely the information each athlete needs about herself. That is why the correct conclusion is not "the phase doesn't matter," but "the group effect is small and the individual one has to be measured."

Cycle tracking is the only way to move from the average to your own case. Noting over two or three months how you feel in quality sessions, alongside objective data such as power in intervals, heart rate, or perceived effort, reveals the personal pattern far better than any meta-analysis. That is the logic the cyclists in Carlin's study applied without setting out to: they adjusted intensity to how their bodies responded, not to a universal rule.

## How to Periodize with Data, Not Taboos

The practical application begins by not canceling anything because of the phase. If a race or a key session falls on menstruation, the evidence says the average impact on performance is trivial, so treating it as a lost day means giving away training. The sensible thing is to compete and train normally, and to reserve adjustments for when the symptoms—pain, heavy bleeding, marked fatigue—are intense enough to justify it in your particular case.

The second layer is placing quality blocks where the body tolerates them best. For many cyclists with natural cycles, that moment appears after menstruation and toward the late follicular phase, in line with the 8% pattern from Carlin et al. (2024). If your records confirm you perform better then, it makes sense to schedule power tests, high-intensity sessions, or the races you can plan there. If you use hormonal contraception, on the other hand, it is better to forget the phase and periodize by accumulated fatigue and the competition calendar, because the hormonal signal is no longer there.

## Track Your Cycle, Not the Taboo

The number that sums it all up is that trivial −0.06, but read with nuance. The best available evidence, in both McNulty et al. (2020) and Schlie et al. (2025), says the phase of the cycle barely moves average performance, and that the worst average moment—the days of the period—is so by a narrow margin. The field data from Carlin et al. (2024) add the missing piece: within that flat average, real cyclists do adjust their intensity to the phase, 8% more at mid-cycle. The conclusion is not to fear your period or to ignore it, but to track your own cycle and let your data, not an inherited taboo, decide when you push and when you ease off.
