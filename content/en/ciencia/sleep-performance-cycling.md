## The measurable cost of a bad night

A single night without sleep lengthens a cycling time trial by 10%. That figure comes from a study by Roberts et al. (2019) with 13 endurance cyclists in a crossover design: after one night of total sleep deprivation, time trial completion rose from 59 ± 4 minutes with normal sleep to 64 ± 7 minutes without it (Roberts et al., 2019, *Journal of Sports Sciences*, PMID: 31526108). Perceived exertion spiked, mean reaction time increased by 8%, and anaerobic threshold showed a strong negative correlation (R = -0.73) with performance deterioration. These are not subjective feelings. This is power lost, measurable in watts.

The accumulated evidence confirms that pattern. A meta-analysis by Lopes et al. (2023) reviewed 31 studies with 478 participants and found that sleep deprivation has a moderate negative effect on endurance performance, with a standardized mean difference of -0.52 (95% CI: -0.67 to -0.38). The most relevant finding for cyclists: exercises lasting more than 30 minutes are the most vulnerable. A 40 km time trial or a mountain stage suffers more than a short sprint (Lopes et al., 2023, *European Journal of Sport Science*, PMID: 36472094). Effort duration amplifies the damage of sleep loss.

## Partial restriction: the real-world cyclist scenario

Total sleep deprivation is an experimental extreme. What a cyclist actually experiences during a heavy training block or a grand tour is different: partial restriction, nights of 4 or 5 hours instead of 7 or 8. Dean et al. (2023) measured exactly this scenario in 10 well-trained cyclists. After a single night restricted to 3 hours between two consecutive days of exercise, peak power in a 6-second sprint dropped from 1250 W to 1159 W — a 7% reduction. Mean power during a 20-minute time trial fell from 255 W to 237 W, a 7% loss that in a race could mean minutes of difference (Dean et al., 2023, *Journal of Sleep Research*, PMID: 37012650).

The striking finding in that study is that conventional physiological markers failed to detect the problem. There were no significant differences in heart rate, blood lactate, or glucose between the sleep-restricted and control conditions. The body appeared to respond identically at the metabolic level, yet power output dropped. Cyclists reported worse overall wellness (14.5 vs 16 arbitrary units), suggesting the deterioration operates through central mechanisms — nervous system fatigue, reduced motivation, altered muscle fiber recruitment — rather than any clear peripheral limitation.

## What happens inside the brain and muscles while you sleep

During slow-wave sleep (stage N3 of non-REM sleep), the brain dramatically reduces its metabolic activity and the body enters an anabolic state. The pituitary gland releases between 70% and 80% of daily growth hormone (GH) during these deep sleep episodes, concentrated mainly in the first hours of the night. GH activates muscle protein synthesis, stimulates collagen production in tendons and ligaments, and promotes fatty acid oxidation. A review by Halson (2014) in *Sports Medicine* detailed how sleep deprivation alters glucose metabolism, neuroendocrine function, and protein synthesis, creating a hormonal environment that hinders muscle recovery (Halson, 2014, *Sports Medicine*, PMID: 24791913).

UC Berkeley researchers demonstrated in 2025 that growth hormone-releasing hormone (GHRH) and somatostatin operate differentially during REM and non-REM sleep phases. During REM sleep, both somatostatin and GHRH surge simultaneously to regulate GH release. This finding reveals that sleeping many hours is not enough: sleep architecture — the proportion and distribution of phases — determines the effectiveness of hormonal recovery. Sleep fragmented by noise, inadequate temperature, or alcohol consumption may have acceptable duration but deficient internal structure.

## Sleeping more: sleep extension and real gains

If losing sleep impairs performance, does gaining it improve it? The most cited study in this direction was conducted by Mah et al. (2011) at Stanford with 11 collegiate basketball players. Over 5 to 7 weeks of sleep extension, total nightly sleep time increased by 110.9 ± 79.7 minutes. Sprint times improved from 16.2 to 15.5 seconds, free throw percentage rose by 9%, and three-point shooting accuracy increased by 9.2%. Reaction times decreased and daytime sleepiness scores dropped (Mah et al., 2011, *Sleep*, PMID: 21731144).

This is not a cycling study, but the mechanisms are transferable. A systematic review by Bonnar et al. (2018) concluded that increasing sleep duration — either overnight or through napping — was the most effective intervention for improving physical and cognitive performance in competitive athletes (Bonnar et al., 2018, *Sports Medicine*, PMID: 29352373). The recommendation emerging from the literature is to extend sleep by 46 to 113 minutes per night in athletes who habitually sleep around 7 hours. For a cyclist training 15 hours per week, that means aiming for 8.5-9 hours of sleep instead of the 7 that many consider sufficient.

## The strategic nap: 20 minutes that change the afternoon

Naps of 20 to 30 minutes — known as power naps — have shown measurable benefits in athletes, particularly when taken between 1:00 PM and 3:00 PM. A randomized crossover study with elite rugby athletes demonstrated that a roughly 35-minute nap improved afternoon peak power in a 6-second sprint, reduced perceived exertion during exercise, and improved fatigue and general muscle soreness levels (*Sleep*, 2023). The effect was more pronounced in athletes who had slept poorly the previous night.

Duration matters. Naps shorter than 20 minutes limit access to restorative sleep phases. Naps longer than 35-40 minutes allow entry into slow-wave sleep, which provides greater recovery benefits but carries a risk: sleep inertia, that sensation of grogginess and reduced performance that can last up to 30 minutes after waking. A systematic review published in *Nature and Science of Sleep* found that napping's physical performance benefits are greater in sleep-restricted athletes than in well-rested ones. In practice, this means napping functions more as a rescue tool than a luxury add-on: it works best when it is needed most.

## How the professionals sleep during the Tour de France

The Tour de France pushes the limits of human recovery: 21 stages in 23 days, with daily efforts of 4 to 6 hours at submaximal and supramaximal intensities. Sargent et al. (2024) studied the sleep of 17 professional cyclists (8 men from the Tour de France, 9 women from the Tour de France Femmes) using tracking devices during competition. Baseline data showed that men slept 7.2 ± 0.3 hours with 87% efficiency, while women slept 7.7 ± 0.3 hours with 88.8% efficiency (Sargent et al., 2024, *Sports Medicine - Open*, PMID: 38625486).

Mountain stages made a clear difference. In men, heart rate variability during sleep reached its lowest levels after mountain stages, indicating persistent sympathetic activation that hinders overnight recovery. In women, the percentage of light sleep was highest after these stages, suggesting a reduction in the proportion of restorative deep sleep. Professional cyclists reported needing 8.2 hours of sleep per night to feel recovered, but rarely achieved this during the race.

Professional teams have turned sleep optimization into a measurable competitive advantage. Team Visma-Lease a Bike travels with a full van of mattresses and accessories during the Giro, Tour, and Vuelta. They install portable blackout curtains, dehumidifiers, and air conditioning units in hotel rooms to standardize rest conditions night after night. The logic is direct: if performance drops between 5% and 10% after a week of reduced sleep, every detail that improves rest quality has a quantifiable return.

## Blue light and the silent sabotage of sleep

Melatonin, the hormone that regulates the sleep-wake cycle, is sensitive to light wavelength. Specialized retinal photoreceptors (intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells) respond most intensely to blue light in the 460-480 nm range — exactly the dominant wavelength of phone, tablet, and monitor screens. When these photoreceptors are activated at night, they send signals to the suprachiasmatic nucleus that inhibit pineal melatonin secretion, delaying circadian phase and increasing sleep onset latency.

A systematic review published in *Frontiers in Physiology* (2022) analyzed the influence of blue light on sleep, performance, and well-being in young adults. The findings were nuanced: one-fifth of studies reported decreased sleep quality, one-third found reduced sleep duration, and nearly half detected increased sleep onset latency. For athletes, the scenario has a particular twist: the same blue light that suppresses melatonin and impairs sleep can improve alertness, reaction time, and cognitive performance during the day. The problem is not blue light itself but the timing of exposure.

The evidence-backed practical recommendation is to limit screen exposure at least 60 minutes before bedtime. Blue-light blocking glasses show modest results: a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials found small improvements in total sleep time and sleep efficiency, with clearer benefits in perceived subjective quality than in objective measurements. The most effective strategy remains the simplest: ambient darkness and distance from screens.

## Evidence-based sleep hygiene for cyclists

Vitale et al. (2019) published a comprehensive review of sleep hygiene practices for optimizing recovery in athletes. Their conclusions support a set of interventions that individually seem obvious but together form a protocol with cumulative effect (Vitale et al., 2019, *International Journal of Sports Medicine*, PMID: 31288293).

Room temperature should be maintained between 18°C and 20°C (64-68°F). Thermoregulation plays a central role in sleep induction: the decline in core body temperature is a physiological signal that facilitates the transition to deep sleep phases. An environment that is too warm keeps peripheral temperature elevated and hinders that decline. Darkness should be total or near-total, using blackout curtains or a sleep mask. Any light source, even low intensity, can activate retinal photoreceptors and reduce melatonin production.

Schedule consistency is another factor with consistent support. Going to bed and waking up at the same time — even on weekends — stabilizes circadian rhythm and improves sleep efficiency. Fullagar et al. (2015) noted in their review that reduced sleep quality and quantity can generate an autonomic nervous system imbalance that mimics overtraining syndrome symptoms (Fullagar et al., 2015, *Sports Medicine*, PMID: 25315456). This means a cyclist with chronically deficient sleep can present HRV metrics, perceived fatigue, and declining performance identical to those of an overtrained athlete, when the real problem is simply not sleeping enough.

## The cheapest tool in the peloton

Sleep costs nothing. It requires no specialized equipment and no platform subscriptions. It has no side effects and no risk of a positive doping test. And yet it is the recovery variable most frequently sacrificed. A cyclist who invests in an €800 power meter but sleeps 6 hours per night is measuring a performance they actively sabotage every night.

The evidence is clear in its direction, though nuanced in its exact mechanisms: habitually sleeping less than 7 hours compromises power output, endurance, and the cognitive capacity needed for race decisions. Extending sleep to 8.5-9 hours improves performance. Naps of 20-30 minutes function as a rescue intervention when overnight sleep has been insufficient. Temperature, darkness, and schedule consistency are the three environmental variables with the strongest experimental support for improving rest quality.

Team Visma does not travel with a van full of mattresses out of superstition. They do it because the difference between 7 and 8 hours of quality sleep, multiplied across 21 stages, can be the difference between the yellow jersey and the peloton. For the amateur cyclist looking to improve their times, the first intervention should not be a new interval plan or a beta-alanine supplement. It should be turning off the screen, closing the curtain, and setting the alarm one hour later.
