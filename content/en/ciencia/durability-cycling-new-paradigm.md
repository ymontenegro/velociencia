## The attack that breaks the physiological mold

Tadej Pogačar attacks the Mur de Huy after 195 kilometers of racing and four climbs, with more than 3800 kJ of accumulated work in his legs. Jonas Vingegaard wins the decisive Tour de France time trial the day after climbing the Galibier. Remco Evenepoel finishes a five-hour World Championship road race with a 1400-watt sprint in the final kilometer. These scenes share something that the classic physiology of cycling did not fully explain: the dominant cyclists of this generation do not win because they produce more watts when fresh, but because they produce nearly the same watts when they should be cooked.

For thirty years, cycling performance was measured by looking at three numbers in a fresh state: VO2max, lactate threshold, and FTP. The lab delivered a physiological profile based on 20- or 60-minute tests performed with rested legs. Those data predicted time trial performance well, but left a dark zone: why two cyclists with the same FTP finished six-hour races in radically different positions. The answer began to emerge in 2021 with a concept that the scientific literature called durability.

## The definition that changed the framework

Maunder et al. (2021), in a review published in *Sports Medicine*, proposed defining durability as "the time of onset and the magnitude of deterioration in physiological-profiling characteristics over time during prolonged exercise." The central idea of the paper was that physiological attributes are not static. VO2max, critical power, ventilatory threshold, and pedaling economy change as work accumulates. A cyclist with a critical power of 330 W at 0 kJ may drop to 280 W after 3000 kJ of racing. Another with the same fresh critical power may only drop to 315 W. That difference in drop is what determines who is still attacking in the final twenty kilometers.

Maunder's proposal dismantled an implicit assumption of performance physiology. Classic tests were run in a fresh state because it was assumed the ranking between cyclists remained stable under fatigue. Evidence from recent years showed that this ranking reorders as kilojoules pass. Some cyclists hold up better, others worse, and that characteristic is partially independent of their fresh power.

## The study that named the phenomenon

Van Erp et al. (2021), in *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise*, published the analysis that consolidated durability as a predictive variable of success. The team collected power data from 26 professional cyclists over 85 seasons between 2012 and 2019, classifying them as climbers or sprinters and ranking them in category 1 (successful, with 400 or more ProCyclingStats points) or category 2 (less successful, below 400 points). They analyzed maximal mean power values across different durations after 0, 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 kJ/kg of accumulated work.

The central finding was counterintuitive. In the fresh state, at 0 kJ/kg, differences between successful and less successful cyclists were marginal. Maximal power at five minutes, one minute, or twenty minutes was similar across categories. The divergence appeared with accumulated fatigue. After 45-50 kJ/kg of work, successful cyclists maintained significantly more power than their less successful peers across nearly all durations. The number that best discriminated was not how much they produced fresh, but how much they lost when four hours of racing were in the legs.

## WorldTour versus ProTeam at La Vuelta

Muriel et al. (2022), in *European Journal of Sport Science*, analyzed 15 professional cyclists during La Vuelta a España 2020: eight at the WorldTour level and seven at the ProTeam level. The design allowed comparison between two high-performance groups within professional cycling, not professionals against amateurs. The question was whether durability distinguished the best from the good.

In the first week of the Vuelta, with cyclists still fresh, differences between WorldTour and ProTeam appeared only in thirty-minute efforts. Outside that specific window, both groups produced similar powers. Everything changed once 35 kJ/kg of work was accumulated. At that load, WorldTour riders maintained significantly more power than ProTeam riders across efforts from thirty seconds to thirty minutes. The ability to repeat efforts above 95% of maximal mean power, by contrast, did not differentiate the two groups. The authors concluded that durability, not repeatability, functions as the real performance discriminator between levels of professional cycling.

## The 40 kilojoules per kilo threshold

Valenzuela et al. (2022), in the *International Journal of Sports Physiology and Performance*, designed a more controlled field study. Twelve male professional cyclists (mean age 26 years, VO2max of 83 ml/kg/min) performed two twenty-minute time trials separated by 48 hours. One in a fresh state, the other after four hours of submaximal riding that accumulated around 40 kJ/kg of prior work. Mean power dropped from 386 W fresh to 375 W in the fatigued state, an average reduction of 2.9%.

The relevant detail was the individual dispersion. Some cyclists lost up to 8.5% of their power, others lost nothing or even improved by 1.1%. That variability was not explained by VO2max or ventilatory threshold measured in the lab. Cyclists with nearly identical fresh physiological profiles showed radically different durability under fatigue. The study consolidated 40 kJ/kg as the reference threshold where durability begins to clearly discriminate between cyclists, and demonstrated that classic tests are not enough to predict it.

## Intensity matters more than volume

Mateo-March et al. (2024), in the *Journal of Science and Medicine in Sport*, investigated what type of accumulated work most damages durability. They analyzed 17 professional cyclists exposed to equivalent total-work loads but at different intensities. The result was clear: work accumulated above critical power caused significant drops in all maximal durations, between 1.7% and 4.0%. Critical power itself decreased between 2.2% and 16.2% after such high-intensity work. In contrast, equivalent work in kilojoules but performed below critical power produced no measurable deterioration.

The methodological implication was strong. Counting accumulated kilojoules without differentiating intensities is a poor approximation of real stress. Two cyclists who accumulate 3500 kJ in a stage can arrive at the finish with very different durability levels if one completed them in zone 2 and the other alternated easy pedaling with attacks above threshold. Coaches who prescribe volume without considering the distribution of intensities within that volume are leaving a key variable uncontrolled.

## What predicts durability from the lab

Spragg, Leo, and Swart (2023), in *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise*, measured durability as the change in critical power between a fresh and a fatigued state (delta CP) in ten young professional cyclists with a mean age of 19.2 years and a VO2max of 74.4 ml/kg/min. They correlated that delta with variables obtained in standard lab tests. Three measures showed high correlation with durability: relative peak power output (r = 0.891), VO2max (r = 0.835), and gross efficiency at 200 W (r = 0.792). A fourth correlation, with negative sign, was carbohydrate oxidation at 200 W (r = -0.702). The higher the efficiency of burning carbohydrates during submaximal efforts, the worse the durability.

The data on substrate oxidation points to metabolic resilience as one piece of the puzzle. Cyclists with a greater capacity to oxidize fat at moderate intensities protect their glycogen reserves, which lets them reach the final stretch of the race with substrate available for intense efforts. Durability, read through this lens, is in part a consequence of metabolic flexibility accumulated through years of volume at low intensities.

## Training to last longer

Spragg, Leo, and Swart (2022), in another study published in *European Journal of Sport Science*, followed 30 under-23 professional cyclists across a competitive season. The results showed that the fatigued power profile varies significantly throughout the season while the fresh profile remains relatively stable. Maximal five- and twelve-minute power in the fatigued state was lower at the end of the season than in the early months. Training volume below the first ventilatory threshold correlated with better fatigue resistance in two-minute efforts. Teams adopting a polarized intensity distribution showed improvements in fatigued twelve-minute power between training phases.

Durability is therefore trainable and responds to different stimuli than those that raise FTP. Volume in zone 1 develops the mitochondrial base, capillary network, and oxidative capacity that sustain performance when glycogen runs low. Punctual intensity above threshold refines peak power. Both stimuli are necessary, but one does not replace the other: a cyclist who only does high-intensity intervals can have a high FTP and mediocre durability. One who only does volume will have durability but a limited power ceiling.

## What it means for a cyclist outside the WorldTour

Durability matters at every scale of endurance cycling. A 160-kilometer sportive accumulates between 30 and 40 kJ/kg for an amateur: the threshold where the literature detects the first significant drop in performance. Whoever reaches the last climb with the ability to repeat their threshold power will finish minutes ahead of whoever has lost 8% along the way. In long mountain granfondos, long gravel events, or three-hour races, the question is not what your FTP is, but what is left in your legs at kilometer 120.

Training for durability has practical translations. Long sessions in zone 2 of three to five hours build the oxidative base. Intervals performed at the end of long rides, not at the start, mimic real race demand and train the capacity to produce watts with low glycogen. In-ride nutrition, with 80-120 grams of well-tolerated carbohydrates per hour, protects durability by reducing the drop in available substrate. None of these practices are new. What is new is the conceptual framework that explains why they work and which physiological indicator is moving. Durability does not replace VO2max or FTP as metrics, but it is the missing piece that explains why two cyclists with the same lab results finish the race in different places.
