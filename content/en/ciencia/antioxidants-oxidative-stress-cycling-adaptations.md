## The paradox of the cyclist who takes too much care

An amateur cyclist finishes a threshold interval set, opens the supplement pouch and swallows a 1000 mg capsule of vitamin C with 400 IU of vitamin E. The reasoning feels solid: exercise generates free radicals, those radicals oxidise muscle tissue, antioxidants neutralise the damage. He does this every day, convinced it speeds recovery and unlocks better performance. The evidence published over the last decade suggests he is doing exactly the opposite. By neutralising the free radicals produced during exercise, he is silencing the signal that tells his muscle to adapt.

The paradox is uncomfortable because it contradicts decades of marketing messages. Vitamins C and E are sold as protection against athletic oxidative stress, and the logic seems airtight: if exercise oxidises, an antioxidant protects. The problem is that trained muscle does not behave like a rusting metal. The reactive oxygen species it produces during contraction act as signalling molecules, not as toxins. When a megadose supplement wipes them out, the muscle stops receiving the instruction to build more mitochondria, more capillaries and more oxidative enzymes.

## What ROS are and why muscle makes them on purpose

Reactive oxygen species, known as ROS, are oxygen-derived molecules with an unpaired electron that makes them chemically reactive. Superoxide anion, hydrogen peroxide and hydroxyl radical are the three main actors in the context of exercise. During an intense cycling session, muscle mitochondria increase their baseline ROS production several fold, while other systems such as NADPH oxidase and xanthine oxidase also raise the local concentration inside the active muscle fibre.

The classical view treated that rise as a toxic by-product of aerobic metabolism. The current view, built on two decades of molecular exercise biology, treats it as an indispensable signal. ROS at physiological concentrations activate transcription factors such as PGC-1α, AMPK and Nrf2, the master switches of mitochondrial biogenesis, glucose metabolism and the endogenous antioxidant response. Without that transient stimulus, the muscle does not receive the order to remodel itself.

## The experiment that changed the conversation

Ristow et al. (2009), in a study published in *PNAS*, randomised 39 healthy young men, both trained and untrained, to a four-week exercise programme with or without a daily supplement of 1000 mg of vitamin C and 400 IU of vitamin E. Exercise improved insulin sensitivity measured by hyperinsulinaemic-euglycaemic clamp, one of the most consistent metabolic benefits of training. That benefit vanished entirely in the antioxidant group.

The molecular analysis explained the mechanism. In muscle biopsies from the placebo group, exercise induced the expression of PGC-1α, PGC-1β, PPARγ and the endogenous antioxidant enzymes superoxide dismutase and glutathione peroxidase. In the antioxidant group, that induction was blocked or severely blunted. The supplements did not just prevent the functional metabolic adaptation — they prevented the muscle from switching on its own antioxidant defence machinery, because that activation depends on the ROS stimulus itself.

## The mitochondrion that does not grow

Gomez-Cabrera et al. (2008) had anticipated the finding a year earlier in the *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition*. Combining experiments in rats and humans, they showed that oral administration of high-dose vitamin C reduced mitochondrial biogenesis induced by endurance training. In rats, the supplement lowered the expression of PGC-1α, nuclear respiratory factor 1 (NRF1) and mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM), three essential regulators of mitochondrial DNA replication. In humans, VO2max improved by 22% in the placebo group and only 11% in the vitamin C group, a statistically significant difference with a daily dose of 1000 mg.

Paulsen et al. (2014) replicated the pattern with a larger and longer trial in the *Journal of Physiology*. Fifty-four men and women trained for eleven weeks with three to four running sessions a week, including high-intensity intervals. Half received 1000 mg of vitamin C and 235 mg of vitamin E daily; the other half received placebo. Running performance improved equally in both groups, but muscle biopsies revealed that the content of COX4, a critical subunit of complex IV of the mitochondrial respiratory chain, increased only in the placebo group. The supplement also blunted acute PGC-1α signalling after the intense sessions.

## Hormesis: the dose makes the signal

The concept that organises the entire body of evidence is mitochondrial hormesis, or mitohormesis. Merry and Ristow (2016), in an integrative review in the *Journal of Physiology*, synthesised the available data and proposed a clear principle. A transient, moderate oxidative stimulus activates protective adaptations, while chronic and excessive oxidative stress produces damage. The curve is not linear; it is an inverted U. Too little ROS produces no adaptation; too much produces disease. Training creates the transient spike that falls in the productive window.

Megadose antioxidants flatten that curve and erase the spike. The muscle still receives the mechanical contraction but not the chemical signal that normally accompanies it, and it responds as if the stimulus were smaller. That is the mechanism by which cyclists who train with chronic antioxidant supplementation show less gain in mitochondrial biogenesis, less increase in oxidative enzymes and, over the medium term, smaller improvements in metabolic health markers, even if absolute performance in some tests keeps progressing.

## What the most recent review concludes

Mason et al. (2020), in a mechanistic review published in *Redox Biology*, analysed the accumulated evidence on antioxidant supplementation and endurance performance. Their conclusion was nuanced. Chronic intake of 1000 mg of vitamin C combined with vitamin E during periods of heavy training is not recommended, because it interferes with key skeletal muscle adaptations. N-acetylcysteine may have an acute ergogenic effect in the days before an endurance event, acting as a glutathione donor during prolonged efforts. Melatonin, vitamin E alone and alpha-lipoic acid reduce markers of oxidative stress but do not consistently improve performance.

The operational takeaway is that most commercial antioxidants lack evidence of performance benefit, and several actively interfere with training adaptations. The initial enthusiasm for "protecting the athlete from oxidative damage" did not translate into demonstrable gains. For a healthy cyclist training at high load, the risk-benefit balance of chronic supplementation with pharmacological doses of vitamins C and E is probably negative.

## When an antioxidant does have a place

Evidence against chronic supplementation does not mean antioxidants are useless in every context. During viral or bacterial infections, systemic oxidative stress does produce pathological damage and the endogenous antioxidant defence can be overwhelmed. In chronic inflammatory diseases, in exposure to toxins such as tobacco smoke or heavy urban pollution, or in documented pathological oxidative stress confirmed by clinical biomarkers, targeted and supervised supplementation may make therapeutic sense.

For the cyclist, scenarios in which a targeted antioxidant might be reasonable include a very specific competition where the goal is acute performance rather than chronic adaptation, recovery from a recent infection, or extreme environmental conditions such as a multi-day stage race with intense heat and altitude exposure. Outside these contexts, the equation inverts: what is gained in reduced oxidation markers is lost in adaptations that never occurred.

## Food is not the same as a pill

A key detail separates pharmacological supplementation from dietary intake. When a cyclist eats a cup of berries, he ingests about 50 to 100 mg of vitamin C together with dozens of polyphenols, fibre and phytochemicals that reach the gut and are absorbed in a complex matrix. When he takes a 1000 mg capsule, he is delivering a dose ten to twenty times larger in isolated form, with pharmacokinetics that push plasma concentration to levels the endogenous system never encounters from a normal diet.

The studies that showed adaptation blockade used pharmacological, not dietary, doses. No trial has shown that eating fruit, vegetables, nuts or legumes regularly interferes with mitochondrial biogenesis. On the contrary, polyphenol-rich diets are associated with better metabolic and cardiovascular profiles in athletes and non-athletes alike. The difference lies in the dose, the chemical form and the matrix in which the compound reaches the tissue.

That allows a simple recommendation. Prioritising a diet with high density of colourful vegetables, berries, nuts, pure cacao, green tea and extra virgin olive oil covers the dietary antioxidant intake without risking adaptation blockade. The doses obtained from whole food are orders of magnitude smaller than those used in the interference studies.

## Practical recommendation for the cyclist

The operational reading of the evidence is direct. A healthy cyclist training to improve aerobic capacity, sustained power or metabolic health should not take vitamin C or vitamin E chronically at doses above the reference daily intake. Moderate-dose multivitamins, providing around 100% of the daily requirement, are not implicated in the blockade studies and there is no solid reason to avoid them if the diet is poor.

For post-training recovery, foods naturally rich in antioxidants, such as berries, tart cherries, green tea or cacao, are reasonable choices and show no interference with adaptation. Adequate hydration, sufficient sleep and the combination of protein with carbohydrate after intense sessions are interventions supported by far more robust evidence than any antioxidant capsule.

When a cyclist considers supplementation, the right question is not "will this protect me from oxidative damage?" but "which adaptation am I willing to sacrifice for that supposed benefit?". Exercise-induced free radicals are the tool with which muscle builds its own upgraded version. Neutralising them with a capsule is equivalent to paying to dismantle the construction before it is finished.
