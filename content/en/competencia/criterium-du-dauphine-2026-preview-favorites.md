On June 7, the peloton rolls out from Vizille, a small town south of Grenoble where in 1788 the assembly that ignited the French Revolution was convened. What begins there carries a different kind of historical weight: for the first time in 77 editions, the Critérium du Dauphiné will not be called the Critérium du Dauphiné. The race returns under the name Tour Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes, rebranded after the host region increased its financial backing by 18 percent, pushing the budget to roughly eleven million euros. The name changes. The mountains do not.

Since 1947, this race has been the most reliable barometer for the Tour de France. Four of the last ten Dauphiné winners also won the Tour that same year. The list includes Chris Froome in 2013, 2015 and 2016, Geraint Thomas in 2018 and Jonas Vingegaard in 2023. It is the only race ever won by all five riders with the most Tour de France victories — Anquetil, Merckx, Hinault, Indurain and Froome. The 78th edition carries a new institutional identity but retains every gram of its relevance.

## Eight stages, one brutal finale

The 2026 edition covers 1,204.3 kilometers across eight stages with 19,850 meters of total elevation gain. There is no individual time trial, which is unusual for a race that has historically featured one, but there is a 28.4-kilometer team time trial on Tuesday June 9 around the Perreux circuit. The organizers describe it as a dress rehearsal for the Grand Départ — the 2026 Tour de France starts in Barcelona with a team time trial of its own. Two different races, same opening format, same tactical template.

Stage 1 from Vizille is not a formality. Five categorized climbs across 146 kilometers, ending with the Côte de Rousset (8.2 km at 7.6%), the kind of opening stage that looks manageable on paper but tends to expose someone before the racing even begins. Stage 2 covers 234 kilometers — longer than any Tour de France stage this year — an endurance test through the Massif Central that arrives the day before the team time trial. The three-day block of long rolling stage, team TTT and hilly transition is a deliberate compression designed to test team depth before the mountains take over entirely.

## The triptych that decides everything

The real general classification differences develop over the final three stages. On Friday June 12, Stage 6 delivers the first summit finish to Crest-Voland: 5.9 kilometers at 7.7%, a new arrival in the Dauphiné's history, preceded by the Col du Granier. This stage will thin the leading group to whatever survives the week. It functions as an X-ray before the two days that truly matter.

Stage 7 on Saturday June 13 finishes at the Grand Colombier. The final climb measures 8.4 kilometers at 10.2% average gradient from Virieu-le-Petit — one of the steepest sustained ascents in European racing. Earlier in the 133.6-kilometer stage the peloton crosses the Lacets du Grand Colombier (7 km at 8.4%) and the Col de Richemond. Total elevation gain: 3,720 meters. In a stage this short and this loaded, there is no place to hide.

Stage 8 on Sunday June 14 is where the race ends and the analysis begins. Eleven kilometers at 9.1% average to the Plateau de Solaison, preceded by the Col du Pré (6.9 km at 10.1%), the Montée de Bisanne (11.4 km at 7.7%) and the Col des Aravis (7 km at 6.8%). The route provides no gentle introduction: climbing starts from kilometer zero in Beaufort and does not stop until Solaison. The organization calls it "the most demanding summit arrival of the European spring season." The altitude profile confirms the description without requiring embellishment.

## Vingegaard: the arithmetic of the impossible

Jonas Vingegaard's situation is the most difficult to calibrate in this Dauphiné. On May 31, assuming the Giro d'Italia finishes according to plan, the Visma-Lease a Bike Dane crosses the finish line in Rome. On June 7, seven days later, he rolls out from Vizille. In the modern history of professional cycling, it is rare for a rider targeting the Grand Tour podium to appear a week later as a favorite for an eight-stage Alpine race. Roglič has attempted similar sequences before; the fatigue residue always shows somewhere.

Visma confirmed Vingegaard's entry on April 30, alongside Wout van Aert and Matteo Jorgenson. The presence of Van Aert in the team time trial is not a minor detail — on the Perreux circuit, Visma carries the horsepower to build gaps that neither Roglič nor del Toro can fully recover in the mountains. The form picture justifies the ambition. Vingegaard won the 2026 Paris-Nice by four minutes and 23 seconds over Daniel Felipe Martínez, a margin that borders on anomalous for a week-long stage race in March. At the Giro, he has won stages and arrives at the second week just 27 seconds behind leader Afonso Eulálio.

The question is not form. It is accumulation. Competing for the Giro podium requires three weeks at maximum output. Arriving at the Dauphiné seven days later means carrying legs that have already absorbed the Blockhaus, the Passo Giau, the Falzarego and two weeks of Dolomite racing. Even at his level, muscle reserves do not regenerate in a week. The Grand Colombier and the Plateau de Solaison will be the moment of truth: if Vingegaard can match the pace of fresher rivals there, he will arrive at the Tour de France in a state no rider has previously matched before July.

## Roglič: the man who has memorized the route

Primož Roglič is the rider with the deepest tactical knowledge of this type of race and the most specific experience of the 2026 course. He won the Dauphiné in 2022 and 2024. The Slovenian Red Bull-BORA-hansgrohe leader has the Vuelta a España as his primary Grand Tour target this season, which makes the Dauphiné something between a form test and a stage-hunting exercise. Red Bull has positioned Roglič as an option for stages 7 and 8 specifically, while giving Florian Lipowitz latitude to chase the overall podium with fresher legs.

Lipowitz finished second overall at the 2026 Tour de Romandie and brings consistent results from a spring campaign that includes a third place at the Volta a Catalunya. He arrives with the freshness that Roglič may not guarantee after his own racing load. Red Bull's two-card strategy — Roglič for explosive mountain moments, Lipowitz for the general classification — is built on the realistic assessment that neither rider needs this Dauphiné as a career milestone, only as preparation confirmation before the Tour.

## Del Toro: two wins and a point to prove

Isaac del Toro arrives at the Tour Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes as the rider in the sharpest form among the confirmed favorites. The 22-year-old Mexican already has two stage-race victories in 2026 — the UAE Tour and Tirreno-Adriatico, where he beat Matteo Jorgenson by 40 seconds across eight days of racing in central Italy. UAE Team Emirates-XRG lists him as GC leader for this race, the first Dauphiné in three years where Pogačar will not defend. For del Toro, this is the last evaluation before his Tour de France debut, where he will ride alongside Pogačar but with freedom to chase stages and demonstrate that his early-season form was not circumstantial.

His profile matches what the final stages demand: lightweight, explosive on sustained climbs, unencumbered by an individual time trial where heavier rollers would recoup time. The team time trial in Perreux is the one moment where UAE's lineup may not match Visma's depth. Everything else in the final three stages suits del Toro precisely. He has already shown he belongs in the front group on major climbs; the question at Solaison is whether he can impose that pace rather than merely survive it.

## The riders who can disrupt the script

Paul Seixas reaches June with a 2026 season built deliberately around this part of the calendar. The French Decathlon CMA CGM rider has the profile of a climber whose form peaks in early summer, when the field thins and the grades lengthen. Eleven kilometers at 9.1% on the Plateau de Solaison is exactly the terrain where a rider like Seixas can overrun better-known names who arrive with more fatigue in their legs.

Juan Ayuso returns to racing after the injury that kept him out of the Ardennes classics. The Lidl-Trek Spaniard carries an interrupted season but the Dauphiné, without the pressure of a Grand Tour three-week campaign, gives him the conditions to recover rhythm quickly on familiar mountain terrain. Antonio Tiberi — the Bahrain-Victorious leader who finished second at the UAE Tour 2026 — and Tobias Halland Johannessen of Uno-X Mobility complete the group of riders with real capacity to disrupt the outcome on the weekend stages.

## The paradox of the absent

The central paradox of the 2026 Dauphiné is that the two most likely Tour de France champions chose different paths. Tadej Pogačar, the defending race champion, opted for the Tour de Suisse as his final pre-Tour test. The Slovenian, who has already won Strade Bianche, Milan-San Remo and the Tour of Flanders this spring, is building toward a preparation that finishes two weeks before the Barcelona Grand Départ rather than three. Remco Evenepoel has gone 69 days without racing since Liège-Bastogne-Liège — a Red Bull bet on total freshness that involves an altitude camp and no competitive load before July 4. Both decisions are coherent strategies. Together they make this Dauphiné something it has not been in four years: winnable by someone outside the established triumvirate.

## The verdict Solaison will deliver

Roglič arrives with the course memorized and the motivation of a two-time champion who still has something to prove in a year where his team leads with younger riders in June. Del Toro carries the form and the freshness that the others cannot guarantee. Vingegaard brings the level but drags the question of the Giro behind him like a shadow over every climb. If the Dane recovers fully and Visma converts the team time trial into a buffer, the Tour Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes could launch what would be the most powerful July preparation on record. If the Dolomites show at the Grand Colombier on Saturday, the title resolves between del Toro and Roglič — the two riders with the clearest reasons to win. Either outcome, the Plateau de Solaison on Sunday June 14 will tell you more about who is ready for Barcelona than any altitude training session ever could. The thermometer has been working since 1947. Eight stages and a new name have not changed what it measures.
