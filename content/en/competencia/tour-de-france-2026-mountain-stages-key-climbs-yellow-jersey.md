The 113th Tour de France stacks 54,450 metres of vertical gain across eight high-mountain stages between July 4 and 26. For the first time in the race's 123-year history, Alpe d'Huez will serve as a summit finish on two consecutive days — stages 19 and 20 on July 24 and 25. This double ending is not a marketing gimmick; it is a structural decision that concentrates the decisive moments of the general classification into the final 48 hours before Paris.

The route designed by race director Christian Prudhomme builds in a deliberate crescendo. The Pyrenees deliver two opening tests in week one. The Vosges, low-altitude hills that rarely matter in Tour history, appear as a mid-race trap with seven consecutive climbs. And the Alps close the race with a time trial, a first Alpine summit finish, and then the hardest stage the Tour has proposed in over a decade. What follows is a climb-by-climb breakdown of where the yellow jersey will change hands.

## Stage 3: the Pyrenean preview

On July 6, two days after the Barcelona start, the Tour hits its first summit finish. The 196-kilometre stage from Granollers to Les Angles accumulates around 3,850 metres of elevation and crosses from Catalonia into the French Pyrenees. The Col de Toses (9.3 km at 6.5%) and the Col du Calvaire (11.4 km at 4.1%) precede a brief 1.7-kilometre finale at Les Angles (6.5%) that won't produce major time gaps but will reveal the form of the GC contenders in their first real test. This stage is not where the Tour is won, but an early mechanical issue or a rider arriving at the Pyrenees undertrained could cost several minutes.

Les Angles sits at around 1,600 metres and last hosted a Tour stage in the 1980s and 1990s, when Pyrenean ski-station finishes were routine. Its return in 2026 functions as an early snapshot before the real Pyrenean test on stage 6.

## Stage 6: the Tourmalet's 91st Tour appearance

The [Col du Tourmalet](/data/climbs/col-du-tourmalet-luz) is the most-used climb in Tour de France history. On July 9, it makes its 91st appearance in the race during stage 6, which departs Pau and arrives for the first time ever at Gavarnie-Gèdre, a mountain commune of 350 residents near the Cirque de Gavarnie. The 186.2-kilometre stage climbs 4,100 metres and sequences the Col d'Aspin (12 km at 6.5%, summit at 1,490 m) with the Tourmalet via the Sainte-Marie-de-Campan approach (17 km at 7.3%, summit at 2,115 m) before a descent into the final valley approach.

The Gavarnie-Gèdre finish, 18.7 kilometres at a modest 4% average, arrives after more than 200 kilometres of accumulated effort and a major Alpine pass. In Tour history, shallow-gradient finishes after major climbs tend to favour teams that control from the top of the last big pass: the final kilometres become a tactical conversation more than a pure climbing contest. Stage 6 is the first meaningful test of team dynamics before the Alps.

## Stage 14: the Vosges wildcard

On July 18, stage 14 presents a scenario the peloton frequently underestimates. The 155.3-kilometre route from Mulhouse to Le Markstein Fellering packs 3,800 metres of climbing across seven categorised ascents through the Vosges, the low-lying massif of northeastern France. The Grand Ballon — the highest point in the range at 1,424 metres — comes just 36 kilometres into the stage, in a version that includes a newly paved forest track with ramps touching 8–10%. Seven successive climbs without genuine recovery between them make this a muscular endurance test rather than a pure altitude battle.

The Vosges have a specific pattern in Tour history: fatigue accumulates across repeated short efforts rather than a single decisive punch. Riders arriving in the Alps with residual tiredness from stage 14 will face the Alpe d'Huez with a measurably higher handicap. For any rider arriving with accumulated Giro fatigue — Vingegaard being the most likely candidate — stage 14 may prove costlier than the profile suggests.

## Stage 16: the time trial that reorders the race

Stage 16 on July 20 covers 26 kilometres of individual time trial from Evian-les-Bains to Thonon-les-Bains with elevation. It is the race's only individual TT, and one of the few opportunities for specialists to claw back time on pure climbers. Total time trial distance in 2026 amounts to just 45 kilometres between the opening team time trial and this stage — historically low compared to recent editions. In 2024, the TT distance reached 59 kilometres and Pogačar used each one to extend his advantage. In 2026, mountain stages absorb an even larger share of the final margin.

The tactical weight of stage 16 comes from its position immediately before the Alps. A rider leading by two or three minutes here can manage the Alpine stages with freedom; a rider within a minute of the leader will need to attack on passes where the margin for error is significantly smaller.

## Stage 19: the first Alpe d'Huez finish

Stage 19 on July 24 brings Alpe d'Huez back to the Tour for the first time since 2022, after a four-year absence. A route of approximately 130 kilometres threads the Col Bayard, the Col du Noyer, and the Col d'Ornon (5.6 km at 6.2%) before the final ascent of [Alpe d'Huez](/data/climbs/alpe-dhuez). The 13.8 kilometres at 8.1% from Le Bourg-d'Oisans to the summit — 21 numbered hairpin bends, each named after a stage winner — are the most recognisable high-mountain finish in professional cycling.

The climb's last appearance as a stage finish was 2022, when Tom Pidcock soloed to victory with an attack six kilometres from the top that no GC contender could follow. In 2026, the earlier connector climbs create space for long-range moves before the Alpe itself. The first two kilometres of the Alpe average around 10% and represent the decisive filter: pace held through that section determines whether the lead group arrives intact or fractured.

## Stage 20: the queen stage and the roof of the race

Saturday July 25, stage 20, is the hardest day of the Tour. The 170.9-kilometre route from Le Bourg-d'Oisans to [Alpe d'Huez](/data/climbs/alpe-dhuez) for a second consecutive day accumulates 5,600 metres of vertical gain across five passes. The sequencing and volume of that climbing make this stage qualitatively different from anything the Tour has staged in recent years.

The Col de la Croix de Fer opens the day with 24 kilometres at 5.2%. Its length and irregular gradient deplete domestiques from the outset, exposing leaders sooner than planned. The Col du Télégraphe follows with 11.9 kilometres at 7.1% and connects directly into the [Col du Galibier](/data/climbs/col-du-galibier-lautaret) — 17.7 kilometres at 6.9% to 2,642 metres, the highest point of the 2026 Tour. At that altitude, the ability to exchange oxygen becomes the differentiating factor: a rider just below the top level loses rhythm quickly, breathing becomes laboured, and gaps open faster than the same gradient would produce at 1,000 metres.

After the Galibier descent, the Col de Sarenne (12.9 km at 7.3%) provides an alternative approach to Alpe d'Huez from the back side. The final 3.8 kilometres at 6.1% of the classic Alpe hairpin ascent then serve as the stage finish. The Sarenne acts as a positioning trap: riders who reach its last kilometre without the leading group have virtually no way to close the gap on the Alpe. The overall sequence — Croix de Fer, Télégraphe, Galibier, Sarenne, Alpe — is an endurance test designed to exhaust whatever reserves survived stage 19.

## Alpe d'Huez through the lens of time

Alpe d'Huez has been officially timed at the Tour since 1994, though the standardised 13.8 km segment from Le Bourg-d'Oisans only became consistent from 1999 onwards; earlier editions measured from a point approximately 700 metres lower, making direct comparison problematic. On the modern standard, the all-time record is Marco Pantani's 37 minutes 35 seconds from 1997, equivalent to 22.0 km/h. Lance Armstrong recorded comparable times in 2001 (38'01") and 2004 (37'36"), but the doping context of that era complicates any direct comparison. In the modern era of stringent anti-doping controls, the clearest reference point is 2022, when Jonas Vingegaard, Tadej Pogačar and Geraint Thomas reached the summit in 39 minutes and 8 seconds — the fastest verified ascent since 2006.

Thirty-nine minutes over 13.8 km at 8.1% corresponds to estimated power outputs of between 6.0 and 6.4 watts per kilogram depending on the rider's weight. Pogačar, around 66 kg, and Vingegaard, near 60 kg, produce different watt totals but near-identical W/kg figures. The open question for 2026 is whether either rider — or both — can arrive at stage 19's Alpe with enough in reserve to hit those numbers, then repeat the exercise 24 hours later with five more major passes in the legs.

## Where the general classification will be decided

Just 45 total kilometres of time trialling across two stages is the lowest figure in a decade of Tour editions. That arithmetic means the final gaps between Pogačar, Vingegaard and Evenepoel will be written in the mountains, not against the clock. The route concentrates the decisive moments at three points: stage 14 in the Vosges as the first indicator, stage 16's time trial as the reordering mechanism, and stages 19 and 20 as the final verdict.

The consecutive Alpe d'Huez double is tactically unprecedented precisely because it forces all GC contenders to manage two completely different scenarios at the same summit. Stage 19 is an attacking stage: shorter, arriving after connector climbs that suit long-range moves, with the Alpe available for the kind of sustained pressure Pogačar has turned into a signature. Stage 20 is an attrition stage: whoever burned matches the day before arrives depleted, and whoever conserved energy can surprise. That back-and-forth dynamic across the same landmark is unlike anything the Tour has proposed in recent memory. The [Galibier](/data/climbs/col-du-galibier-lautaret) at 2,642 metres on Saturday July 25 will be the race's highest arbiter. What happens on that summit, with 30 kilometres still to ride, will determine who carries yellow to Paris.
