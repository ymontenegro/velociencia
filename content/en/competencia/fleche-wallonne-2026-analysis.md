Paul Seixas climbed the final Mur de Huy in 2 minutes and 43 seconds, the third-fastest ascent ever recorded at La Flèche Wallonne. It was Wednesday, April 22, the 90th edition of the most unforgiving race in the Belgian Ardennes, and the Decathlon CMA CGM rider did it with a bloodied right elbow from a mid-race crash, on his first-ever start in the event, at just 19 years and 210 days old. Nobody in the history of the race had won it younger. Mauro Schmid (Jayco-AlUla) and Ben Tulett (Visma–Lease a Bike) crossed the line three seconds back, together, contesting second place while the Frenchman was already celebrating alone up the road.

## Before the Mur: control, crashes and the protocol breakaway

The 90th Flèche Wallonne rolled out of Herstal with 200 kilometres to cover, 11 categorised climbs and the race's standard template: a six-rider breakaway the peloton was content to let go, provided it never strayed beyond three minutes. UAE Team Emirates-XRG set the tempo through the opening hours, playing the regulator role that falls to them whenever they have a nominal favourite to shepherd into the final circuit. That circuit includes the Côte d'Ereffe and the Côte de Cherave before the Mur de Huy, climbs that tighten the group and put a price on every breakaway move.

The Ardennes had other plans. Former winner Marc Hirschi was forced out by a crash before the decisive sector. Guillaume Martin, Warren Barguil and Diego Ulissi also went down, leaving their teams with reduced options for the finale. Seixas himself crashed during one of the race's most tense passages: he remounted with his right elbow raw and his jersey stained with blood, having lost a few seconds he could not afford to lose in any other context. That it made no difference at all says something precise about the form he carried into Belgium that Wednesday.

## The plan and the improvisation: how Seixas dismantled the Mur

Decathlon CMA CGM's tactical blueprint for the final ascent was textbook. Léo Bisiaux would lead the group to the one-kilometre mark, Jordan Labrosse would take over for the next 300 metres, and Paul Lapeira would burn himself for another 300 metres, leaving Seixas with roughly 400 metres of open road to launch from the front. The idea was to minimise the leader's exposed time on the steepest gradient and maximise the momentum he arrived with when the moment came.

At 800 metres to go, reality diverged from the script. Bisiaux peeled off earlier than planned and Seixas was already on the front, with Ben Tulett and Mauro Schmid on his wheel. The Frenchman did not wait. He attacked from where he was, with Labrosse still visible on the road behind him but no longer in a position to provide guidance. The acceleration came from instinct rather than from a clock, and it was so effective that no rival could respond. Tulett held for the first few metres of Seixas's maximum effort, but when the Frenchman shifted again on the steepest ramp, the Briton had no answer. Schmid arrived three seconds later with Tulett. The total ascent time: 2:43, three seconds short of the all-time record.

## The Mur in context: what 2:43 means in the history of this climb

Julian Alaphilippe holds the all-time Mur de Huy record with 2 minutes and 40 seconds, set during his victory in 2021. Alejandro Valverde — five-time winner of La Flèche Wallonne and the rider who turned the race into his private property for the better part of a decade — recorded 2:41 in his 2014 triumph. Seixas, with 2:43, becomes the third-fastest rider in history up that wall, ahead of the 2:46 Pogačar needed in his 2023 win. For a 19-year-old debutant to enter the historical top three of the Mur is a statistical anomaly that professional cycling takes years to produce.

Context compounds the significance. The conditions on Wednesday were not those of record-setting editions: lower temperatures, damp tarmac on the steepest sections and the accumulated cost of a crash. More revealing: Seixas attacked 400 metres earlier than planned, meaning he sustained maximum effort for longer than he was supposed to. Had the original plan unfolded as designed — the attack coming from 400 metres rather than 800 — Alaphilippe's record would have been genuinely at risk.

## Who is Seixas: from junior Liège winner to Mur de Huy conqueror

On April 11, eleven days before his victory on the Mur, Paul Seixas won the Tour of the Basque Country with three stage wins and the overall classification. He became the first French rider to win a WorldTour stage race in nearly two decades. He is 19 years old. Two seasons ago, at 17, he won the junior edition of Liège-Bastogne-Liège solo — the same race that presents him with the biggest test of his career this Sunday. It is not a minor footnote: Seixas arrived in the Ardennes in 2026 already knowing what it felt like to win Belgium's longest classic, even if it was in a different category.

What sets Seixas apart from other precocious talents is not just the power output on steep gradients — which is exceptional — but the capacity to read the race in real time and adapt his tactics to what is actually happening, not to what was written on the clipboard. When Bisiaux peeled off early, another rider might have waited for Labrosse or lost the window entirely. Seixas attacked. When he crashed mid-race, another rider might have arrived at the Mur with his mind still on the wound. Seixas arrived gunning for the record. That composure, at that age and on a debut, is what has the peloton studying him carefully heading into Sunday.

## The absences and what they actually change

Tadej Pogačar, two-time Flèche Wallonne champion with his victories in 2023 and 2025, was not on the start line in Huy on Wednesday. The Slovenian has his sights on Liège-Bastogne-Liège on Sunday and, further ahead, on the Tour de Romandie as a block of preparation for the Tour de France. Remco Evenepoel made the same call: the Belgian rider, fresh from his Amstel Gold Race win, opted to reserve himself fully for Sunday's monument.

The temptation to discount Seixas's win because of those absences does not hold up to scrutiny. Schmid and Tulett are WorldTour-level riders who arrived at the foot of the Mur under identical conditions to the Frenchman — and neither of them came within three seconds of him. A 2:43 ascent of the Mur de Huy needs no asterisk for absent riders: the gradient does not adjust itself according to who is in the race. The history of La Flèche Wallonne is full of victories built under similar circumstances — Stephen Williams won the 2024 edition in a comparably open field — and nobody has since argued the record-book entry is invalid. Seixas earned this.

## Sunday at Liège: different race, different questions

The 112th edition of Liège-Bastogne-Liège takes place on Sunday, April 26 — the oldest classic on the calendar. The design is nothing like the Mur de Huy: 260 kilometres, ten climbs in the final loop, none of them with the near-vertical ramps of Huy but accumulated elevation that breaks the race in a different way altogether. La Redoute, La Roche aux Faucons and the final drag to Boulevard de la Sauvenière are longer, less explosive walls than the Mur, rewarding riders who can hold high power for four or five minutes rather than the 90-second burst that decides La Flèche Wallonne.

Pogačar and Evenepoel arrive Sunday with their full arsenals intact. The Slovenian has won Liège multiple times and knows the final as well as anyone in the peloton. The Belgian has the advantage of racing on home roads and the demonstrated form from Amstel. But Seixas arrives having just shown the world that he can climb the Mur de Huy at a pace that only two riders in the history of that wall have beaten. He won the junior edition of Liège two years ago. The muscle memory of that finish line is already in his legs. And the entire peloton arrives at Sunday's start having watched Wednesday's performance and still trying to work out exactly what they're dealing with.

Three riders, one podium, the longest race of the spring classic season. In the past twenty years, professional cycling has produced very few debutants who arrive at a monument having already demonstrated they can climb at a pace that challenges historical records. Seixas is one of them. Sunday will tell us with considerably more precision exactly where he stands.
