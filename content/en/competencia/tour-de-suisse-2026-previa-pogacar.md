Tadej Pogačar announced his participation in the Tour de Suisse at UAE Team Emirates-XRG's media day in December. The statement was brief, but its strategic weight is significant: the Slovenian chose this race over the Critérium du Dauphiné — now rebranded as the Tour Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes — as his final competitive outing before the Tour de France. It is the first time since 2022, when Pogačar used the Tour of Slovenia as his last pre-Tour hit-out, that the defending champion arrives at the Grand Départ having raced in the two weeks prior. The decision is not trivial: the Tour de Suisse finishes on June 21. The start from Barcelona is July 4. Between the two lie thirteen days and a planned altitude block in Isola 2000.

The context explains everything. Pogačar arrives at this point in the season with three monument victories already secured — Strade Bianche, Milan-San Remo, and Tour of Flanders — along with the Tour de Romandie overall, where he won four of the five stages and finished 42 seconds clear of Florian Lipowitz. After two weeks of altitude training at Sierra Nevada from May 15, the Slovenian returns to sea level for the Tour de Suisse before one final ascent to altitude ahead of the Grand Départ. The plan is surgically precise. What happens across five days in Switzerland in June will define how he arrives in France.

## The 89th edition reinvents the format

The 2026 Tour de Suisse is not the eight-day race it ran as for decades. The organisation reduced the format to five stages and 634.5 kilometres in a reform that sporting director David Loosli described without euphemism: "A route that leaves virtually no room to recover. Transitional stages are nowhere to be found." The change is not cosmetic. In the new format, every stage has direct consequences on the general classification: there are no days to manage, no stages where the peloton arrives together automatically. The reduction in days means every kilometre carries double weight.

The other structural novelty is historic. For the first time in 89 editions, the Tour de Suisse starts outside Switzerland. Stage 1 on Wednesday, June 17 departs and finishes in Sondrio, in Italy's Valtellina. The 144-kilometre circuit crosses the UNESCO-listed terraced vineyards of the region and accumulates 2,455 metres of elevation across four climbs. Choosing Italy for the opening is not geographical caprice: the Valtellina has a deep historical relationship with high-mountain cycling, and the Sondrio circuit profile is technically demanding from the very first kilometre.

The other major novelty is one of gender: for the first time in WorldTour history, the men's and women's races are run on the same day over the same course. The women's race starts at 9:20 with the finish expected at 12:15; the men's race departs at 14:15 and concludes around 17:45. This is a real, not merely declarative, shift in visibility.

Stage 2 on Thursday, June 18 runs through the area around Lake Maggiore, crossing the Monte Ceneri before arriving in Locarno: 157.7 kilometres with 2,110 metres of elevation, punchy terrain that can provoke team tactics without demanding pure high-mountain explosiveness. Stage 3 on Friday, June 19 at Bad Ragaz features the Schwägalp as the centrepiece — a landmark Swiss climb — with 2,690 metres of total elevation gain. It is neither the longest nor the highest stage of the race, but its position — the day before the time trial — makes it a trap for riders who arrive already at the limit. Anyone depleted heading into Saturday will lose real time on Saturday.

## Aarburg: 23.8 kilometres where gaps are built or lost

The fourth stage, on Saturday, June 20, is the individual time trial in Aarburg: 23.8 kilometres described by the organisation as "fast and technical." Its length makes it a genuine test without being decisive on its own: unlike a 50-kilometre time trial, the gaps between riders will sit in the range of 30 to 90 seconds between the best specialist and a pure climber. For Pogačar, who in recent seasons has demonstrated he is also a time-triallist of the highest order, the stage is an opportunity to build a lead he does not need to defend on the final mountain day. For Van der Poel, who has established himself as one of the best rouleurs in the peloton, it is the moment to construct an advantage over the pure climbers. The logical sequence is that the true GC battle comes into focus in Aarburg before the mountains either confirm or overturn it the following day.

On Sunday, June 21, the queen stage from Villars-sur-Ollon provides the finale. The route accumulates 4,226 metres of elevation gain over 151.1 kilometres, with three ascents of the Col de la Croix — a 1,778-metre pass in French-speaking Vaud — as its defining structure. The organisation describes it without softening: "Virtually no flat metre in sight throughout, always going up or down." In that design, gaps between riders are layered on top of each other from kilometre zero. There is no straight road to neutralise the accumulated effort of three crossings of the Col de la Croix. Whoever arrives at the third ascent with something in the legs will win the stage. Whoever arrives empty will lose the race outright.

The choice of Villars-sur-Ollon as the finale is coherent with the philosophy of the reformed race. The historical Tour de Suisse typically finished with stages in the cantons of Uri or the Graubünden — classic Alpine climbs with lower accumulated elevation. The 2026 design explicitly aims to maximise selectivity on the one day where the mountains are total and definitive. It is a stage built to create hierarchy, not to confirm it.

## Pogačar: the spring that changed everything

The rider arriving in Sondrio on June 17 comes off a spring with no modern parallel. Three monuments in the same year — Strade Bianche, Milan-San Remo, Tour of Flanders — is a mark that in recent cycling history only riders in partial campaigns had approached. Added to that is the Tour de Romandie overall, where he won four stages in five days and finished 42 seconds ahead of Lipowitz. The volume of spring victories raises a specific question: does Pogačar arrive at the Tour de Suisse with legs loaded from accumulated fatigue, or with machinery tuned to its highest level?

His team's answer is that the altitude block at Sierra Nevada between May 15 and early June is precisely designed to convert the spring's workload into mountain-specific power. Pogačar has not risked racing this close to the Tour de France since 2022, and the management of this Tour de Suisse reflects that tactical caution: the stated objective is not to win the race at any cost, but to complete it in good shape and with useful information about physical condition before the Grand Départ. That said, Pogačar rarely manages without attacking. If the race shatters in Villars-sur-Ollon, no one in the peloton is betting he finishes second by choice.

## Van der Poel and Roglič: rivals with their own agendas

Mathieu van der Poel arrives at the Tour de Suisse with unfinished business. The Dutch rider of Alpecin-Premier Tech closed the spring campaign with victories, but lost the three monuments where he crossed paths with Pogačar: Flanders, Milan-San Remo, and — held back by a double puncture — finished fourth at Paris-Roubaix. The Tour de Suisse is his last chance to measure himself against the Slovenian before July in a format that, unlike the one-day classics, also rewards capacity in the time trial and on sustained mountain terrain. Van der Poel has shown over the past two years that he is not only a one-day rider: his power profile tolerates high-mountain stages at a level that five years ago would have seemed incompatible with his victories at Roubaix. The Tour de Suisse is the laboratory where that hypothesis is tested again.

Primož Roglič, who targets a fifth Vuelta a España as his season's primary objective, is among the expected starters for this edition. For the Slovenian of Red Bull-BORA-hansgrohe, the Tour de Suisse serves as a June form block without the pressure that comes with needing the victory to justify his presence in July. Tom Pidcock (Q36.5 Pro Cycling) and Jasper Philipsen (Alpecin-Premier Tech) are also listed among expected participants, the latter with genuine possibilities on selective stages should the peloton arrive together for any intermediate finish. Maxim Van Gils, returning to international racing after the injuries suffered during the spring classics, also features in the organisation's anticipated lineup.

## The Tour de Suisse as a predictor of the Tour de France

History connects both races more strongly than simple calendar proximity. The Tour de Suisse established its June position in the 1950s and has since functioned as a barometer of form for the summer Grand Tour. Egan Bernal won here in 2019 and that same July took the Tour de France. Richard Carapaz won the 2021 edition. Adam Yates took 2024. João Almeida won 2025. None of the last four Tour de Suisse winners repeated that result at the Tour de France, which puts the relationship in perspective: the Swiss race does not predict who wins in July, but who arrives in the conditions to fight for it.

Pogačar knows this. So does the team at Visma-Lease a Bike, which chose to take Vingegaard down the opposite path — Giro d'Italia, then Dauphiné — leaving the Tour de Suisse to the Slovenian and Van der Poel. The bifurcation in the preparation of the two best riders in the world over the past four years is not accidental: it is a tactical declaration about what kind of stress each rider wants to accumulate before Barcelona. Vingegaard seeks volume and sustained altitude; Pogačar seeks intensity and real confrontation. The Col de la Croix in Villars-sur-Ollon, on June 21, will provide the first concrete answer to which bet was right.
