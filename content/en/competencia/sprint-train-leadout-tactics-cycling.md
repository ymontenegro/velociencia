Three kilometres from the finish in Saint-Vulbas, stage 5 of the 2024 Tour de France, Mark Cavendish carried 34 stage victories into that afternoon — level with Eddy Merckx's all-time record. The Astana team had a lead-out train assembled. A handful of riders who, individually, do not win bunch sprints; together, they are the reason a pure sprinter can keep his legs relatively fresh until the final 200 metres. Cavendish won his 35th Tour stage that day, breaking a record that had stood for nearly half a century.

That sequence — domestiques at the front, timed hand-offs, the sprinter tucked away like a bullet in the last carriage — was not invented in 2024. It is the product of decades of tactical refinement that began with Mario Cipollini and his red escort in the mid-1990s. What started as Italian tactical intuition has since become global infrastructure: no elite sprinter today competes without a train built specifically around him.

## The roles: from controller to pilot fish

A well-executed sprint train carries between four and six riders, each fulfilling a specific function according to the kilometre in which they are working. The control riders operate between five and three kilometres out. Their job is not to accelerate towards the finish but to keep the peloton moving fast enough to discourage attacks while guiding the train into the correct position relative to crosswinds and road camber. It is not about speed yet — it is about ensuring no one else goes faster.

Next come the link riders, working from two kilometres to roughly 1,500 metres. Their role is to bridge between the controlled tempo and the attacking tempo: during this stretch, speed climbs from around 50 to nearly 60 km/h. These are the riders who absorb the most cumulative punishment, because they take a frantic peloton from the rollers and hand it over hotter still to the next stage.

The penultimate rider — known in the peloton as the "pilot fish" — is the system's critical connector. This rider takes the relay inside the final kilometre and holds it for between 200 and 300 metres before the line, pushing the speed to 65–70 km/h. That represents the ceiling of what a non-sprinter can sustain. When the pilot fish burns out and swings off, the finisher is already launched. The analogy is apt: like the small fish that guides the shark to its prey, without him, the predator has no direction.

The sprinter, or finisher, enters the moment the pilot fish peels away. With legs comparatively fresher than rivals who have been fighting for position throughout the bunch, the finisher has less accumulated fatigue. In the final 200 metres, the gap between a sprinter with a working train and one without can be measured in the watt-hours spent — or saved — during the preceding hours.

## The treno rosso: how Cipollini rewrote the sprinter's playbook

Mario Cipollini arrived at the peak of professional sprinting with a conviction few were willing to articulate: a pure sprinter needed his own army. At Saeco through the late 1990s, Cipollini managed to have between six and seven riders working exclusively for him in the final four or five kilometres of every flat stage. The "treno rosso" — the red train, named for the team's colours — had no precedent in scale. Riders like Paolo Fornaciari, Gianmatteo Fagnini and Mario Scirea held no significant individual palmares, but together they formed a machine capable of arriving at the final kilometre at speeds the rest of the peloton could not sustain.

The results were overwhelming. Cipollini accumulated 42 stage victories at the Giro d'Italia — an all-time race record — and 12 at the Tour de France. At the 1997 Giro, the treno rosso delivered him four stages in the same edition. The history of the discipline split cleanly into before and after: the proof that collective work could transform a fast rider into an untouchable one. The train did not merely help Cipollini win; it created the conditions in which no one else could.

## The treno d'argento: Petacchi and Fassa Bortolo

Alessandro Petacchi inherited the Italian school of the supported sprinter and refined it at Fassa Bortolo in the early 2000s. The team built what Italian journalists called the "treno d'argento" — the silver train — in deliberate contrast to Saeco's red livery. The tactical difference from Cipollini's model was one of degree, not principle: Fassa Bortolo also bet on numerical dominance in the closing kilometres, but with a more metronomic relay sequence.

The season that best illustrates the model is the 2004 Giro d'Italia. Petacchi won nine stages in that edition alone — a display that stands alongside Cipollini's most dominant Giro performances. The silver train controlled the peloton from a distance, absorbed breakaways and delivered Petacchi with a regularity that eventually caused rivals to stop contesting before the final kilometre. It was depressing as a system and brilliant as a tactic. Across the Grand Tours in 2004, Petacchi won 13 stages — nine at the Giro and four at the Vuelta — a volume of victories unseen since Cipollini's peak years.

## HTC-Highroad: sprint as industrial process

HTC-Columbia — later HTC-Highroad — elevated sprint train logistics to a level of systematisation no previous team had approached. Cavendish was the finisher; Mark Renshaw was the reference pilot fish; Bert Grabsch, Bernie Eisel and Tony Martin built the ramp across the preceding kilometres. Directeur sportif Rolf Aldag coordinated the formation from the team car with near-industrial precision.

According to Renshaw's own accounts, the plan started roughly 20 kilometres from the finish: the entire team positioned at the front of the peloton, working relays to keep the speed high enough to discourage attacks. By ten kilometres to go, Grabsch and Tony Martin — both powerful rouleurs — would push harder. In the final kilometre, Renshaw absorbed the chaos of the collapsing bunch and cleared the lane for Cavendish. Commentators at the time called him "the world's best lead-out man" with justification: his ability to hold peak power for 30 to 60 seconds and swing off at exactly the right moment was decisive across dozens of victories.

The cumulative product of that period is the record Cavendish finally formalised in 2024: 35 Tour de France stage wins, more than any rider in the history of the race. During the HTC years, the team built a dominance over bunch sprints that reduced every flat arrival to a question of who would finish second. It was the first time a lead-out system was so thoroughly engineered that rivals began conceding before the sprint itself had started.

## The physics of the final kilometre

At 60–70 km/h, sprinting is almost more aerodynamic than athletic. The rider in second position, sheltered behind the pilot fish, needs roughly 25–30% less power to maintain the same speed as the rider taking the wind at the front. That is the physics that makes the system viable: the sprinter conserves energy precisely because another rider is absorbing the aerodynamic resistance. When the pilot fish swings off — between 200 and 250 metres from the line, depending on the speed and the layout — the finisher launches with a fuller tank than rivals who arrived unsupported.

Positioning within the peloton in the kilometres before the sprint is as important as the sprint itself. A train that arrives at the final kilometre in 15th or 20th position must spend additional energy fighting back to the front before it is too late. The best sprint trains aim to be in the first ten positions when the last kilometre begins — a goal that requires coordinated control work well before the sprint proper. Crashes in mass finishes occur almost exclusively during this positioning phase: 100 riders compressed into six metres of road at 55 km/h, where tyre contact is nearly inevitable.

The slight drop in the final 200 metres is not an error: it reflects the sprinter already past his peak power, holding maximum velocity as best he can while pursuers may still be accelerating. That curve explains why bunch sprints are often decided by centimetres despite significant power differentials between riders.

## The modern model: two schools

Alpecin-Deceuninck constructed for the 2023 Tour de France what the team's own management described as "probably the best sprint combination in the world": Mathieu van der Poel as pilot fish and Jasper Philipsen as finisher. Van der Poel, a Monument winner and one of the most complete cyclists of his generation, accepted the role of final lead-out man without reservation. His power and race-reading ability allowed him to enter the final kilometre from compromised positions and still deliver Philipsen into a clean lane with precision that rivals could not neutralise. The result: Philipsen won four stages and the points classification jersey at the 2023 Tour.

Soudal Quick-Step operates on a different but equally effective model. Bert Van Lerberghe is the natural pilot fish for Tim Merlier — a partnership the team itself describes as "one of the most formidable in the professional peloton." Van Lerberghe is a power rouleur without Van der Poel's classics pedigree, but with the capacity to sustain very high velocities on the flat stretches of the final kilometre. Merlier accumulated 16 victories during the 2024 season, including three at the Giro d'Italia — a volume that echoes Petacchi's peak years.

A third school — freelancing — coexists with both organised models. There are sprinters who win without structured trains, particularly in finishes with late climbs or descents that dissolve formations before the sprint. Biniam Girmay is the clearest contemporary example: capable of winning in chaotic finales where trains cannot complete their sequence, a profile that illustrates why collective organisation has never entirely displaced individual talent in bunch sprinting.

The table is not exhaustive: there are dozens of regional variants, one-day race configurations and seasons in which the pilot fish changed due to injury or team strategy. The pattern, however, is consistent: every dominant generation of sprinters has had behind it a largely anonymous rider whose peak speed is lower but whose ability to hold maximum power for 30 to 60 seconds defines the race outcome. The pilot fish is, in that sense, professional cycling's most thankless role: indispensable to the result, invisible to most of the watching public.

## The train as a tactical argument

Mass sprints have a poor reputation among fans who prefer mountain stages: predictable, a ritual where the outcome is settled before the final 500 metres. That reading misses the real complexity of the system. A sprint train only functions if all six or seven riders are healthy, coordinated and in the right position. A puncture at three kilometres, a badly negotiated junction, a rival who slips into the main lane: any variable can unravel hours of planning in five seconds.

When the train breaks down, the sprint becomes chaotic in the best sense. That is when mass finishes produce their most unpredictable moments: the unsupported rider who comes from fifth position, the sprinter who loses his pilot fish at 400 metres and has to launch alone, the pure speed race where the fastest man wins without assistance. The coexistence of the organised train and the peloton's inherent disorder is what makes the mass sprint a distinct narrative genre within road cycling — one where the outcome is never quite as obvious as it looks from outside.
