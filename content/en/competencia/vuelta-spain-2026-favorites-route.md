## Monaco Opens One of the Most Demanding Routes in Years

The 2026 edition of La Vuelta a España will begin on August 22 with a 9.6-kilometer individual time trial through the streets of the Principality of Monaco. The same circuit where Formula 1 cars negotiate the Hairpin and the Swimming Pool chicane becomes a cycling track: a technical, fast and short test where the first general classification leader will be decided by tenths of a second. From the Casino to the harbor, the route includes the same elevation changes and tight corners that define the Monaco Grand Prix, a setting never before used by La Vuelta. The race will cross four countries — Monaco, France, Andorra, and Spain — before finishing on September 13 in Granada.

The complete route covers 3,275 kilometers spread across 21 stages with nearly 58,000 meters of accumulated elevation gain. The structure is the most demanding in the race's recent history: nine summit finishes, two individual time trials, and a third week that concentrates the hardest climbs in southern Spain. For the first time since 2009, the race bypasses Asturias, opting instead for a Mediterranean and Andalusian profile that pushes the mountain weight toward the final days. Unipublic has designed a linear route that avoids the area around Madrid — unusual in La Vuelta's recent history — with a clear emphasis on the Mediterranean coast, the Eastern Pyrenees, and the Sierra Nevada.

## The Two Time Trials That Frame the Race

Two time trials at the extremes of the race define the tactical approach for every team. The Monaco opening (9.6 km, Stage 1) establishes the first gaps in a test so short that 30 seconds between first and tenth would already be exceptional: here the best technical TT specialist has an advantage, but nobody loses the race on day one. The second time trial carries far greater weight: on September 10, Stage 18 runs from El Puerto de Santa María to Jerez de la Frontera over 32 kilometers through the roads of the Marco de Jerez wine region. It is the longest TT La Vuelta has included in years, and it arrives when the GC contenders have two weeks of accumulated fatigue in their legs with only three mountain stages remaining. A rider who loses a minute against the clock in Jerez needs to recover it in three days in the Sierra Nevada: every team's tactical calculation revolves around that equation.

## First Week: Pyrenees, Andorra, and the Mediterranean Coast

Stage 2 covers 215 kilometers from Monaco to Manosque in the Provençal interior, serving as a transition toward the first mountain block. Stage 3 (Gruissan → Font Romeu, 166 km) introduces the first summit finish of the edition: Font Romeu sits at nearly 1,800 meters in the French Pyrenees and is a long, demanding ascent that functions as the first genuine filter of the contenders. Stage 4 is the most explosive day of the first week: 104 kilometers entirely within Andorra with four categorized climbs, a design that favors pure climbers over any other rider profile. The density of ascents — with no recovery kilometers between passes — makes this Andorran stage one of the hardest in all three weeks.

After transition stages along the Valencian coast, the first week closes with two more summit finishes. Stage 7 takes the peloton from Vall d'Alba to Valdelinares (149 km) through the Aragonese Sistema Ibérico, a solitary high-altitude finish where crosswinds can create unexpected gaps. Stage 9 (Villajoyosa → Alto de Aitana, 187 km) ends on the Alicante mountain with irregular gradients that punish riders who cannot change pace. Three summit finishes in the first week — Font Romeu, Valdelinares, and Aitana — collectively filter out who arrives at the first rest day (August 31) with legs capable of fighting for the general classification for two more weeks.

## Second Week: The Andalusian Block That Could Decide Everything

The second week moves the race to southeastern Spain and Andalusia with three consecutive summit finishes on days 12, 13, and 14. Stage 12 (Vera → Calar Alto, 166 km) climbs to the astronomical observatory in the Sierra de los Filabres: 17 kilometers at 5.6% average gradient with mounting difficulty in the final kilometers. Stage 13 connects Almuñécar with Loja (193 km) in another summit finish in the Granada region. Stage 14 arrives at the Sierra de la Pandera from Jaén (152 km): 12 kilometers at 7% average in the full September Andalusian heat, with ramps exceeding 15% and sun exposure that can break riders who aren't perfectly hydrated. Three consecutive mountain days in Andalusia, in the second week of a Grand Tour, are the point where general classifications genuinely fracture.

The rest day on September 7 follows the flat Stage 15 from Palma del Río to Córdoba, a necessary pause before the definitive week. Teams with multiple leaders will use the transition stages to launch breakaways and accumulate points; contenders' teams will regroup tactically. The rest day is when sports directors make their calculations: if the gap between first and second is two minutes with 32 kilometers of time trial still pending, who attacks in the final week and who defends?

## Third Week: Collado del Alguacil and the Alhambra

Stage 19 (Vélez-Málaga → Peñas Blancas, 205 km) opens the decisive week with 21 kilometers at 6.6% in the Serranía de Ronda, the first shot before the definitive stage. On September 12, Stage 20 makes La Calahorra the starting point of the edition's queen stage: 187 kilometers with more than 5,000 meters of accumulated elevation gain, including the Puerto de Blancares, the Purche, a double ascent of Hazallanas, and as a final blow, the Collado del Alguacil. This climb is unprecedented in La Vuelta: 8.3 kilometers at 9.8% average gradient, with ramps reaching 20% on the hardest sections. The organizers have not exaggerated in classifying it as a special category climb.

Stage 21 closes the race with a 99-kilometer circuit through the streets of Granada, with five ascents to the Alhambra finishing each lap. The climb to the UNESCO World Heritage complex is not decorative: the final kilometers on the fifth ascent are hard enough to produce gaps between riders separated by only a few seconds. The image of a cyclist crowned in front of the Nasrid fortress is the historical backdrop Unipublic has sought for this edition. In reality, the winner will almost certainly be decided the day before, on the Collado del Alguacil.

## Roglič and the Record That Drives Everything

Primož Roglič arrives at the 2026 edition with a statistical argument that overshadows all others: his four victories (2019, 2020, 2021, and 2024) match Roberto Heras's all-time record of wins at La Vuelta. A fifth victory would make him the most successful rider in the race's history, outright. At 36, the Red Bull-BORA-Hansgrohe Slovenian remains a complete rider whose combination of consistent climbing and elite time-trialing fits this route better than any other favorite's profile — the 32-kilometer TT in Jerez is terrain where he can create real gaps over pure climbers.

Roglič's question mark remains the same as five years ago: stages with extreme accumulated elevation and severe gradients can favor pure climbers like Almeida or Ayuso more than him. But La Vuelta is not won in a single day: the rider who manages three weeks with consistency, avoiding time losses on medium-difficulty finishes, accumulates advantages that become impossible to recover. That is Roglič's profile, and the 2026 route — with many medium finishes before the final blow of the Alguacil — suits his racing style better than a route with a single extreme high-altitude finish.

## Almeida, Mas, and Ayuso: The Three Most Serious Rivals

João Almeida was runner-up at the 2025 Vuelta and arrives at the 2026 edition as the most credible candidate to interrupt Roglič's dominance of the Spanish race. The UAE Team Emirates Portuguese rider has improved year by year: his climbing now competes with the very best, his time-trialing has progressed considerably, and he knows how to manage three weeks of Grand Tour racing with tactical intelligence. His 2025 Vuelta runner-up result — pressing Jonas Vingegaard to the finish — gives him the credibility of having competed with the defending champion under maximum pressure. The Jerez TT is where Almeida can build real gaps over pure climbers.

Enric Mas (Movistar Team) is the Spaniard with the most realistic options: he finished third at the 2024 Vuelta at 3 minutes and 13 seconds off the leader, and he knows every climb on the Iberian circuit with an intimacy no foreign rival can match. Racing at home, with the support of Spanish fans, cuts both ways: it can fuel attacks or it can turn every bad day into a media catastrophe. Juan Ayuso (Lidl-Trek) is the other local option — a young climber with a considerably improved time trial and Grand Tour experience already demonstrated at the highest level. If Ayuso arrives in form for the Sierra Nevada stages, the podium is within reach.

Jonas Vingegaard, the 2025 Vuelta champion, had not confirmed his presence in the 2026 edition at press time, with his calendar focused on the July Tour de France. Remco Evenepoel has the Jerez TT as natural terrain — his ability against the clock ranks among the world's best — but nine summit finishes spread over three weeks can punish his profile more than dedicated climbers. The 2026 route has no automatic favorite: the rider who best combines three-week consistency with the capacity for the extreme finishes at the Alguacil and the Alhambra is the one who will win in Granada.

## A Route Built for Uncertainty

Unipublic has constructed the 2026 edition around one clear idea: that the general classification should not be decided until the penultimate day. The distribution of nine summit finishes across three weeks, combined with two time trials at the race's extremes and a queen stage on the unprecedented Collado del Alguacil, ensures that the standings remain fluid from Monaco to Granada. There will be no single day before the Alguacil on which the race breaks apart definitively. The rider who reaches the third week with fresh legs and a clear head will hold the race in his hands. On September 13 at the Alhambra, we will know whether it was Roglič who reached the record, Almeida who confirmed himself as a Grand Tour dominant force, or one of the Spanish climbers who won at home.
