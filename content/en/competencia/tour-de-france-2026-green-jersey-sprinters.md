## A greener prize, but a harder one

The Tour de France 2026 has done something sprinters had been asking for years: it raised the bounty. From this 113th edition, the winner of a stage classified as flat takes 70 points in the points classification, up from the 50 it was worth before. Hilly stages stay at 50. The arithmetic of the green jersey has suddenly become far friendlier to the man who wins a bunch sprint.

The catch is that this same Tour hands out that treasure sparingly. Of the 21 stages, only seven are listed as flat, and not even all of them offer a clean mass sprint. Organisers set five days at the high 70-point value and four hilly ones at 50, so the gap between nailing a sprinter's stage and missing it is now enormous.

That tension between a bigger prize and fewer chances defines the whole fight. The fastest man in the bunch can take a perfect stage and bank 70 points in one afternoon, but if he punctures on a key day or gets dropped in the mountains, recovery is slow. The 2026 green jersey punishes mistakes like rarely before.

## How the points break down

The points classification rewards consistency, not the clock. Each stage hands out points by order of finish, and the value of first place depends on the day's profile. Add the intermediate sprints, the mid-stage points hot spots that offer a second pot and almost always sit on flat days.

The 2026 reform widens the gap between stage types. Before, a third-category climb or a nervy finish handed out almost as much as a sprint on a wide avenue. Now the 20-point swing per win between flat and hilly forces teams to decide which days they go all-in.

Intermediate sprints change the maths more than they look. With two intermediate sprints on flat days, green stops being about winning two or three stages and becomes about steady scoring. A rider who wins no stage but consistently finishes in the top five and scoops the hot-spot points can wear green in Paris ahead of the fastest man.

## Seven stages, but not seven bunch sprints

The 2026 route is hard on the fast men. It opens with a [team time trial in Barcelona](/competition/tour-de-france-2026-grand-depart-barcelona), brings the Pyrenees in week one and stacks more than 50,000 metres of climbing across three weeks. On top of the seven flat days come four medium-mountain and eight high-mountain stages that split the calendar into blocks and leave the sprinters few chances in a row.

The big sprint dates are localised. Stage 7, from Hagetmau to Bordeaux, is the first prestige bunch sprint proper; Bordeaux is one of the cathedrals of Tour sprinting. Stage 8 to Bergerac and stage 11 to Nevers, right after the Massif Central day, offer fast finishes, and stage 12 to Chalon-sur-Saône chains a second sprinters' day back to back.

Others are traps in disguise. Stage 5 to Pau is listed as flat, but it lands before the Pyrenees and has biting terrain that can complicate the heavier sprinters. Stage 17 to Voiron is "flat on paper", with more than 2,000 metres of climbing and the Alps already in the legs. Whoever wants green will have to score on those odd days too.

## The grupetto fights for green too

Here is the key that separates the stage sprinter from the green jersey contender. With so many high mountains between each chance, green is not won only by sprinting: it is won by reaching Paris. Whoever fails to survive the time cut in the Pyrenees, the Massif Central, the Jura and the Alps —the same terrain we cover in [the analysis of the favourites and the route](/competition/tour-de-france-2026-barcelona-alpe-d-huez-and-the-race-to-dethrone-pogacar)— simply will not be there on the days that pay 70 points.

The grupetto, the group of non-climbers who band together to finish inside the time limit, becomes the real logistical battlefield. A sprinter who fades in the mountains not only risks elimination: he arrives empty at the next flat stage and cannot contest the sprint with any guarantee. Durability counts as much as raw power.

That is exactly the profile that sets a Jonathan Milan or a Mads Pedersen apart from a pure sprinter. The Italian pairs a sprint engine with a resilience that lets him hold terrain that drops others, which is why he starts as favourite. Pedersen, his Lidl-Trek teammate, is perhaps the most complete rider on broken days, opening the classic internal leadership dilemma.

## The 2026 contenders

The field of pretenders is the deepest in years. Milan defends green with Lidl-Trek and starts as the reference. Jasper Philipsen, who won the jersey in 2023 with a four-stage display, returns with Alpecin and the bonus of a lead-out like Mathieu van der Poel when the route allows it.

Tim Merlier is, in a straight line, probably the fastest man in the bunch, and with Soudal Quick-Step he can win any clean sprint. His problem is structural: as a pure sprinter he suffers more on broken days and needs the Tour to deliver clean finishes, something this route does not guarantee. Biniam Girmay, who made history in 2024 by winning green with Intermarché-Wanty by 33 points over Philipsen and three stages, embodies the consistent points hunter that fits this rulebook so well.

Behind them press Arnaud De Lie (Lotto), Olav Kooij — now at Decathlon-CMA CGM after leaving Visma — Jordi Meeus (Red Bull-Bora) and the veteran Bryan Coquard (Cofidis). None starts as an outright jersey favourite, but all can steal points at the hot spots and blow up the plans of the big names.

## Montmartre, the finish that breaks the script

The 2026 finale is the great unknown for the fast men. For the second year running, stage 21 does not end with the classic Champs-Élysées bunch sprint: it includes three passes up the steep Rue Lepic on Montmartre, with the last climb roughly 15 kilometres from the line. The traditional mass sprint is no longer guaranteed.

Organisers tried a conciliatory gesture. After a wave of criticism, they added an extra Champs-Élysées lap to give sprinters a window to regroup after Montmartre. Even so, Merlier was blunt: "it is and will remain difficult for us, especially because of those extreme stages before that."

The backdrop is political. Route director Thierry Gouvenou has long lamented that bunch sprints have become monotonous, "totally controlled by the sprinters, a coalition between them", and has even suggested there will be fewer of their stages in the future. That 2026 offers seven chances, more than the four of 2024, sounds almost like a truce before the route's next offensive.

## The forecast

The favourite is Milan, and not for raw speed. He is, because he ticks the three boxes this Tour demands: he wins bunch sprints, he holds the mountains inside the cut, and he has a team built to protect him for three weeks. On a route that rewards consistency over a 200-metre record, that complete package is worth more than being the fastest on any given Tuesday.

Philipsen is the most natural rival for his standing and his lead-out, and Girmay the perfect wildcard for a rulebook that rewards the rider who always scores. Merlier will win stages if the Tour gives him clean finishes, but his jersey bid depends on a route that does not suit him. The 2026 green will not necessarily go to the fastest; it will go to whoever best survives between bunch sprints, and that will be decided as much by the sprint in Bordeaux as by the grupetto in the Alps.
