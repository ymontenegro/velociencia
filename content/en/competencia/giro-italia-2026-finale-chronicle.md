The 13 kilometers of the Blockhaus took 38 minutes and 26 seconds to redefine the 2026 Giro d'Italia. Jonas Vingegaard covered the final climb of stage 7 — 246 kilometers from Formia, with the Apennines as the final backdrop — at a pace no rider had ever reached on that mountain before. The previous record belonged to Nairo Quintana in 2017: 39 minutes and 29 seconds, set during the year the Colombian won the Giro. Vingegaard erased it by over a full minute. Afonso Eulálio (Bahrain Victorious) crossed the line in 15th place, 2 minutes and 55 seconds behind the Dane, and retained the maglia rosa with an overall lead of 3 minutes and 17 seconds over the stage winner. The gap in the standings, however, does not fully describe what happened on that final stretch of Abruzzi asphalt.

## The Week That Changed the Race

Two stages in Bulgaria were enough to write the first chapter of this race. The four days that followed — Catanzaro, Potenza, Naples and the Blockhaus — rewrote it entirely. Stage 4, 138 kilometers from Catanzaro to Cosenza, was the first positive news for UAE Team Emirates-XRG after losing Vine, Soler and Yates in the opening crashes. Jhonatan Narváez won the sprint from a reduced group formed after the Cozzo Tunno climb. The Ecuadorian's victory came as Giulio Ciccone intercepted the intermediate sprint bonus at exactly the right moment to seize the maglia rosa, exactly ten years after his first Giro stage win. The Italian celebrated with the discretion of a rider who knows pink is not his destination.

Stage 5 was the most improbable episode of the edition. Two hundred and three kilometers from Praia a Mare to Potenza in relentless, bone-chilling rain; a breakaway with enough of a gap to reach the final ramps; Igor Arrieta and Afonso Eulálio constructing the decisive move together on the Montagna Grande di Viggiano. With 13.5 kilometers to go, Arrieta crashed and lost contact. The Portuguese Bahrain rider rode ahead. Arrieta, miraculously, rejoined. A wrong turn briefly disrupted the Emirati. Eulálio also crashed in the final kilometers. In the Potenza straight, both exhausted and soaked, Arrieta finished first; Eulálio was second. The accumulated gap over the peloton, however, turned the Portuguese rider into the third rider from his country to wear the maglia rosa in the entire history of the Giro, after Acácio da Silva and João Almeida.

Stage 6 produced another collective crash. On the wet cobblestones of the Naples finish in the Mergellina district, the Unibet Rose Rockets train — leading the sprint lineup — went down on the final bend with less than 500 meters to go. Jonathan Milan, Dylan Groenewegen and Tobias Lund Andresen all hit the deck. Davide Ballerini of XDS Astana threaded through the chaos and won with room to spare. It was the third chaotic sprint in six stages: this race refuses to let any flat day end cleanly.

## The Anatomy of a Record

The Blockhaus measures 13 kilometers at 8.3% average, with sections exceeding 12%. It is the type of climb where threshold power alone is not enough; a rider must sustain oxygen debt through the upper ramps where the gradient consistently refuses to relent. Visma-Lease a Bike controlled the group of GC contenders from the start of the ascent with a pace designed to reduce the race to its most fundamental question: who can stay and who cannot. At 5.5 kilometers from the summit, Vingegaard accelerated. Giulio Pellizzari was the only rider able to respond. The Italian from Red Bull-BORA-Hansgrohe held on for roughly a kilometer before visibly cracking; Vingegaard continued alone. Felix Gall (Decathlon CMA CGM) reacted too late but found enough reserves to finish second, 13 seconds behind the Dane. Jai Hindley arrived third, 1 minute and 2 seconds back.

Behind the stage podium, the damage report was extensive. Egan Bernal (Netcompany-Ineos), who had navigated the first six stages without conceding ground to GC rivals, could not hold the pace on the upper Blockhaus and finished 2 minutes and 57 seconds behind Vingegaard. In the general classification, the Colombian sits 15th at 6 minutes and 18 seconds. Lennert Van Eetvelt (Lotto-Intermarché), who had matched Vingegaard's attack on the Bulgarian second stage with apparent ease, lost 3 minutes and 47 seconds on the same day the Dane set the mountain record. Enric Mas suffered what Spanish media described as a personal catastrophe on the upper slopes, with losses that remove him entirely from any podium conversation.

## Eulálio: The Pink Jersey With an Asterisk

Afonso Eulálio is 26 years old and arrived at the 2026 Giro as a climbing domestique for Antonio Tiberi, not as a declared leader. The stage 5 breakaway changed that. On the wet Calabrian Apennines, the Portuguese rider made the solo race decisions that a domestique should never have to handle alone: when to attack, when to recover, when to let Arrieta go in the final sprint. That he ended up in the lead jersey with 2 minutes and 51 seconds over a peloton that failed to coordinate the chase is a result that nobody at Bahrain Victorious had penciled in.

The problem with Eulálio's lead is its origin. The 3 minutes and 17 seconds separating him from Vingegaard were not earned on the climbs; they were earned in a 203-kilometer breakaway where the main field misjudged the threat. That type of gap has a different shelf life than time built by attacking on the final meters of a pass. On the Blockhaus, Eulálio lost 2 minutes and 55 seconds to Vingegaard — who only converted 22 seconds in net GC gain thanks to the pre-existing time buffer — and crossed the line in 15th place. The trend line is already drawn.

The Bahrain Victorious team now faces a tactical paradox. The squad arrived at the Giro with Tiberi as the podium objective and Eulálio as climbing support. The crash that took out Santiago Buitrago in the second Bulgarian stage had already reduced the mountain train. Having the support rider lead the race complicates the team's racing logic in fundamental ways. Tiberi sits far outside the podium positions in the GC; Eulálio, in the leadership spot, needs more assistance than the team can realistically provide once the Dolomites arrive.

## The Time Trial That Could Break the Race Open

Stage 10, on Tuesday May 19, is a 42-kilometer individual time trial from Viareggio to Massa along the Tuscan coastline. The profile is completely flat: no meaningful gradients between the start line and the finish, meaning the final time depends on sustained aerodynamic power over approximately 47 to 52 minutes at maximum intensity. This is precisely the type of effort in which Jonas Vingegaard has no comparable rivals among the current race leaders. The Dane won the TT at the 2025 Tour de France in Compiègne and was fastest in the Critérium du Dauphiné TT in April. Felix Gall, currently third overall, is a pure climber with a well-documented deficit on flat terrain.

The scale of what may happen on Tuesday is difficult to quantify precisely at this moment, but the direction is unambiguous. Vingegaard could arrive at the Giro's first rest day — scheduled for Monday the 18th — as the virtual race leader, with a cushion that Eulálio will struggle to recover in the climbing stages remaining before the Dolomites. Stage 9 on Sunday — Cervia to Corno alle Scale, 184 kilometers with a summit finish at 1,471 meters in the Bolognese Apennines — will have its own dynamic, but the main GC board updates in Tuscany at full time-trial speed.

## The Dolomites as Final Verdict

The third-week route concentrates everything the Giro has to offer for a change of leadership, should the Tuesday time trial not settle matters. Stage 16, finishing at Piani di Pezzè after crossing the Passo Giau — Cima Coppi 2026 at 2,233 meters — is the tappone where the gap between winner and the rest can reach its maximum. The Giau runs 9.9 kilometers at 9.3% average with sections at 14%: it is not a climb to survive under pressure; a rider must attack or be shelled. Stage 20, from Gemona to Piancavallo over 199 kilometers with the final ramps at 12%, represents the last opportunity for a leadership change before the ceremonial arrival in Rome on May 31.

Under the most probable scenario: if Vingegaard maintains his form and takes the maglia rosa in the Tuscan TT, what remains of the Giro becomes a test of attrition for those who chase him. Felix Gall has the profile of the climbers who can absorb sustained attacks on long ascents, but his TT weakness condemns him to arrive late in any tactical battle fought on flat ground. Ben O'Connor, sixth overall, has three-week race experience and could benefit from the war of attrition between those more directly in the firing line. Giulio Pellizzari, who matched Vingegaard's initial attack on the Blockhaus better than anyone else, could be the third-week surprise if he arrives in the Dolomites with legs intact.

## The Hardest Jersey to Defend

There is something poetically cruel about Afonso Eulálio's situation this afternoon on the Blockhaus. He crossed the line in 15th place, nearly three minutes behind the man who dominated the race's first real climb. But the scoreboard still says he leads. He will wear the maglia rosa tomorrow, on Sunday at Corno alle Scale, and — barring a surprise — into Tuesday's time trial in Tuscany. Three minutes and seventeen seconds is a substantial margin for a clock; it is a narrow margin to look at too comfortably.

Jonas Vingegaard, who had ridden through the Apennines without any rival capable of following his attack, was characteristically brief at the finish: "It was a good day for us. There's a lot of Giro left." The sentence describes the state of the race with more precision than any number could.
