## 16% more endurance from half a liter of beetroot juice

In 2009, a research group at the University of Exeter led by Stephen Bailey published a study in the *Journal of Applied Physiology* that redefined what we knew about beetroot and athletic performance. Eight healthy men drank 500 ml of beetroot juice daily for six consecutive days. The result was a 5.1% reduction in oxygen cost during submaximal exercise and a 16% increase in time to exhaustion during high-intensity exercise. No legal supplement had previously demonstrated such a magnitude of effect in placebo-controlled trials.

Bailey et al.'s study did not appear out of nowhere. Two years earlier, Larsen and colleagues from the Karolinska Institute in Stockholm had published in *Acta Physiologica* the first study demonstrating that sodium nitrate supplementation reduced oxygen consumption during submaximal exercise in humans. What Bailey and his team did was translate that finding to a real and accessible dietary source: beetroot, one of the vegetables with the highest natural concentration of inorganic nitrates.

Since then, over a hundred studies have investigated the effects of dietary nitrate on athletic performance. McMahon, Leveritt, and Pavey published in 2017 in *Sports Medicine* a meta-analysis that included 47 studies with 76 trials. The main conclusion was that nitrate supplementation improves performance in time-to-exhaustion tests, though its effect on time trials is more modest and less consistent. That distinction between test types is key to understanding what beetroot can and cannot do for a cyclist.

## The nitrate pathway: from mouth to muscle

The mechanism by which beetroot juice improves performance is not mysterious, but it is elegant. Lundberg, Weitzberg, and Gladwin described it in detail in a review published in 2008 in *Nature Reviews Drug Discovery*. The inorganic nitrate (NO₃⁻) ingested with beetroot is absorbed in the intestine and enters the bloodstream. From there, the salivary glands actively concentrate it: saliva contains nitrate levels up to 20 times higher than plasma.

In the oral cavity, anaerobic bacteria on the dorsum of the tongue reduce nitrate to nitrite (NO₂⁻) through nitrate reductase enzymes. This step is entirely dependent on the oral microbiome. Studies that administered antibacterial mouthwash to participants before drinking beetroot juice completely eliminated the rise in plasma nitrite and nullified the effects on blood pressure and performance. Using chlorhexidine mouthwash before training invalidates the supplementation.

The nitrite swallowed with saliva reaches the stomach, where the acidic pH partially converts it to nitric oxide (NO). But most of the remaining nitrite circulates through the blood and is reduced to NO in the tissues that need it most: those with low oxygen, low pH, and high metabolic demand. In other words, exactly the conditions that prevail in skeletal muscle during intense exercise. Andrew Jones, one of the leading researchers in this field, explained in a 2014 review in *Sports Medicine* that this pathway complements enzymatic NO production by nitric oxide synthase, and is activated precisely when the enzymatic pathway is least efficient.

## Less oxygen for the same effort: the efficiency that matters

Nitric oxide derived from dietary nitrate acts on performance through several simultaneous mechanisms. Jones (2014) grouped them into three main categories: reduction in oxygen cost of exercise, improvement in muscle contractile function, and increased blood flow to active muscle fibers. Each of these effects has been independently documented in controlled studies.

The reduction in oxygen cost is the most replicated effect. Bailey et al. (2009) found a 5.1% decrease in VO₂ during moderate-intensity exercise. Larsen and colleagues subsequently demonstrated that this reduction is associated with improved mitochondrial efficiency: NO reduces the expression of certain uncoupling proteins and decreases proton leak in mitochondria, meaning each molecule of oxygen consumed produces more ATP. For a cyclist, this translates to maintaining the same power with lower cardiovascular demand, or producing more power at the same perceived effort level.

The improvement in muscle contractile function is particularly relevant for explosive efforts. NO increases calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum and improves calcium sensitivity of contractile proteins. In practical terms, the muscle contracts with more force and greater speed in response to the same neural stimulus. Several studies have shown that nitrate supplementation improves force production during rapid contractions, a type of contraction that predominates in sprints and accelerations within the peloton.

## 6 to 8 mmol: the dose supported by evidence

Not every amount of beetroot produces the same effect. Wylie and colleagues published in 2013 in the *Journal of Applied Physiology* a dose-response study that established effective ranges with precision. Ten healthy men ingested 70, 140, or 280 ml of concentrated beetroot juice, equivalent to 4.2, 8.4, and 16.8 mmol of nitrate respectively, or a placebo. Tests were performed 2.5 hours after ingestion.

The results showed a clear pattern. The 4.2 mmol dose did not alter the physiological response to exercise or performance. The 8.4 mmol dose reduced oxygen consumption during moderate exercise by 1.7% and extended time to exhaustion by 14%. The 16.8 mmol dose reduced VO₂ by 3.0% but did not improve time to exhaustion beyond 12%. In other words, doubling the dose did not double the benefit. The dose-response relationship plateaus around 8 mmol.

For a 70 kg cyclist, the effective dose of 6-8 mmol of nitrate can be obtained from approximately 500 ml of fresh beetroot juice or 140 ml of commercial concentrate. Concentrated "shot" products (such as those manufactured by Beet It or similar brands) are standardized to deliver between 6 and 7 mmol of nitrate per 70 ml serving, so two shots reach the optimal range. Homemade juice is possible but introduces variability: the nitrate content of beetroot varies with soil type, variety, season, and cooking method.

## The time trial: real but modest improvements

If the effect of nitrate on time to exhaustion is striking, its impact on simulated time trials, a format more relevant to actual competition, deserves separate analysis. Lansley and colleagues published in 2011 in *Medicine and Science in Sports and Exercise* a study with nine competitive cyclists who completed 4 km and 16.1 km time trials after ingesting concentrated beetroot juice with 6.2 mmol of nitrate. The improvement was 2.8% in the 4 km time trial and 2.7% in the 16.1 km event.

Cermak, Gibala, and van Loon replicated these findings in 2012, publishing in the *International Journal of Sport Nutrition and Exercise Metabolism* a study with 20 trained cyclists who completed 10 km time trials after six days of beetroot juice supplementation. Mean power increased by 1.7% and time trial time decreased by 26 seconds compared to placebo, a statistically significant difference. In an effort lasting approximately 31 minutes, 26 seconds represent a marginal improvement but one with real competitive consequences.

The McMahon et al. (2017) meta-analysis offers a more cautious perspective when analyzing 28 time trial studies. The overall effect was positive but small and did not always reach statistical significance. Heterogeneity between studies was high, reflecting differences in dose, supplementation duration, participants' training level, and protocol type. The authors concluded that nitrate supplementation is more likely to produce positive results in time-to-exhaustion tests than in fixed-duration time trials.

## The elite athlete problem

One of the most consistent findings in the nitrate literature is that the ergogenic effect diminishes as the athlete's training level increases. Hoon and colleagues published in 2014 in *Applied Physiology, Nutrition, and Metabolism* a study with national-level cyclists who completed 4-minute time trials after ingesting nitrate at various doses. The effect was "unclear" in the first time trial and "possibly harmful" in the second. Under the study conditions, nitrate supplementation did not improve performance in these competitive cyclists.

The most accepted explanation involves the adapted physiology of elite athletes. Jones (2014) noted that highly trained cyclists already have elevated baseline levels of plasma nitrite and more efficient enzymatic nitric oxide production. Their muscle capillary density is greater, their mitochondrial function is optimized, and their proportion of type I fibers, which are the most responsive to nitrate, already functions near maximum potential. In these individuals, the margin for improvement offered by exogenous nitrate is inherently smaller.

This does not mean elite cyclists should dismiss beetroot entirely. Some recent studies suggest that chronic supplementation protocols with high doses, exceeding 12 mmol daily for more than a week, could overcome this resistance threshold. A 2019 study published in the *International Journal of Sports Physiology and Performance* found that 12.4 mmol of nitrate daily for 7 days improved time trial performance by 1.6% in well-trained cyclists. However, the evidence in this subgroup remains limited and inconsistent.

## 2 to 3 hours before: the timing that maximizes the effect

The pharmacokinetics of dietary nitrate determine when to take it. Wylie et al. (2013) measured plasma nitrite concentration after different doses of beetroot juice and found that the peak occurs between 2 and 3 hours after ingestion. This is the moment when nitric oxide availability is at its maximum and, therefore, the optimal time to begin exercise.

For a race or key training session starting at 9 AM, the cyclist should consume their beetroot juice dose between 6 and 7 AM. If the competition is in the afternoon, the window is more comfortable but the principle remains the same. Acute supplementation, a single dose before exercise, has demonstrated efficacy in multiple studies, although some chronic supplementation protocols of 3 to 6 days produce slightly greater effects according to the McMahon et al. (2017) meta-analysis.

A practical factor to consider is the effect of nitrate on blood pressure. Beetroot reduces systolic blood pressure by 3 to 10 mmHg in the hours following ingestion. For most cyclists, this effect is irrelevant or even beneficial. However, athletes with low baseline blood pressure may experience dizziness or premature fatigue if they combine beetroot with other hypotensive circumstances such as extreme heat or dehydration.

## Practical guide: from the lab to the bottle

Translating evidence into practice requires attention to detail. The following table summarizes the supplementation parameters supported by the research.

| Parameter | Recommendation | Evidence |
|-----------|---------------|----------|
| Optimal dose | 6-8 mmol NO₃⁻ | Wylie et al. 2013 |
| Fresh juice equivalent | ~500 ml | Bailey et al. 2009 |
| Concentrate equivalent | ~140 ml (2 × 70 ml shots) | Wylie et al. 2013 |
| Acute timing | 2-3 h before exercise | Wylie et al. 2013 |
| Chronic protocol | 3-6 consecutive days | McMahon et al. 2017 |
| Best effect for | Efforts lasting 5-30 min | McMahon et al. 2017 |
| Least effect for | Elite athletes (VO₂max >65 ml/kg/min) | Jones 2014 |
| Caution | Avoid antibacterial mouthwash | Jones 2014 review |

Beetroot has one cosmetic drawback worth mentioning: it stains urine and stool red. The phenomenon, called beeturia, is completely harmless and is caused by betalains, the pigments responsible for beetroot's purple color. It affects 10-14% of the population and has no implications for health or supplement efficacy.

Nitrates are also present in other vegetables such as spinach, arugula, celery, and lettuce. However, standardizing the dose from whole vegetables is difficult because nitrate content varies widely. Concentrated beetroot juice remains the most studied form and the most practical way to reach the effective dose without ingesting large volumes of food before competition.

## What beetroot can and cannot do

Beetroot juice is not a shortcut to performance. It is a supplement with solid level-A evidence according to the Australian Institute of Sport classification, the same category occupied by caffeine, creatine, and carbohydrates. Its effect is real but modest: a recreational cyclist can expect a 1-3% improvement in time trials and a larger increase in fatigue resistance. An elite cyclist will likely obtain smaller or negligible benefits with standard protocols.

The combination of nitrate with caffeine is a strategy that some studies have explored without finding negative interactions. Both supplements act through different mechanisms: caffeine blocks adenosine receptors in the central nervous system, while nitrate operates on muscle efficiency and peripheral blood flow. In theory, their effects could be additive, although direct evidence of synergy remains limited.

What the evidence makes clear is that beetroot offers a legal, accessible, and inexpensive advantage for cyclists seeking to optimize their performance through science-based interventions. Half a liter of juice, two hours before the start, with a mouth free of antibacterial rinses. That is the prescription endorsed by Bailey, Lansley, Cermak, Wylie, and dozens of researchers across more than a decade of controlled studies. The purple stain on your teeth at the bottom of the bottle is probably the lowest price a cyclist can pay for a proven ergogenic benefit.
