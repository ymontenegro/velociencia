Magnesium serves as a cofactor in more than 300 enzymatic reactions in the human body, including ATP synthesis and the regulation of muscle contraction. That central role makes it one of the most supplemented minerals in sport. What's less expected is how poorly much of the received wisdom about its effects — cramps, sleep, performance — holds up to clinical scrutiny. Between 45 and 50 % of adults in Western countries fall short of the recommended daily intake — a figure documented for the US by Rosanoff et al. in *Nutrition Reviews* (2012; PMID 22364157) and consistent with national nutrition surveys across Europe — and athletes face higher demands; but supplementing without a prior deficit delivers far less than the labels suggest.

## A Cofactor Behind Almost Every Pedal Stroke

The most performance-relevant role of magnesium is in energy production: the Mg²⁺ ion forms a stable complex with ATP and is essential for ATPase to catalyze the conversion of ATP to ADP, releasing the energy that drives muscle contraction. Volpe SL described in *Current Sports Medicine Reports* (2015; PMID 26166051) that without adequate magnesium, this metabolic cascade loses efficiency in proportion to the degree of deficit. At the neuromuscular level, magnesium acts as a physiological antagonist to calcium: it regulates Ca²⁺ entry into muscle cells and modulates the threshold for nerve excitability. A drop in intracellular reserves disrupts that balance and can increase muscular irritability.

The adult body contains between 22 and 26 grams of magnesium distributed primarily in bone (~60 %), muscle (~27 %), and soft tissue (~13 %); only 1 % circulates in plasma. That distribution makes serum magnesium a poor indicator of actual magnesium status — it's possible to have a plasma value within the reference range while still having a functional deficit at the muscle level, a condition sometimes called subclinical magnesium deficiency. For a cyclist, the relevant data point is not an isolated serum reading but the pattern of dietary intake and accumulated losses.

## How Much Does a Cyclist Need — and Lose?

The Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA) for magnesium is 400–420 mg/day for adult men and 310–320 mg/day for adult women. Nielsen and Lukaski estimated in their 2006 review in *Magnesium Research* (PMID 17172008) that intense exercise raises requirements by 10 to 20 % above sedentary values through two mutually reinforcing loss pathways: sweat and urine. Sweat losses average around 3–4 mg per liter, which translates to 24–32 mg of additional loss during a four-hour ride at a two-liter-per-hour sweat rate. At the same time, the kidneys increase urinary magnesium excretion in the hours following a hard effort, likely associated with changes in blood pH and the activation of cortisol and aldosterone.

A meta-analysis published in *Food Science and Human Wellness* (2023; DOI 10.1016/j.fshw.2023.02.015) integrating 14 studies with 855 athletes and 521 sedentary controls found that athletes had significantly lower serum magnesium concentrations (mean difference: −0.04 mmol/L) despite reporting a dietary intake that was 52 mg/day higher on average. That paradox — eating more magnesium but having less in the blood — reflects the higher turnover and excretion imposed by systematic training. It doesn't necessarily mean all endurance athletes are deficient, but it does mean the buffer is thinner.

## The Cramp Myth: What the Cochrane Actually Found

The belief that magnesium prevents muscle cramps is deeply entrenched in cycling culture. The scientific evidence does not support it for the general adult population. The Cochrane review by Garrison SR et al. (2020; DOI 10.1002/14651858.CD009402.pub3), which updated and extended prior reviews with eleven trials (nine parallel-group and two crossover) enrolling 735 participants, concluded that oral magnesium supplementation produced no clinically meaningful reduction in the frequency or intensity of skeletal muscle cramps in adults without underlying pathology. The authors rated the certainty of evidence as low or very low across all assessed outcomes.

Partial exceptions include cramps associated with pregnancy, where some trials show a signal — though also with weak evidence — and cramps in patients with chronic liver disease or diuretic use, where true hypomagnesemia is genuinely common. For a cyclist without underlying disease, supplementing magnesium as an "anti-cramp" agent lacks solid clinical backing. Exercise-induced cramps during high-intensity efforts are most likely attributable to neuromuscular fatigue and altered spinal cord motor modulation rather than acute electrolyte imbalance. That said, if a genuine magnesium deficit exists — not uncommon in cyclists following restrictive dietary patterns — correcting it can restore neuromuscular excitability to normal ranges.

## Magnesium and Sleep: A Plausible Mechanism, Thin Evidence

Interest in magnesium as a sleep aid rests on a real mechanistic foundation: the ion enhances the inhibitory effect of GABA-A receptors and blocks NMDA receptors, reducing excitatory activity in the central nervous system. Animal studies and epidemiological data linking low magnesium intake to poorer sleep quality are consistent. The problem is clinical evidence in healthy humans. A meta-analysis by Mah J and Pitre T published in *BMC Complementary Medicine and Therapies* (2021; PMID 33865376) found only three eligible randomized trials, all conducted in adults aged 55 or older, with a combined total of 151 participants.

The pooled analysis showed a 17-minute reduction in sleep onset latency (statistically significant) and a 16-minute gain in total sleep time (not statistically significant) following supplementation, and the authors rated the certainty of evidence as low or very low, noting high risk of bias across the included studies. For active adult cyclists without clinical insomnia, there is no direct evidence that magnesium supplementation improves sleep quality or overnight recovery. Any benefit, if it exists in this population, would most plausibly concentrate in athletes with documented deficiency where hypomagnesemia alters autonomic nervous system function during sleep.

## Performance: The Benefit Switches On When Deficits Are Corrected

Zhang et al. published in *Nutrients* (2017; PMID 28846654) a systematic review of magnesium supplementation trials and physical performance. The central finding is that magnesium can improve functional markers such as quadriceps torque and muscle glucose availability, with an effect on lactate accumulation that may delay fatigue during moderate-intensity efforts. The crucial caveat is that these benefits are more pronounced — and clinically meaningful — in individuals with suboptimal baseline intake. In athletes who are already magnesium-replete, evidence that adding more magnesium improves performance is sparse, and high-quality trials are essentially absent.

This doesn't make magnesium irrelevant for cyclists; it means the primary objective is to ensure adequate dietary intake, and when that's not achievable, to supplement specifically to correct the deficit. Positioning magnesium as a direct ergogenic — in the same category as caffeine or sodium bicarbonate — is not justified by current evidence.

## Forms and Bioavailability: Not All Supplements Are Equal

Pardo MR et al. systematically reviewed in *Nutrition* (2021; DOI 10.1016/j.nut.2021.111294) the bioavailability of 14 different magnesium supplement forms. Organic forms (citrate, glycinate, malate) consistently show better absorption than inorganic ones (oxide, carbonate). Magnesium oxide — despite being the cheapest form and the one most commonly included in low-grade multivitamin blends — has an estimated bioavailability of just ~4 %, functioning more as an osmotic laxative than an absorbable magnesium source. Citrate and glycinate achieve absorption rates in the 30–40 % range and are the best-supported choices.

## The Best Food Sources

Before opening any bottle, it's worth auditing the plate. Pumpkin seeds lead the list at 592 mg per 100 g (USDA FoodData Central, FDC #170556); two heaped tablespoons over a bowl of oats can contribute 60–80 mg on their own. Chia seeds (335 mg/100 g), almonds (270 mg/100 g), and dark chocolate at 70 % cocoa solids (228 mg/100 g) are concentrated sources that integrate easily into a cyclist's diet. Cooked spinach and legumes round out the picture with lower density per serving but important contributions when eaten regularly.

## When and How Much to Supplement

If a dietary review reveals a consistently low magnesium intake — common in cyclists who prioritize refined carbohydrates over vegetables and seeds, or in those following calorie-restricted patterns — supplementation is warranted. The practical dose of elemental magnesium sits between 200 and 400 mg per day, taken at night with water to reduce gastrointestinal interference and to leverage the potential calming effect on the central nervous system. Citrate and glycinate are the recommended forms; oxide is not worth the money regardless of how cheap the label looks.

The primary adverse effect at higher doses — above 400 mg of elemental magnesium per day — is osmotic diarrhea, most marked with the oxide and citrate forms. People with renal insufficiency should consult their physician before supplementing, since the kidneys are the main excretion route and excess magnesium can accumulate to produce hypermagnesemia. In healthy cyclists without kidney disease, the ranges described are generally safe and well tolerated.

## The Honest Bottom Line

Magnesium holds an undeniable physiological role in energy production and neuromuscular function. The issue is that the three uses most aggressively marketed in the sports supplement industry — cramp prevention, sleep improvement, and performance enhancement — have weak or conditional clinical backing. The Cochrane is explicit in dismissing the anti-cramp effect for the general adult population; sleep studies are small and limited to older adults; and performance benefits only materialize when starting from a real deficit.

The sensible protocol starts not at the pharmacy but with the plate: a cyclist whose diet regularly includes legumes, leafy greens, seeds, and whole grains has a reasonable probability of meeting requirements without supplements. When the diet consistently falls short — through caloric restriction, elimination of food groups, or very high training volume in hot environments — magnesium glycinate or citrate at 200–350 mg before bed is a safe and physiologically grounded addition. But honesty is required: this is not caffeine, it won't work in the first week, and it will not compensate for a fundamentally poor diet.
