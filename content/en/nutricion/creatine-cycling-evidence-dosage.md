## The world's most studied supplement arrives in the peloton with caveats

Creatine monohydrate is the sports supplement with the largest body of accumulated scientific evidence. The International Society of Sports Nutrition (ISSN) published its position stand in 2017 in the *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*, where Kreider and colleagues reviewed over 500 studies and concluded that creatine is the most effective nutritional supplement available for increasing high-intensity exercise capacity and lean body mass during training (PMID: 28615996). The ISSN also stated that long-term use, even at doses up to 30 g/day for 5 years, is safe and well tolerated in healthy individuals.

Most of that evidence, however, comes from strength and power sports involving short, explosive efforts. Cycling, a discipline where aerobic endurance dominates and every gram of body weight matters on climbs, raises different questions. The short answer: creatine won't improve your FTP or steady-state performance, but it can make a real difference in the moments that decide races, from the final sprint to repeated attacks in a criterium circuit.

## Phosphocreatine: the energy currency of the first 10 seconds

Understanding where creatine fits in cycling requires understanding the ATP-PCr system. Adenosine triphosphate (ATP) is the molecule muscles use directly for contraction. The problem is that muscular ATP stores are depleted in 1 to 2 seconds of maximal effort. The phosphocreatine (PCr) system acts as an immediate buffer: the enzyme creatine kinase transfers a phosphate group from phosphocreatine to ADP to regenerate ATP without requiring oxygen or glucose.

This system dominates energy production during the first 6 to 10 seconds of maximal effort. A 200-meter sprint on flat road, an attack to close a gap, a violent change of pace in the peloton: all depend on the ATP-PCr system. Creatine supplementation increases muscular phosphocreatine stores by 20% to 40%, allowing peak power to be sustained for a few extra seconds and PCr reserves to be replenished faster between efforts.

Hickner and colleagues demonstrated in 2010 that 28 days of supplementation with 3 g/day of creatine monohydrate in trained cyclists increased total muscle creatine by 24.5% and phosphocreatine by 38.4% (PMID: 20609257). That increase in reserves is not trivial: it means more ATP available for each acceleration and faster PCr resynthesis during lower-intensity periods.

## For pure endurance, the effect is zero

Fernández-Landa and colleagues published a meta-analysis in 2023 in *Sports Medicine* that included 13 studies in trained athletes and evaluated the effect of creatine monohydrate on endurance performance (PMID: 36877404). The conclusion was straightforward: creatine did not improve endurance performance in this population. The standardized mean difference was -0.07, with a p-value of 0.47. Results remained consistent even after excluding studies with risk of bias.

This data point is fundamental for cyclists. If your goal is to improve your one-hour time trial, increase threshold power, or sustain prolonged effort on a climb, creatine will not help. The phosphocreatine system simply is not the limiting factor in those efforts. Energy production during sustained efforts depends on aerobic metabolism, mitochondrial capacity, oxygen transport, and carbohydrate availability. Creatine plays no role in any of those processes.

Barranco-Gil and colleagues confirmed this limitation in 2024 with a randomized controlled trial in professional cyclists (PMID: 38606895). They administered 20 g daily of creatine for 7 days to a group of professional cyclists during a training camp. They found no significant effects on time trial performance, critical power, body composition, or recovery markers compared to the placebo group. High-dose, short-term supplementation in professionals already operating near their physiological ceiling produced no measurable benefit.

## Where creatine does make a difference: repeated sprints and pace changes

The picture shifts when the effort is fragmented. Glaister and Rhodes published a meta-analysis in 2022 in the *International Journal of Sport Nutrition and Exercise Metabolism* that included 14 double-blind randomized studies evaluating the short-term effect of creatine on repeated sprint ability (PMID: 36041731). Participants performed between 4 and 20 sprints of up to 10 seconds with recoveries of up to 90 seconds. Supplementation consisted of approximately 20 g/day for 3 to 7 days.

The result: creatine increased mean power during repeated sprints with an effect size of δ = 0.61, a statistically significant difference (p = 0.002). The effect on peak power was smaller (δ = 0.41) and did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.10). Body mass increased by an average of 0.79 kg.

Crisafulli and colleagues added cycling-specific evidence with a 2018 study in 23 recreational cyclists (PMID: 29743825). After 6 weeks of creatine-electrolyte supplementation, the experimental group showed a 4% increase in overall peak power and a 5% increase in mean power during repeated sprints, while the placebo group showed no significant changes.

Forbes, Candow, Antonio, and colleagues published a narrative review in 2023 in the *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition* that articulated the central thesis for endurance sports (PMID: 37096381). Creatine is more effective at improving performances that require multiple sudden intensity changes than for steady-paced efforts. In cycling, this translates to criteriums, stages with chaotic finishes, races with short repeated climbs, and any scenario where attacks and counterattacks determine the outcome.

## Loading protocols vs. maintenance dosing

The literature describes two main protocols for saturating muscular creatine stores. The traditional loading protocol consists of 20 g/day (divided into 4 doses of 5 g) for 5 to 7 days, followed by a maintenance dose of 3 to 5 g/day. The ISSN position stand (Kreider et al., 2017) supports this approach as the fastest method to elevate muscular stores.

The alternative is a constant low-dose protocol: 3 to 5 g/day from the start, with no loading phase. Evidence indicates both protocols achieve the same muscular saturation, but the no-loading protocol requires 3 to 4 weeks to get there. The advantage is that it avoids the gastrointestinal effects that can accompany high doses: bloating, cramping, and in some cases, diarrhea.

| Protocol | Daily dose | Time to saturation | GI effects | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Loading + maintenance | 20 g/day (7 days) → 3-5 g/day | 5-7 days | Possible | Upcoming competition |
| Constant dose | 3-5 g/day | 3-4 weeks | Minimal | Planned chronic use |

For cyclists, the constant-dose protocol makes more sense in most scenarios. The season is long, competitions are spaced out, and there is no urgency to saturate stores in one week if you can do it gradually without digestive discomfort. The loading protocol is justified when a cyclist decides to start creatine shortly before a specific competition involving intermittent efforts, such as a criterium or a stage race with explosive finishes.

## The weight problem: 0.8 kg that matter on the mountain

The meta-analysis by Glaister and Rhodes (2022) documented an average body mass increase of 0.79 kg following creatine supplementation. This increase is primarily due to intracellular water retention, not fat or muscle tissue gain. Antonio and colleagues confirmed this in their 2021 review in the *JISSN* (PMID: 33557850): creatine is not a steroid, it does not cause fat accumulation, and the initial weight gain reflects an increase in intracellular water associated with greater creatine storage in muscle.

For a road cyclist competing on flat terrain or in criteriums, an extra 0.8 kg may be irrelevant if they gain 5% more power in decisive sprints. But for a 58 kg climber fighting for every second on 20-minute climbs, those extra grams represent a direct cost to the watts-per-kilogram ratio with no benefit, given that creatine does not improve sustained effort performance.

The decision is specific to the cyclist's profile and competition type. A sprinter or track cyclist has a clearly favorable cost-benefit balance. A pure climber faces the opposite. An all-rounder or classics rider, who needs power in repeated short efforts but also has to climb, enters a gray zone where periodizing the supplement makes sense: use it during blocks of competitions with explosive finishes and stop it before mountain races.

## Recovery and muscle damage: mixed evidence

Doma and colleagues published a meta-analysis in 2022 in *Sports Medicine* that included 23 studies with 240 participants in the creatine group and 229 in the placebo group (PMID: 35218552). The results were paradoxical. Following a single acute session of intense exercise, creatine reduced indirect markers of muscle damage (creatine kinase, lactate dehydrogenase, and myoglobin) between 48 and 90 hours post-exercise, with an effect size of -1.09 (p = 0.03).

However, the pattern reversed when evaluating the effect over prolonged training periods. With chronic supplementation, muscle damage markers were higher in the creatine group at 24 hours post-exercise, with an effect size of 0.95 (p = 0.04). The authors suggested that creatine allows training at higher intensity over extended periods, which generates more muscle damage as a consequence of greater training load, not as a direct effect of the supplement.

For cyclists in stage races or intensive training blocks, this evidence suggests creatine could facilitate recovery between acute intense efforts. But a cumulative protective effect should not be expected over weeks of elevated training load. The recovery benefit appears limited to the acute context: a sprint, a stage, a one-off effort.

## Cognitive function: making better decisions in the race finale

A lesser-known aspect of creatine is its effect on the brain. Xu and colleagues published a meta-analysis in 2024 in *Frontiers in Nutrition* that included 16 randomized controlled trials with 492 participants evaluating the effects of creatine on cognitive function (PMID: 39070254). Supplementation significantly improved memory, attention time, and processing speed. No significant improvements were found in overall executive function.

The brain consumes approximately 20% of the body's total energy, and creatine contributes to cerebral energy metabolism in the same way it does in muscle. In the final hours of a road race, when fatigue has accumulated hours of tactical decisions (when to attack, when to sit on wheels, when to conserve energy), cognitive processing capacity deteriorates. An improvement in processing speed and attention could translate to better tactical decisions under fatigue.

The evidence in this area is still early, and most studies were conducted in laboratory settings, not during cycling competition. However, the biological plausibility is solid and the direction of evidence is consistent. It is a potential benefit worth considering, especially for cyclists competing in long races with complex finishes.

## The kidney damage myth: what the evidence says

De Souza e Silva and colleagues published a meta-analysis in 2019 in the *Journal of Renal Nutrition* that evaluated 290 studies and selected 15 for qualitative analysis and 6 for quantitative analysis (PMID: 31375416). The conclusion: creatine supplementation did not significantly alter serum creatinine levels or plasma urea values. Creatine did not induce renal damage at the doses and durations studied.

The historical confusion stems from the fact that creatine is metabolized to creatinine, a waste product filtered by the kidneys and used as a marker of renal function. A cyclist supplementing with creatine may show elevated serum creatinine levels on a routine blood test. This does not indicate kidney damage: it simply reflects a larger creatine pool in muscle that generates more creatinine as a normal metabolic byproduct.

Antonio and colleagues directly addressed this and other myths in their 2021 review (PMID: 33557850). Creatine does not cause dehydration or muscle cramps. It is not an anabolic steroid. It does not damage the liver. And it does not cause hair loss, a concern that arose from a single study in rugby players that was never replicated. The ISSN position is clear: creatine monohydrate is the sports supplement with the most extensively documented safety profile in the scientific literature.

## When creatine makes sense for a cyclist

Creatine is not a universal cycling supplement. Its benefit depends on the cyclist's profile and competition type. A sprinter who needs peak power in the last 200 meters has a clear case. A criterium cyclist, where attacks and accelerations are constant, does too. A track rider competing in keirin, sprint, or scratch benefits from the same mechanism.

For the road cyclist facing races with mixed finishes (a climb followed by a sprint), creatine can contribute to the explosive moments without harming aerobic performance. The key is accepting that the small weight increase may be an acceptable cost if competitions are not exclusively mountainous.

For gran fondo riders and recreational cyclists, creatine has less direct competitive relevance, but the cognitive benefit and facilitated recovery between efforts may justify its use if the cyclist performs training that includes high-intensity intervals. A maintenance dose of 3 to 5 g/day has low economic cost, minimal side effects, and a solid safety profile.

The most studied and recommended form is creatine monohydrate powder. Alternative forms such as creatine HCl, creatine ethyl ester, or buffered creatine have not been shown to be superior in any controlled trial. The ISSN position is explicit: creatine monohydrate is the most effective form with the best evidence-to-value ratio.

## Practical guide: protocol for cyclists

For the cyclist who decides to incorporate creatine, the most sensible protocol is simple. Take 3 to 5 g of creatine monohydrate per day, mixed with water or with a meal containing carbohydrates (insulin facilitates muscular uptake of creatine). A loading phase is not necessary unless there is competitive urgency. Maintain supplementation continuously or in blocks of 8 to 12 weeks aligned with competition periods that include intermittent efforts.

There is no evidence that cycling creatine (periods of use and rest) is necessary. Muscular reserves return to baseline levels within 4 to 6 weeks after stopping supplementation. If a cyclist wants to eliminate the extra weight before a specific mountain race, stopping creatine 4 to 6 weeks in advance allows reserves and associated intracellular water to normalize.

Adequate hydration during supplementation is recommended, not because creatine causes dehydration (it does not), but because the increase in intracellular water content means the body distributes water slightly differently. In a sport where thermoregulation and hydration are critical, maintaining appropriate fluid intake is common sense, whether you supplement or not.
