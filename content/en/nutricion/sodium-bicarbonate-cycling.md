## Fifty-Four Seconds Over 40 Kilometers

Shannon and colleagues published a 2024 crossover trial in the *European Journal of Applied Physiology* with 14 trained male cyclists in which sodium bicarbonate improved 40 km time trial performance by an average of 54.14 seconds (± 18.16 s; p = 0.002). The improvement came alongside a 5.6 mmol/L increase in plasma bicarbonate concentration before exercise, confirming that the physiological mechanism worked as intended. The protocol used NaHCO₃ mini-tablets delivered within a carbohydrate hydrogel system and recorded no differences in gastrointestinal symptoms compared to placebo. For a cyclist fighting for seconds in a time trial, 54 seconds is a figure that commands attention.

Sodium bicarbonate has appeared in sports science literature for over 90 years, but the past two years have brought renewed interest at the highest level of the sport. The emergence of formulations that address its biggest practical limitation — gastrointestinal distress — has drawn professional teams back to the supplement and generated a new wave of cycling-specific research. Understanding when it works, when it does not, and how to administer it correctly requires reading the recent evidence carefully.

## How Bicarbonate Slows the Drop in Muscle pH

During a high-intensity effort — a climb attack, a 4 km pursuit, a set of VO₂max intervals — the muscle produces hydrogen ions (H⁺) as a byproduct of anaerobic metabolism. The accumulation of H⁺ lowers intracellular pH, disrupts muscle contraction, and ultimately causes the power drop that cyclists experience as "hitting the wall" during a sustained sprint. The most important buffering system for counteracting this acidosis in the extracellular space is plasma bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻).

Ingesting NaHCO₃ artificially raises the blood bicarbonate reserve before exercise. According to the International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand led by Grgic and colleagues (2021) in the *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*, doses between 0.2 and 0.5 g/kg of body mass produce a transient metabolic alkalosis that expands extracellular buffering capacity. The mechanism is bidirectional: plasma HCO₃⁻ captures H⁺ ions exported from the muscle, and the resulting CO₂ is eliminated through pulmonary ventilation. The practical outcome is that muscle pH remains in more functional ranges for longer, delaying the onset of fatigue in efforts where acidosis is the primary limiting factor.

## The Effective Dose: 0.3 g/kg as the Reference Point

The ISSN position stand establishes that the effective range sits between 0.2 and 0.5 g/kg, with the largest body of evidence concentrated at 0.3 g/kg. For a 70 kg cyclist, that equates to 21 grams of bicarbonate — roughly four level teaspoons — before each relevant session or competition. Doses below 0.2 g/kg generally fail to reach the alkalosis threshold needed for a consistent ergogenic effect; doses close to 0.5 g/kg deliver the maximum buffering effect but also sharply increase the likelihood and severity of gastrointestinal side effects, so most of the practical evidence sits within the 0.2-0.4 g/kg sub-range.

Lopes-Silva and Correia-Oliveira (2023) published in the *European Journal of Sport Science* the first meta-analysis focused exclusively on cycling time trials, analyzing randomized controlled trials of NaHCO₃ versus placebo. Their results confirmed a statistically significant benefit of NaHCO₃ on cycling time trial performance. The specificity of the analysis — cycling only, not a blend of sports — makes it a particularly relevant reference for interpreting the supplement's utility on the bike.

## Timing Matters as Much as Dose

Sodium bicarbonate does not reach its peak blood concentration immediately after ingestion. According to the ingestion-recovery framework proposed by Gurton and colleagues (2024) in the *European Journal of Applied Physiology*, peak metabolic alkalosis typically occurs in a window of 60 to 180 minutes after ingestion, with high interindividual variability. This means two cyclists taking the same dose at the same time may arrive at the start line with very different HCO₃⁻ concentrations. The cyclist who begins their effort exactly at their personal alkalotic peak captures the full ergogenic benefit; the cyclist who races too early or too late sees the effect diminished.

The strategy of individualizing timing — testing in training to determine when personal peak bicarbonate occurs — appears consistently in the studies with the strongest results. Shannon et al. (2024) used a protocol where each cyclist determined their own time to peak bicarbonate in a prior session, then administered supplementation at a personalized 90-240 minutes before the effort. This is the difference between taking bicarbonate "as directed on the label" and actually optimizing its use.

## Which Efforts Benefit — and Which Do Not

The evidence converges on NaHCO₃ producing its greatest ergogenic effect in high-intensity efforts lasting 1 to 10 minutes, where the anaerobic lactic system's contribution is maximal and muscle acidosis is the primary limiting factor. In cycling, this includes track pursuits, climb finishes lasting 3 to 8 minutes, explosive attacks, and — most importantly — repeated high-intensity interval series separated by partial recovery. Grgic et al. (2021) in the ISSN position stand classified the evidence for high-intensity cycling as moderate quality, meaning the effect is real but its magnitude varies between individuals.

Gurton et al. (2024) identified that sodium bicarbonate delivers its greatest strategic value when an athlete competes in multiple efforts with 30 to 90 minutes of recovery between them — exactly the pattern of a team time trial composed of multiple segments, a pursuit competition across rounds, or an omnium track event. In these settings, bicarbonate both enhances the first effort and accelerates acid-base recovery between attempts. For long aerobic rides at submaximal intensity, where the oxidative system handles most of the energetic demand, the benefit is marginal or absent: NaHCO₃ does not improve aerobic efficiency or pedaling economy.

## The Achilles' Heel: Gastrointestinal Effects

The biggest barrier to sodium bicarbonate use is not price or availability — it is gastrointestinal impact. Aktitiz and colleagues (2024) in the *European Journal of Applied Physiology* documented that 33% of cyclists experienced diarrhea and abdominal bloating with a standard acute dose of just 0.2 g/kg — not the more commonly recommended 0.3 g/kg. Symptoms include nausea, cramping, urgent bowel movements, and in severe cases vomiting, and they can appear 30 to 90 minutes after ingestion — exactly when a cyclist should be warming up or lined up at the start. Diarrhea 20 minutes before an important time trial may inflict more damage on performance than any buffering benefit can compensate.

The gastrointestinal side effects of NaHCO₃ follow a dose-dependent pattern and stem from bicarbonate reacting with gastric acid, generating CO₂ in the stomach before the compound can be absorbed. Strategies shown to reduce this incidence include splitting the dose into multiple smaller portions (split protocol) taken 3-4 hours before the effort, co-ingesting bicarbonate with a carbohydrate-containing meal (~1.5 g/kg), or switching to multi-day protocols using lower individual doses. Aktitiz et al. (2024) found that a multi-day protocol of 0.2 g/kg per day over four days reduced symptoms to just 16.7%, with only mild bloating on supplementation days and none on the competition day itself.

## Hydrogel Formulations Change the Equation

Two controlled trials published in 2024 evaluated a system of NaHCO₃ mini-tablets embedded in a carbohydrate hydrogel — specifically designed to circumvent the gastrointestinal problem. Gough and Sparks (2024) documented in *Sports Medicine - Open* that this system reduces aggregate gastrointestinal discomfort by 79 arbitrary units (p < 0.001) compared to traditional vegetarian capsule administration, completely eliminating diarrhea. The proposed mechanism is that the 3 mm mini-tablets pass through the pyloric sphincter directly without dissolving in the stomach, where NaHCO₃ would otherwise generate CO₂ by reacting with hydrochloric acid.

The performance results with this formulation are the strongest published to date in cycling. Shannon et al. (2024) documented the 54.14-second improvement over 40 kilometers with no GI symptom differences versus placebo. Gough and Sparks (2024) in a second study published in *Sports Medicine* reported that the same system improved the first 4 km time trial by 5.1 seconds (1.6%) and the second, separated by 45 minutes of recovery, by 4.4 seconds — again with minimal gastrointestinal symptoms. The effect on acid-base recovery between efforts — documented through post-exercise pH and lactate measurements — suggests that bicarbonate acts not only during exercise but also accelerates the return to baseline for the subsequent attempt.

## Protocols Compared: What to Use and When

| Protocol | Dose | Pre-effort Timing | Advantages | Limitations |
|----------|------|-------------------|-----------|-------------|
| Standard acute dose (powder/capsules) | 0.3 g/kg | 60-90 min | Simple, low cost | ~33% experience moderate/severe GI symptoms |
| Split loading (3-4 divided doses) | 0.3 g/kg total | 3-4 h beforehand | Reduces GI symptom frequency | Requires advance logistical planning |
| Multi-day low dose | 0.2 g/kg/day × 4 days | Final dose on competition day | Only 17% with mild symptoms; none on race day | Somewhat lower efficacy than 0.3 g/kg acute |
| Hydrogel mini-tablets | 0.3 g/kg | 90-240 min (individualized) | Minimal GI symptoms, eliminates diarrhea | Higher cost; requires determining personal timing |

## Bicarbonate Versus Beta-Alanine and Sodium Phosphate

NaHCO₃ acts in the extracellular space; beta-alanine acts intracellularly. The muscle carnosine generated by chronic beta-alanine supplementation (3.2-6.4 g/day for 4-12 weeks) buffers H⁺ directly inside the muscle cell, while bicarbonate captures it in the blood as it exits. Curran-Bowen and colleagues (2024) published in *Biology of Sport* a meta-analysis of 10 studies (243 participants) finding that the combination of both supplements produces a significantly greater effect (SMD = 0.32; p = 0.02) than either alone, where neither supplement reached statistical significance in isolation within that analysis. The synergy is physiologically coherent: they attack acidosis from two separate compartments.

Sodium phosphate operates via a distinct mechanism: it increases 2,3-diphosphoglycerate concentrations in erythrocytes, enhancing oxygen delivery to the muscle. Its utility is clearer for aerobic performance than for anaerobic capacity, and the evidence in cycling is more heterogeneous — a 2023 study found no improvements in 30 km time trials with standard doses. Unlike bicarbonate, which produces a measurable acute effect from a single dose, sodium phosphate requires 3-6 days of loading (50 mg/kg/day) and yields less consistent results across studies.

| Supplement | Mechanism | Standard Dose | Time to Effect | Best Indication |
|------------|-----------|---------------|---------------|-----------------|
| Sodium bicarbonate | Extracellular buffer (HCO₃⁻) | 0.3 g/kg acute | 60-180 min | 1-10 min efforts, repeated intervals |
| Beta-alanine | Intracellular buffer (carnosine) | 3.2-6.4 g/day | 4-12 weeks loading | 60-240 second efforts |
| Sodium phosphate | 2,3-DPG increase; O₂ delivery | 50 mg/kg/day × 3-6 days | Days of loading | Aerobic endurance events |

## Practical Application: Who Should Use It and When

The cyclist profile that benefits most from sodium bicarbonate is one competing or training in repeated high-intensity efforts: criteriums with continuous sprinting, omnium events, 3-5 minute VO₂max interval sets, team pursuits, or stage finishes on climbs lasting 5-8 minutes. Time trialists are also reasonable candidates, particularly in events under 30 minutes where average intensity is high. The data from Aktitiz et al. (2024) on recreational cyclists using low doses suggests that efficacy is most consistent in trained cyclists using the standard 0.3 g/kg protocol, implying that protocol adherence — not just taking something called "bicarbonate" — is what determines the outcome.

The practical starting point is testing the protocol in at least two training sessions before using it in competition, to identify personal gastrointestinal response and determine the optimal peak alkalosis timing. Bicarbonate's effects carry high interindividual variability — some cyclists tolerate it perfectly in powder form with water, others need the hydrogel formulation, and a meaningful percentage experience GI symptoms regardless of the protocol. Using the supplement for the first time on race day, given the available evidence, is a risk-to-reward calculation that rarely favors the athlete.
