In September 2023, the International Olympic Committee published a consensus in the *British Journal of Sports Medicine* (Mountjoy et al., 2023, Br J Sports Med 57(17):1073-1097) that renamed the syndrome: RED-S became REDs, "relative energy deficiency in sport." The change was not cosmetic. The document rewrites the conceptual framework and stops treating low energy availability as an on-off switch, describing it instead as a spectrum. At one end sits adaptable low energy availability, mild and transient, with benign effects; at the other lies the problematic kind, which drags along a cascade of damage to health and performance. For a cyclist, that distinction is the difference between a smart strategy and a hole that takes years to climb out of.

The problem is that road cycling lives obsessed with a single number: the power-to-weight ratio. Dropping weight raises that figure without touching power, so the incentive to eat less is baked into the culture of the sport. On top of that, pedalling is a non-impact activity that does not stimulate bone, and many cyclists confuse periodised fasting with chronically eating below their needs. The upshot is that the peloton is probably one of the athletic populations most exposed to REDs, and almost no one manages it as what it really is: a clinical condition.

## The Equation Every Cyclist Should Know

Energy availability is calculated with a simple formula: the energy you take in minus the energy you burn training, divided by your fat-free mass. The result is expressed in kilocalories per kilogram of fat-free mass per day (kcal/kg FFM/day) and represents the energy left over to maintain everything else: bone, hormones, the immune system, muscle repair, cognitive function. It is not the same as calorie balance. A cyclist can fail to lose weight and still leave the body without fuel for those background functions, because almost everything eaten goes toward sustaining training.

The classic literature placed balance for years at around 45 kcal/kg FFM/day and set 30 kcal/kg as the threshold below which problems began to appear. That cutoff came from Anne Loucks's short, elegant laboratory studies in sedentary women, in whom energy availability was manipulated to observe how reproductive hormones shifted. The 2023 consensus acknowledges that this 30 kcal/kg figure served as a compass, but warns that it is not universal: the response varies enormously between individuals, between the sexes, and across populations. In men, in fact, symptoms appear to set in at considerably lower values, in a range of roughly 9 to 25 kcal/kg FFM/day, as the document itself notes.

### Adaptable or Problematic: Not All Restriction Is Pathology

The major novelty of the 2023 consensus is separating adaptable low energy availability from the problematic kind. A brief, controlled dip—a block of a few days, a specific phase of the season—can produce benign, reversible physiological adjustments without lasting consequences. The problematic version is another matter: it is sustained over time, often involuntary or denied, and is linked to a range of adverse outcomes that the document groups into two separate "wheels," one for health and one for performance. The table below sums up the practical border between the two.

| Feature | Adaptable LEA | Problematic LEA |
|---|---|---|
| Duration | Brief, transient | Sustained over time |
| Magnitude | Mild | Marked or repeated |
| Intent | Planned and controlled | Often involuntary or denied |
| Effects | Reversible, benign | Damage to bone, hormones, immunity, performance |
| Signs | None persistent | Fractures, fatigue, infections, amenorrhea |

The consensus also expanded the list of affected systems compared with earlier versions. To the classics—bone, reproductive function, metabolism, the immune system, the haematological profile—it added sleep disturbances, impaired growth in young athletes, and even urinary incontinence. On the performance side, sustained energy deficiency hits endurance, strength, the response to training, injury risk, and even judgment in racing. In other words, the cyclist who eats little to gain watts ends up, past a certain point, losing them.

## The Cyclist's Skeleton: The Costliest Bill

The figure that best exposes the problem in cycling comes from Hilkens et al. (2023), published in *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise*. The study measured the bone mineral density of elite cyclists at different stages of their careers and found that low bone density is, in its own words, endemic. Among men in the early stages of their careers, 27% already showed low density in the lumbar spine; in those with advanced careers the figure climbed to 64%, with nearly half meeting osteoporotic-range criteria. In women, around 45% showed low lumbar density in both early and advanced phases.

Hilkens and his team identified four factors associated with that bone loss: body mass index, fracture history, bone-specific physical activity, and triiodothyronine (T3) levels, the thyroid hormone that drops when energy runs short. That last point is telling, because it connects bone directly to energy availability: low T3 is one of the hormonal fingerprints of REDs. The skeleton, in other words, deteriorates not only because of cycling's non-impact nature but also because of the lack of fuel that prevents it from repairing itself. And at 50 km/h, an osteopenic femur or pelvis turns a routine crash into a serious fracture.

### Hormones, Metabolism, and Watts

Below the problematic threshold, the body cuts the functions it deems expendable. Reproduction is usually the first to go: in women the menstrual cycle is lost—its hormonal phases and their interaction with performance deserve a separate analysis—and in men testosterone and sex drive decline. Basal metabolism slows to save energy, which explains why a cyclist in chronic deficit sometimes fails to lose weight despite eating little. The immune system weakens and respiratory infections increase, the kind that wreck entire weeks of base training. Each of these pieces is at once a health problem and a brake on performance that the consensus documents with its physiological model.

## The Dark Flip Side of Train-Low

The most dangerous misunderstanding in the peloton is confusing train-low with REDs. Training with low carbohydrate availability—doing specific sessions with depleted glycogen stores to stimulate mitochondrial adaptations—is a periodised, targeted, deliberate tool. It has a beginning and an end, is applied in specific sessions, and is followed by a refuel. REDs, by contrast, is the chronic, uncontrolled version: days, weeks, or months eating below what the body needs to function. The border lies in duration and intent, exactly the same line that separates adaptable LEA from the problematic kind.

The risk appears when train-low stops being a session and becomes a lifestyle. A cyclist who strings together fasted workouts, restricts carbohydrates permanently, and never refuels is no longer periodising: they are settled into sustained low energy availability. The 2023 consensus warns that it is still unresolved whether the deficit from eating less is equivalent to the deficit from training more, or whether what does the most harm is the shortfall in total energy or specifically in carbohydrate. What is clear is that the tool, poorly dosed, turns into pathology.

## How Much to Eat: The Numbers That Matter

The practical target for most cyclists is to stay at or above 45 kcal/kg of fat-free mass per day outside planned weight-loss phases. For a 70 kg cyclist at 12% body fat, fat-free mass sits around 62 kg, which works out to roughly 2,800 kcal available after subtracting the cost of training. If that day they burn 1,500 kcal on the bike, they need to take in on the order of 4,300 kcal just to avoid falling into deficit. The math is daunting, and that is why so many cyclists live below the line without knowing it: they underestimate how much they spend and overestimate how much they eat.

The way to fix it is not to eat more in general but to eat around training. Arriving at hard sessions with carbohydrate on board, taking in carbs during long efforts, and recovering with protein and carbohydrate in the post-exercise window closes the gap where the deficit usually hides. Calcium deserves specific attention for its role in bone: it has been reported that prolonged sweating can draw out calcium and promote bone resorption, so securing intake before long rides may help protect the skeleton that cycling does not stimulate. Adequate energy is, in every case, the prerequisite: without it, no bone supplement works.

## The Warning Signs and What to Do

The red flags of REDs are concrete and worth watching. Loss or irregularity of menstruation, repeated stress fractures, fatigue that does not lift with rest, frequent respiratory infections, low libido, sleep disturbances, and stalled performance despite training more are the signs the 2023 consensus lists. No single one confirms the syndrome, but their accumulation is a pattern that calls for intervention. The clinical tool that accompanies the consensus, the IOC REDs CAT2, exists precisely so that doctors and nutritionists can stratify risk in a standardised way.

For the everyday cyclist, the takeaway is direct: being light and being healthy are not the same thing, and the shortcut of eating little has a biological ceiling that, once crossed, costs watts and bones. Before starting a weight-loss block, it pays to calculate real energy availability, keep it above the threshold outside those phases, periodise the restriction rather than make it chronic, and surround training with food instead of emptying it. If the warning signs appear, the next step is not to train more or eat less but to consult a professional who applies the REDs framework. Sustainable performance is built on adequate energy; chronic deficit only brings the bill forward.
