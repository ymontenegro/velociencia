## 56% of Athletes Don't Reach the Optimal Threshold

A meta-analysis by Farrokhyar et al. (2015) published in *Sports Medicine* assessed 2,313 athletes across 23 studies worldwide and found that 56% had inadequate vitamin D concentrations (PMID: 25277808). This isn't a marginal finding: insufficiency was even more pronounced among indoor athletes and worsened during winter months, when ultraviolet radiation drops below the threshold needed for cutaneous synthesis. For a cyclist logging base miles on an indoor trainer between November and March, the numbers are worse still.

The issue extends well beyond bone health. Over the past two decades, research has identified vitamin D receptors (VDRs) in virtually every tissue in the body, including skeletal muscle, the heart, and the immune system. The scientific community still debates how much of that biology converts into real watts on the bike. The honest answer: it depends entirely on where you're starting from.

## What 25(OH)D Is — and Why Riding Outside Isn't Enough

Vitamin D is not a conventional nutrient but a prohormone the body synthesizes when UVB radiation strikes the 7-dehydrocholesterol in the skin. The liver converts it into 25-hydroxyvitamin D — 25(OH)D — which is what blood tests measure and what serves as the marker of bodily status. The final conversion happens in the kidneys, where 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (calcitriol) acts on VDRs to regulate hundreds of genes, including those involved in muscle protein synthesis and intestinal calcium absorption.

Road cyclists face a specific vulnerability that isn't immediately obvious. While they train outdoors, they typically do so in the early morning or evening hours, when the solar angle is low and the UV index insufficient to trigger skin conversion. Add to this the full-coverage lycra that shields most of the body's surface area, the routine use of sunscreen, and the tendency to complete winter volume sessions indoors. A review by Larson-Meyer and Willis (2010) in *Current Sports Medicine Reports* identified these factors as distinguishing cyclists from the general population (PMID: 20622540). A road cyclist can log hundreds of hours outdoors annually and still accumulate a meaningful seasonal deficit.

## Clinical Thresholds: Where the Floor Is and What Optimal Looks Like

The debate over the ideal 25(OH)D level for athletes remains open, but there is clinical consensus on the lower boundaries. Values are expressed in nanograms per milliliter (ng/mL) or nanomoles per liter (nmol/L); to convert, multiply ng/mL by 2.496.

| Status | 25(OH)D (ng/mL) | 25(OH)D (nmol/L) | Practical Implications |
|---|---|---|---|
| Severe deficiency | <10 | <25 | High stress fracture risk, pronounced muscle weakness |
| Deficiency | 10–20 | 25–50 | Impaired immune function, documented neuromuscular effects |
| Insufficiency | 20–30 | 50–75 | Gray zone with measurable impact on aerobic power |
| Sufficiency | 30–50 | 75–125 | Range associated with optimal athletic performance |
| Excess | >100 | >250 | Risk of hypercalcemia and renal damage |

Owens, Allison, and Close argued in their 2018 *Sports Medicine* review that the target for athletes should be 40–60 ng/mL (100–150 nmol/L), somewhat higher than the general clinical consensus of 30 ng/mL (PMID: 29368183). Their rationale: skeletal muscle under high demand may have greater vitamin D requirements to sustain protein synthesis and type II fiber function. That said, there is a meaningful distinction between what is physiologically desirable and what direct clinical trial evidence in cyclists actually supports.

## Why Type II Fibers Are at the Center of the Story

Skeletal muscle expresses VDRs, and vitamin D appears to directly influence fast-twitch (type II) muscle fibers. Deficiency leads to selective atrophy of these fibers and partial replacement by fat tissue, a process documented histopathologically in multiple studies. The narrative review by Książek, Zagrodna, and Słowińska-Lisowska (2019) in *Nutrients* synthesized the available preclinical and clinical evidence: deficiency correlates with reduced contraction velocity and lower explosive force output — both critical capacities for sprinting and attacking on short climbs (PMID: 31382666).

The mechanism runs in two directions. On one hand, calcitriol stimulates the synthesis of contractile proteins — actin and myosin — through nuclear VDRs. On the other, it modulates calcium uptake by the sarcoplasmic reticulum, which is essential for the contraction-relaxation kinetics of muscle fibers. In athletes who already have sufficient vitamin D, this second pathway operates at full capacity; further supplementation provides no additional benefit through these routes.

Most et al. (2021) published a cross-sectional study in *Sports Medicine Open* examining 112 professional indoor athletes, 30% of whom showed vitamin D insufficiency even during summer (PMID: 34648100). Athletes with levels ≥30 ng/mL achieved a peak aerobic power of 3.9 ± 0.9 W/kg compared to 3.5 ± 0.8 W/kg in the insufficient group (p = 0.03). Vitamin D emerged as the only independent predictor of peak aerobic power in their multivariate model. The cross-sectional design prevents drawing causal conclusions directly, but a gap of 400 mW/kg is large enough to be clinically meaningful for any competitive cyclist.

Close et al. (2013) evaluated professional athletes during the UK winter and, after eight weeks of supplementation at 5,000 IU/day of vitamin D3 in the deficient group, observed significant improvements in 10-meter sprint times and vertical jump height (PMID: 23083379). A parallel dose-response trial by the same group randomized 30 club-level athletes to placebo, 20,000 IU/week, or 40,000 IU/week of vitamin D3 for 12 weeks, confirming dose-dependent increases in serum 25(OH)D without reaching significant differences in the musculoskeletal performance measures assessed (PMID: 23410885). The critical detail: there is no robust evidence that supplementing already-sufficient athletes produces additional performance gains. Owens et al. (2017) confirmed this ceiling in a trial with 46 elite athletes using doses of 35,000 or 70,000 IU/week: the higher regimen elevated the inactivation metabolite 24,25-dihydroxyvitamin D, indicating the body actively limits conversion when sufficiency is already achieved (PMID: 27741217).

## Solar Exposure: The Calculation Most Cyclists Never Run

Cutaneous vitamin D synthesis requires UVB radiation with a UV index of 3 or higher. At latitudes like Madrid, Melbourne, or Santiago, that threshold is only reached during the middle hours of the day between spring and autumn. A 10- to 20-minute session with arms and legs exposed to midday sun can generate between 1,000 and 4,000 IU — enough to cover daily requirements if done consistently. The problem for most cyclists is that this window rarely coincides with their training hours during winter, or doesn't exist at all above 40° latitude in the colder months.

Diet alone cannot bridge the gap. The most vitamin D-rich foods — wild salmon, mackerel, sardines, egg yolks, fortified dairy — contribute at most 200 to 400 IU per day in a varied diet, a fraction of what skin can produce with adequate sun exposure. The Farrokhyar et al. (2015) meta-analysis confirmed that indoor athletes have systematically lower 25(OH)D levels than outdoor athletes, and that values across all athlete groups fall significantly during winter. A cyclist in the Southern Hemisphere between June and August, or in the Northern Hemisphere between November and February, faces conditions comparable to those of an indoor sport athlete during those same months: solar synthesis effectively stops.

### High-Risk Groups Among Cyclists

The cyclists most likely to develop insufficiency are those training primarily indoors during winter, those living above 40° north or south latitude, those with darker skin tones (who require up to six times more sun exposure to synthesize equivalent vitamin D), and those using sunscreen consistently on all outdoor rides. A fair-skinned road cyclist riding from 7 to 9 AM in January in Chicago or Edinburgh can spend three hours outdoors without generating any meaningful vitamin D synthesis, simply because the UV index never reaches the activation threshold at those hours and latitudes.

## Supplementation Dosing: The Safe Range and the Line You Shouldn't Cross

The Owens et al. (2018) review and Endocrine Society guidelines place the reasonable supplementation range for athletes at 1,000 to 4,000 IU/day of vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol). This form is approximately 87% more potent than vitamin D2 (ergocalciferol) at raising and maintaining serum 25(OH)D, according to the crossover trial by Heaney et al. (2011) in the *Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism* (PMID: 21177785). Vitamin D toxicity is real but requires chronic daily intake above 10,000 IU for weeks to months. Symptoms include hypercalcemia, nausea, cognitive impairment, and kidney damage. At doses of 2,000 to 4,000 IU/day, the safety margin is wide.

| Starting Status | 25(OH)D (ng/mL) | Recommended Daily Dose | Form | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Severe deficiency | <10 | 4,000–6,000 IU | D3 with medical supervision | Retest at 12 weeks |
| Deficiency | 10–20 | 2,000–4,000 IU | D3 | Blood recheck at 3 months |
| Insufficiency | 20–30 | 1,000–2,000 IU | D3 | Autumn and winter only |
| Sufficiency | 30–50 | None or 400–800 IU maintenance | D3 | Winter only if prolonged |
| Toxicity threshold | — | Do not exceed 10,000 IU/day chronically | — | Hypercalcemia risk |

The 2026 *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition* review by Rowland et al. on supplements for cycling performance categorizes vitamin D as a medical support supplement — alongside calcium and omega-3s — rather than a direct ergogenic (PMID: 41685663). Its performance benefit is indirect and contingent on correcting a pre-existing deficit. Practically speaking, this means that pushing vitamin D above the sufficiency threshold does not add watts.

## When and How to Get Tested

A 25(OH)D blood test costs between $20 and $50 at most labs and is the only reliable way to know where you stand. The most informative times are at the end of winter — February or March in the Northern Hemisphere, August or September in the Southern — when levels reach their seasonal nadir, and before the start of the competitive season. Supplementing without a prior blood test means acting on an unknown: you may already be in the sufficient range, or you may have a genuine deficiency that requires therapeutic doses over several weeks to correct.

If your result shows sufficiency (>30 ng/mL) and there are no additional risk factors, preventive supplementation during summer months adds little value. If the level falls between 20 and 30 ng/mL in winter, 1,000 to 2,000 IU of D3 daily is a very low-cost, low-risk intervention with potential benefit for musculoskeletal health, immune function, and — if the gap observed by Most et al. holds in cyclists specifically — peak aerobic power. If the result is below 20 ng/mL, correction is a clinical priority: 4,000 IU daily for 12 weeks, with a follow-up blood panel, is the standard protocol.

## The Benefit Is Correcting the Deficit, Not Exceeding It

Treating vitamin D as just another performance supplement creates the wrong expectations. Athletes with deficiency can expect genuine improvements in neuromuscular function, bone health, and immunity after correcting it; those already sufficient have no solid evidence justifying supplementation to gain extra watts. What is well established is that deficiency carries a real and measurable cost: elevated stress fracture risk, slower recovery from heavy training blocks, greater susceptibility to respiratory illness during high-load winter periods, and — based on the Most et al. data — a significantly lower peak aerobic power than peers with adequate levels.

A blood test in September, and if warranted, 1,000 to 2,000 IU of D3 daily through the darker months, is probably the highest-value intervention available to a competitive cyclist at temperate latitudes. It is not a performance hack. It is basic maintenance.
