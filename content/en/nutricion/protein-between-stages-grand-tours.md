With 0.8 g of carbohydrate plus 0.4 g of protein per kilogram of body weight per hour during the first two hours after exhaustion, nitrogen balance shifts from –82.4 ± 11.5 mg/kg to +7.0 ± 15.4 mg/kg. That figure, published by Sollie et al. in the *Journal of Applied Physiology* in 2018 (PMID: 30212306) with eight elite cyclists, captures the problem with rare precision: after an intense stage, relying on carbohydrates alone during the recovery window leaves the body in continued net muscle catabolism; adding protein tips the balance toward neutral. Multiplied across 21 stages, that accumulated deficit is what separates the rider who arrives functional in the Alps of week three from the one dragging depleted legs to the finish.

Nutritional logistics in a grand tour tend to revolve around carbohydrates: how many grams per hour on the bike, when to load glycogen the day before, how to distribute intake throughout the day to perform tomorrow. That focus is correct but incomplete. Protein does not compete with carbohydrates for relevance — it operates on a different plane, one of structural repair, mitochondrial synthesis, and preservation of the muscle mass that allows power thresholds to hold three weeks after day one.

## Daily Requirements: Why 1.8 g/kg Is No Longer the Ceiling

The narrative review by Witard, Hearris and Morgan published in *Sports Medicine* in 2025 (PMID: 40117058) sets an updated benchmark for endurance athletes: approximately 1.8 g of protein per kilogram of body mass per day on standard training days, and 2.0 g/kg on rest or active recovery days. The increase on rest days is not counterintuitive: those days carry lower carbohydrate intake and, consequently, a reduced insulin-mediated anabolic signal, which means protein must cover a greater share of the repair demand. The study by Valenzuela et al. (2023, PMID: 36686220) with 24 professional U23 cyclists during a six-day training camp documented that riders at that level habitually maintained intakes above 2.5 g/kg per day without additional supplementation — illustrating the distance between minimum literature recommendations and elite practice.

The 1.6–2.2 g/kg/day range that appears in the most recent guidelines is not arbitrary. The lower limit (1.6 g/kg) represents the threshold below which muscle protein synthesis begins to decline even without intense exercise. The upper end (2.2 g/kg) reflects the most demanding conditions: six-hour mountain stages with partial carbohydrate restriction, where the body oxidizes branched-chain amino acids — leucine first — as an additional energy source. The *International Society of Sports Nutrition* (Jäger et al., 2017, PMID: 28642676) places the effective range at 1.4 to 2.0 g/kg for most exercising individuals, with explicit acknowledgment that certain high-load contexts raise that ceiling. For a 70-kilogram cyclist on a high-altitude stage, reaching 2.0 g/kg means distributing 140 grams of protein across a day where carbohydrates are already competing actively for gastric space and absorptive capacity.

EF Pro Cycling, which has published information about its nutritional protocols during the Tour de France, reports a daily target of 2.0 to 2.5 g/kg for its riders throughout the three-week race. Visma–Lease a Bike uses its own application (Foodcoach) integrated with nutritional tracking software to calculate and monitor individualized protein and energy targets in real time during the race — a system that the *UCI Sports Nutrition Project Race Nutrition for Road Cycling* (2026, IJSNEM Vol. 36) identifies as part of the current WorldTour trend toward digital nutritional management platforms.

## Four or Five Doses: The Frequency That Activates the Anabolic Signal

Daily protein total matters, but dose frequency determines how many times per day the muscle receives the signal to synthesize. The ISSN position stand (Jäger et al., 2017) establishes that protein doses should be distributed every three to four hours throughout the day, with each dose providing between 20 and 40 grams of high-quality protein — equivalent to 0.25 g/kg on a relative basis. The review by Witard et al. (2025) updates that figure slightly for endurance athletes in the immediate post-exercise period, recommending 0.5 g/kg per dose to maximally stimulate contractile protein synthesis, or 0.4 g/kg when gastrointestinal tolerance or carbohydrate availability limits intake.

The mechanism behind this frequency is the leucine threshold. Leucine, a branched-chain amino acid, acts as a metabolic sensor that activates the mTORC1 pathway — the intracellular switch that initiates protein synthesis. Research from Layman and collaborators has established that approximately 2.5 grams of leucine per dose are required to exceed this threshold in young adults. Casein contains approximately 8.5% leucine by weight, meaning 30 grams of casein provides roughly 2.55 grams of leucine — just above threshold — while 40 grams delivers approximately 3.4 grams, with a wider margin. For a 70-kilogram cyclist following the 0.4 g/kg per-dose recommendation, 28 grams of high-quality protein represents the functional minimum per sitting.

Five doses for a total of 167 grams equals 2.4 g/kg for a 70-kilogram cyclist. The distribution is not arbitrary: a 35-gram breakfast three to four hours before the stage fills the plasma amino acid pool during the early part of the race; the on-bike dose (12–15 grams, typically via a protein-enriched bar or gel) maintains amino acid flow when catabolism is at its peak; the post-stage dose covers the window of greatest sensitivity to muscle protein synthesis; the main dinner loads the overnight repair cycle; and the pre-sleep casein sustains synthesis through the hours of rest.

## The Post-Stage Window: The First Ninety Minutes

Rustad et al. published in *PLoS One* in 2016 (PMID: 27078151) a study with eight trained cyclists consuming, during the first two hours of recovery after exhaustive exercise, either 1.2 g of carbohydrate per kilogram per hour or 0.8 g of carbohydrate plus 0.4 g of protein per kilogram per hour. The following day, time to exhaustion was 63.5 ± 4.4 minutes in the CHO+protein group versus 49.8 ± 5.4 minutes in the carbohydrate-only group. Two years later, Sollie et al. (2018, PMID: 30212306) replicated a similar protocol in eight elite cyclists with a VO₂max of 74 ml/kg/min and found an 8.5% improvement in 26-kilometer time trial performance and a 3.7% improvement in 10-second sprint power the following day with CHO+protein versus isoenergetic carbohydrate.

The critical nuance in these data concerns total energy. A meta-analysis published in 2020 in *Sports Medicine* (PMID: 32826640) examining 20 studies on carbohydrate and protein co-ingestion and post-exercise glycogen synthesis found that added protein only improved glycogen resynthesis when carbohydrate intake was below 1.2 g/kg/hour; when carbohydrate provision was already adequate, adding protein did not accelerate glycogen repletion further, but it maintained its independent benefit for muscle protein synthesis. In a grand tour context — where cyclists consume 90 to 120 grams of carbohydrate per hour on the bike and must then replenish glycogen as rapidly as possible — post-stage protein should not replace carbohydrates: it must be added to them to activate both recovery processes simultaneously.

The window of peak sensitivity to muscle protein synthesis post-exercise extends approximately two hours after the stage ends. In WorldTour team practice, this translates into the classic recovery shake served on the team bus at the finish line, with a carbohydrate-to-protein ratio of 3:1 or 4:1. A 70-kilogram rider who ingests 84 grams of carbohydrate and 28 grams of protein in that shake simultaneously meets the minimum protein dose (0.4 g/kg) and contributes meaningfully to glycogen resynthesis before solid foods from dinner can be processed.

## Overnight Casein: Mitochondrial Synthesis While You Sleep

In 2023, Trommelen and collaborators published in *Sports Medicine* (PMID: 36857005) the first randomized controlled trial to specifically quantify the impact of pre-sleep protein on mitochondrial protein synthesis rates after endurance exercise. Thirty-six young men performed evening endurance exercise and 30 minutes before sleep ingested 45 grams of casein, 45 grams of whey protein, or a non-caloric placebo. Mitochondrial protein synthesis rate was 0.087 ± 0.020 %/h in the protein groups versus 0.067 ± 0.016 %/h in the placebo; myofibrillar synthesis was 0.060 ± 0.014 versus 0.047 ± 0.011 %/h. There were no significant differences between casein and whey, suggesting that protein type is less determinative than protein presence.

The study with professional cyclists introduces a necessary qualification. Valenzuela, Alejo and collaborators (2023, PMID: 36686220) recruited 24 professional U23 cyclists (peak VO₂: 79.8 ± 4.9 ml/kg/min) during a six-day training camp and randomly assigned participants to consume 40 grams of casein before sleep, 40 grams of casein in the afternoon, or 40 grams of carbohydrate before sleep as an isocaloric control. No significant between-group differences were found for any fatigue, recovery, or performance outcomes. The explanatory factor is background protein intake: the cyclists in that study were already consuming more than 2.5 g/kg/day habitually, which reduces the marginal effect of an additional casein dose toward zero.

The reconciliation of these two findings has a direct implication for grand tour nutrition. When total daily protein intake sits at 1.8–2.0 g/kg — the range recommended by Witard et al. (2025) for standard training days, and one that many non-professional cyclists fail to reach even during competition — pre-sleep casein provides a real benefit to overnight mitochondrial synthesis. When daily intake already exceeds 2.5 g/kg (as occurs in WorldTour teams with dedicated chef and soigneurs), that additional benefit diminishes because the body already has a steady amino acid supply. The practical goal for most non-professional cyclists participating in multi-day events is therefore to reach the daily total first and only then optimize timing.

## Why This Is Not a Carbohydrate Loading Protocol

Carbohydrate loading is a point-in-time pre-competition strategy: its protocols work in the 24–72 hours before a long event, and its objective is to elevate muscle glycogen stores to supercompensation. Protein management during a grand tour is a continuous process operating across all three weeks of racing, with no equivalent "supercompensation" state: there is no pre-stage protein saturation comparable to glycogen, because muscle proteins are constantly turning over and mechanical damage demands persistent repair.

Both strategies are complementary and non-exclusive. On a high-altitude stage followed by a rest day, the rider must replenish muscle glycogen with high carbohydrate density (8–10 g/kg/day on that recovery day) while simultaneously maintaining or slightly elevating protein intake to 2.0 g/kg to support repair of damaged tissue. The common error in amateur and semi-professional practice is to compensate for increased carbohydrates on rest days by reducing protein — when the opposite should occur.

## What Changes in Week Three

Across three weeks of a grand tour, a cumulative process unfolds that single-day laboratory studies can barely capture. The *UCI Sports Nutrition Project* review (2026, IJSNEM Vol. 36) notes that contemporary WorldTour team practice has moved toward a more homogeneous distribution of caloric intake throughout the day — including protein — precisely to prevent the catabolic peaks that appear when a rider arrives at dinner with a deep caloric deficit accumulated since morning. The nutritional monitoring software used by teams like Visma allows real-time detection when a rider has accumulated a 30–40 gram protein deficit during the first half of the day, enabling adjustments to dinner and the overnight dose accordingly.

The rider facing the final stages of a three-week race is not just managing that day's glycogen: they are harvesting the result of 20 days of nutritional decisions. Protein — in its quantities, its hourly distribution, its quality measured in grams of available leucine per dose — is the variable most frequently underestimated in that balance.

## Practical Application: The Numbers That Matter

For a 70-kilogram cyclist in a three-week grand tour, the available data converges on a manageable protocol. Daily protein intake should sit between 126 and 154 grams (1.8–2.2 g/kg), distributed across at least four doses that each guarantee a minimum of 28–35 grams (0.4–0.5 g/kg) per occasion. The post-stage dose must occur before 90 minutes elapse from the end of the effort, combined with carbohydrates at a rate of at least 1.0–1.2 g/kg/h during the first two hours of recovery. A 30–40 gram overnight casein dose provides real benefit for mitochondrial synthesis when the day's total has not reached the 1.8 g/kg threshold; when the total is already covered, its marginal contribution diminishes but does not disappear.

Witard et al. (2025) conclude that protein in endurance sport has historically been overshadowed by carbohydrates in sports nutrition narratives, and that the metabolic evidence of the past decade justifies a comprehensive revision of that hierarchy. In the context of a grand tour, both macronutrients are equally non-negotiable. Carbohydrates determine whether the cyclist can hold power during the stage; protein determines whether they can do it again the next day.
