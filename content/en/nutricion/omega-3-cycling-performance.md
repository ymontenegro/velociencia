## Only One Elite Athlete Out of One Hundred and Six

Of the 106 elite German winter endurance athletes evaluated by Von Schacky, Kemper, Haslbauer, and Halle in 2014, exactly one had an Omega-3 Index within the recommended range of 8 to 11%. The rest — national and international competitors in disciplines such as cross-country skiing and biathlon — fell below that threshold, with many in ranges the authors described as carrying elevated cardiovascular risk. The number is not a minor anecdote: the Omega-3 Index measures the percentage of EPA and DHA relative to total fatty acids in the erythrocyte membrane, reflecting the chronic state of omega-3 tissue incorporation rather than what the athlete has eaten in the past week.

The Omega-3 Index matters in sports nutrition for a technical reason that is frequently overlooked. Unlike plasma cholesterol, which shifts with recent diet, the Omega-3 Index changes slowly because red blood cells have a half-life of approximately 120 days. Reaching an index of 8% requires weeks of sustained supplementation, and studies lasting fewer than six to eight weeks tend to underestimate the true potential of the intervention. The official position of the International Society of Sports Nutrition published in 2025 in the *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition* (Jäger et al., PMID 39810703) explicitly identifies athletes as a population at risk for long-chain omega-3 PUFA insufficiency.

Cyclists in particular sustain elevated heart rates for hours, accumulate repeated muscular damage between training days, and depend on overnight recovery to adapt. Those three conditions — chronic oxidative stress, repeated inflammation, and the need for cardiovascular efficiency — are precisely the domains where the evidence on EPA and DHA is most robust and most specific to cycling.

## The Oxygen Cost of Pedaling and the Data in Cyclists

Hingley, Macartney, Brown, McLennan, and Peoples published in 2017 in the *International Journal of Sport Nutrition and Exercise Metabolism* a double-blind crossover trial with 26 trained cyclists who took DHA-rich tuna oil (560 mg of DHA and 140 mg of EPA daily) for eight weeks. The most significant variable was not sprint power or time trial result: it was the oxygen cost during high-intensity cycling, which fell by 154 ml of O₂ per minute per 100 watts in the fish oil group compared to –23 ml in the control group (p < 0.05). Peak power and five-minute time trial performance did not change significantly between groups.

This finding carries a direct practical implication: if a cyclist can sustain a given power output at lower oxygen consumption, the available margin before reaching VO₂max widens. The legs do not produce more watts; each watt simply costs less oxygen. The Omega-3 Index in the fish oil group rose from 4.7% at baseline to 6.3% at the end of eight weeks — still below the 8% target — which suggests that longer protocols or higher doses might produce even more pronounced effects.

## Heart Rate: The Most Reproducible Effect

A study published in 2025 in *Frontiers in Nutrition* evaluated 55 endurance-trained amateurs in a three-arm randomized design: EPA-rich fish oil (3 g/day), DHA-rich algae oil (3 g/day), or coconut oil as placebo, over six weeks. Both omega-3 groups raised their Omega-3 Index from below 6% to above 8% after the intervention, while the placebo group showed no change. Submaximal exercise heart rate fell by 4 beats per minute in the EPA-rich group and by 9 beats per minute in the DHA-rich group (p ≤ 0.001 in both cases). Perceived effort (RPE) also decreased with both forms of supplementation. However, neither active group improved 24 km time trial performance significantly more than placebo.

The reduction in submaximal heart rate has a documented physiological mechanism. EPA and DHA incorporate into cardiomyocyte membranes, where they modulate the cell's electrical excitability. In vitro studies have shown these molecules can reduce the contraction rate of cardiomyocytes by 50 to 80% without altering contraction amplitude. For a cyclist holding 150 beats per minute during a four-hour stage who, after eight weeks of EPA/DHA supplementation, sustains the same power at 141 beats per minute, that represents real cardiac headroom for the hardest moments of the race. The correlation between Omega-3 Index increase and heart rate reduction (ρ = –0.43) observed in the 2025 study suggests that the higher the index climbs, the greater the reduction.

## Post-Exercise Inflammation: What Changes and What Doesn't

A systematic review of randomized controlled trials on omega-3 and the post-exercise inflammatory response (published in 2024 in *Nutrients*, PMC11243702) found that creatine kinase (CK), a marker of muscle damage, was significantly lower in the omega-3 group compared to controls in three of the four studies that measured it. Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) was also higher in control groups at 72 hours post-exercise in both studies analyzing it. Results for pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α were more heterogeneous: some studies found no significant differences, while others such as Barquilha et al. (2023) reported reductions.

A meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials published in 2020 in *BioMed Research International* (Lv et al., PMID 32382573) found a statistically significant reduction in delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS) with omega-3 supplementation following eccentric exercise (mean difference: –0.93 points; 95% CI: –1.44 to –0.42; p = 0.0004). The complication is that this reduction falls below the minimum clinically important difference of 1.4 points on a 10-point scale, which led the authors to characterize the evidence as low-quality in terms of practical relevance. Jouris, McDaniel, and Weiss had already reported in 2011 in the *Journal of Sports Science and Medicine* (PMID 24150614) that 3 g/day of omega-3 for seven days reduced DOMS significantly after eccentric bicep curl exercise in 11 participants, though with limited statistical power.

The correct interpretation of these findings for the cyclist is nuanced. Reduced CK and LDH with omega-3 does not mean the muscle sustains no damage or that training fails to produce its adaptive stimulus; it means the acute inflammatory response appears moderated, which could theoretically support better readiness for the following day's session. Whether that moderation of inflammation translates into superior long-term performance is a question the available studies cannot yet answer with certainty.

## Dose and Molecular Form: Details That Change Absorption

The ISSN 2025 position does not prescribe a single EPA+DHA dose for all contexts, but the literature it draws from places the typical effective range between 2 and 3 grams of combined EPA and DHA per day for effects on inflammation and recovery. The 2024 systematic review highlighted that VanDusseldorp et al. (2020) observed dose-dependent effects: 6 g/day of fish oil produced greater DOMS reduction and faster vertical jump recovery than doses of 2 or 4 g. For modulating the Omega-3 Index, Drobnic, Calatayud, and colleagues published in *Nutrients* in 2021 (PMID 34959789) that krill oil supplementation in power-trained athletes increased the high-sensitivity Omega-3 Index, elevated plasma choline, and reduced the post-exercise drop in total antioxidant capacity to 8%, versus 21% in the placebo group.

Molecular form matters. Commercial fish oil can be presented as re-esterified triglycerides, natural triglycerides, or ethyl esters. Dyerberg and colleagues demonstrated in 2010 that the bioavailability of EPA+DHA from re-esterified triglycerides was 124% relative to natural fish oil, while the ethyl ester form reached only 73%. Ethyl esters — the cheapest and most common form in low-cost supplements — require co-ingestion with a fat-containing meal to improve absorption; without that dietary context, a significant portion of the supplement is lost unabsorbed. Krill oil, which carries EPA and DHA as phospholipids, shows absorption profiles comparable to re-esterified triglycerides, though at a markedly higher cost per gram of omega-3.

## Food Sources and the Option for Vegans

Atlantic farmed salmon contains between 1.5 and 2.5 g of EPA+DHA per 100 grams, depending on the fish's feed. Canned sardines in olive oil provide approximately 1.5 g per 100 g; mackerel and herring are comparable. For a cyclist who can realistically incorporate two or three portions of fatty fish per week, EPA+DHA intake may approach 1–1.5 g daily — likely insufficient to reach the 8% Omega-3 Index target according to the data from Peoples et al. Flaxseed oil and chia seeds provide ALA (alpha-linolenic acid), the plant-based precursor of EPA and DHA, but the metabolic conversion of ALA to EPA is below 5–10% in humans, and to DHA it is essentially negligible.

Vegetarian or vegan cyclists can turn to microalgae oil, which is precisely the primary source from which fish obtain their DHA. Algae oil from *Schizochytrium* or *Nannochloropsis* is available in capsules with 200–500 mg of DHA per capsule, with or without EPA. The 2025 study in *Frontiers in Nutrition* (PMC12417169) used DHA-rich algae oil in one of its groups and documented Omega-3 Index increases from below 6% to above 8% in six weeks, with statistically significant heart rate reductions. The environmental sustainability of algae oil is considerably better than that of fish oil, making it an option with a dual argument for athletes with responsible consumption criteria.

## The Question About Training Adaptations

McGlory and colleagues published in 2019 in *Frontiers in Nutrition* (PMID 31555658) a review on omega-3 and muscle protein turnover identifying mechanisms by which EPA and DHA might facilitate muscle protein synthesis. The concern some researchers have raised, however, runs in the opposite direction: if omega-3 attenuates the post-exercise inflammatory response, might it also reduce the adaptive signal that training needs to produce its effects? This question is most relevant in the context of strength and hypertrophy.

The ISSN (Jäger et al., 2025) concludes that EPA+DHA supplementation "may not confer a hypertrophic benefit in young adults," but adds that when combined with resistance training it may improve strength in a dose- and duration-dependent manner. For the endurance cyclist, the more pertinent question is different: does omega-3 attenuate aerobic adaptations, mitochondrial biogenesis, or VO₂max? No published evidence supports that hypothesis. Mitochondrial biogenesis is regulated primarily through PGC-1α, whose activation depends on the mechanical and metabolic stress of training rather than on the resolution of peripheral inflammation. The type of inflammation that omega-3 moderates — mediated by pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α — is distinct from the cellular signaling that activates aerobic adaptations.

## Safety and Maximum Doses

The US Food and Drug Administration classifies marine omega-3 intake up to 3 g/day as "Generally Recognized as Safe" (GRAS). A meta-analysis published in 2024 in the *Journal of the American Heart Association* (PMID 38742535) found that omega-3s were not associated with elevated bleeding risk in controlled clinical trials at standard doses, though it noted that purified EPA at very high doses might carry a modestly increased risk. For athletes without anticoagulant therapy using 2–4 g/day, the evidence does not point to a clinically meaningful bleeding problem.

The more concrete risk with omega-3 supplements is lipid oxidation of the product itself. EPA and DHA are highly unsaturated molecules susceptible to peroxidation when the oil is exposed to light, heat, or air. An oxidized supplement not only loses its activity but can generate aldehydes and free radicals with effects contrary to those sought. The practical criterion for the cyclist is to refrigerate supplements after opening, verify expiration dates, and avoid transparent pouch formats exposed to direct light. The presence of vitamin E (tocopherol) as an antioxidant in the formulation is a quality control indicator.

## How to Measure Your Omega-3 Index Before Supplementing

The Omega-3 Index can be measured with a dried blood spot test, available as a direct-to-consumer laboratory test in several countries. The standard protocol involves a fingertip prick, drying the sample on special filter paper, and gas chromatography analysis. The result arrives expressed as a percentage of EPA+DHA over total fatty acids in erythrocytes. Laboratories such as OmegaQuant in the United States or Vitas AS in Norway process this type of sample using the Harris–von Schacky method, which is the reference standard in the literature.

For most cyclists who have not supplemented regularly with fish oil for months, a starting Omega-3 Index below 6% is expected, as confirmed by Von Schacky et al. (2014) and the baseline values in trained cyclist studies. Reaching 8% from that starting point requires, according to available data, between 12 and 20 weeks of supplementation with 2–3 g/day of EPA+DHA in triglyceride form, taken with a fat-containing meal. Beginning supplementation eight weeks before a key competitive period — not the week before — is the difference between having or not having an erythrocyte index in the range where the physiological evidence places its effects.
