## The intestinal bottleneck: why 60 g/h was never enough

For decades, exercise physiology textbooks taught that the human body has an absorption ceiling of approximately 60 g of exogenous carbohydrates per hour, and that exceeding that figure was pointless —the surplus would ferment in the colon and cause distress. The mechanism has a precise name: the sodium-glucose co-transporter type 1 (SGLT1), located on the brush border of the small intestine, becomes saturated when glucose is ingested at a rate of ~1.0–1.1 g·min⁻¹. More glucose in the lumen does not translate into more glucose in the bloodstream; it simply accumulates in the gut and accelerates transit. The practical consequence was that in stages lasting four hours or more, muscle glycogen remained the dominant fuel and fatigue arrived like an inevitable wall.

What went largely unchallenged was the implicit assumption embedded in that ceiling: that all carbohydrate intake consisted of glucose or glucose polymers (maltodextrin). The intestine has more than one transporter, and that plurality changes the calculation entirely.

## Two pathways, twice the fuel: glucose, fructose and multiple transportable carbohydrates

Fructose uses a different transporter from glucose: GLUT5, which operates independently of SGLT1 and does not compete for the same absorption channel. When glucose and fructose are combined, both transporters work in parallel and the total carbohydrate flux into the bloodstream clearly exceeds the old 60 g/h limit. Jentjens and Jeukendrup demonstrated in *British Journal of Nutrition* (2005) that trained cyclists ingesting a glucose-fructose mixture at a rate of 2.4 g·min⁻¹ achieved peak exogenous oxidation rates of ~1.75 g·min⁻¹, compared with ~1.0 g·min⁻¹ for glucose alone (PMID: 15946410). That translates to moving from 60 g/h to over 100 g/h of carbohydrate effectively oxidized by the muscle.

The ratio that emerged from that research was 2:1 (glucose:fructose), enabling sustained oxidation of ~1.3 g·min⁻¹. More recent formulations with ratios of approximately 1:0.8 (maltodextrin:fructose) have shown similar or superior results in terms of oxidation and gastrointestinal comfort during prolonged exercise. The practical implication is straightforward: a drink containing only glucose limits the cyclist to 60 g/h; the same caloric load distributed between glucose and fructose can raise actual oxidation to 90–100 g/h without changing the total carbohydrate ingested.

## The gut learns: evidence for physiological adaptation

The most significant finding of the past decade was not the discovery of multiple transportable carbohydrates —that was known since the early 2000s— but the demonstration that the transporters themselves are plastic. Cox et al. published in *Journal of Applied Physiology* (2010) the first study to directly document this adaptation in endurance cyclists: athletes who trained with high carbohydrate availability showed a significant increase in exogenous glucose oxidation compared with the group that trained with low intake, with no differences in aerobic capacity or endogenous fat oxidation (PMID: 20466803). The most plausible mechanism is upregulation of SGLT1 expression in the intestinal epithelium: more transporter protein per unit of surface area, greater possible flux.

Jeukendrup systematized this concept in a review in *Sports Medicine* (2017) that established "gut training" as a deliberate nutritional strategy and distinguished three adaptation mechanisms: increased intestinal transport capacity —primarily via SGLT1—, reduction of gastrointestinal symptoms through habituation, and possible improvements in gastric emptying (PMID: 28332114). The review notes that most evidence in humans is indirect —measuring exogenous oxidation as a proxy for intestinal absorption— but is internally consistent with what is observed in animal models of dietary adaptation in the small intestine. The minimum stimulus required appears to be repeated exposure to carbohydrates during exercise at concentrations and volumes close to those used in racing, not simply a high-carbohydrate diet at rest.

## When the gut fails: exercise-induced gastrointestinal syndrome

Approximately 30–50% of endurance cyclists report gastrointestinal distress during competition, with prevalence exceeding 60% in events lasting more than five hours. Costa et al. published in *Alimentary Pharmacology & Therapeutics* (2017) a systematic review that defined "exercise-induced gastrointestinal syndrome" as a set of functional and structural disturbances of the GI tract triggered by intense or prolonged exertion (PMID: 28589631). The mechanisms are multiple: the redistribution of blood flow toward active muscle reduces splanchnic perfusion by up to 80% during maximal exercise, impairing intestinal barrier function and increasing epithelial permeability. The clinical consequence ranges from nausea and cramping to active malabsorption of ingested carbohydrates —feeding the colon instead of the muscle.

According to Costa et al., the threshold for significant disturbances lies at efforts of at least two hours at 60% VO₂max or above. In road cycling that encompasses virtually every mountain stage and all grand tours. High carbohydrate intake —particularly in concentrated gel form without sufficient fluid— can worsen this situation in an untrained gut, because luminal hyperosmolarity aggravates barrier damage and accelerates cecal emptying. The lesson is that ingesting more without having trained tolerance can be counterproductive.

## The 120 g/h frontier in the professional peloton

The jump from 90 to 120 g/h has concrete experimental support. Podlogar et al. compared in *European Journal of Applied Physiology* (2022) two protocols in eleven trained cyclists (VO₂peak 62.6 ± 7 mL·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹): 90 g/h with a fructose:maltodextrin ratio of 1:2, and 120 g/h with a ratio of 0.8:1, over three hours at 95% of the gas exchange threshold (PMID: 35951130). Exogenous oxidation was significantly higher in the high-dose condition: 1.51 ± 0.22 g·min⁻¹ versus 1.29 ± 0.16 g·min⁻¹. Endogenous oxidation did not differ between conditions, indicating that the additional benefit comes entirely from extra absorbed carbohydrate —not from differential glycogen sparing— and that the intestines of these trained athletes could handle the load without saturating.

Wilson reviewed in *Sports Medicine* (2026) the evidence surrounding the ≥100 g/h movement in the professional peloton and concluded that no clear experimental evidence exists that high carbohydrate intake improves performance in a single stage compared with 60–90 g/h, but that the multi-day race context is different: high intake during a stage facilitates overnight glycogen resynthesis and may sustain performance across weeks of competition (PMID: 41343040). Peloton practice has outpaced the science, driven by field data and physiological rationale, but still awaits confirmation in randomized controlled trials with professional cyclists.

## Gut training protocols: building tolerance week by week

The systematic review by Martinez et al. in *Sports Medicine* (2023) analyzed eight studies on gut training in endurance athletes and found that two weeks of repeated carbohydrate exposure during exercise reduced gut discomfort by an average of 47% and malabsorption by 45–54% in studies showing a positive response (PMID: 37061651). The most studied protocol consists of consuming carbohydrates at the target race rate during long training sessions at least four or five days per week for that minimum two-week period. Stimulus specificity matters: if gels plus an isotonic drink will be used in racing, that combination is what must be rehearsed in training; changing format or concentration on race day negates part of the adaptation achieved.

| Week | Target intake | Recommended format | Adaptation signal |
|------|--------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| 1 | 60–70 g/h | Isotonic drink + 1 gel/h | Mild tolerable discomfort |
| 2 | 75–85 g/h | Drink + 2 gels/h | Reduction in nausea |
| 3–4 | 90–100 g/h | Drink + gel + chew | Near-absent symptoms |
| 5+ | 100–120 g/h | Same, adding gel | Stable absorption |

The progression starts at 60–70 g/h with a glucose-fructose blend, increasing by 10–15 g/h each week based on subjective tolerance, reaching the race target at least two weeks before the main event. Liquid formats are better tolerated at the outset because greater fluid volume facilitates luminal iso-osmolarity. Jeukendrup (2017) notes that mild, transient discomfort during loading sessions is expected: it signals that the stimulus is acting, not a reason to reduce intake.

## What gut training cannot do

Not all cyclists respond at the same rate or with the same magnitude. Athletes with a history of irritable bowel syndrome or inflammatory bowel disease should approach gut training cautiously and ideally under medical supervision, given that intense exercise already increases epithelial permeability and can exacerbate symptoms in these cases. Nor is there evidence that 120 g/h is the right target for everyone: Wilson's review (2026) emphasizes that interindividual variability in exogenous oxidation is wide —some cyclists do not improve beyond 1.3 g·min⁻¹ even after weeks of gut training— and that personalization based on individual data outperforms any generic protocol.

Gut training does not replace classical physiological training: it raises the ceiling of available fuel, but that fuel is only useful if the engine can use it. In a five-hour stage with 4,000 m of climbing, the difference between absorbing 60 g/h and 100 g/h amounts to over 200 extra kcal available —enough to sustain power output in the final 30 minutes when glycogen is running low. In high-performance cycling, that is not a minor detail.
