## The Cyclist Who Arrived at Stage Three Still Standing

Morgan, Barton and Bowtell published in 2019 in the *European Journal of Applied Physiology* (PMID: 30617467) a trial with eight trained cyclists who completed a 15 km time trial after seven days of supplementation with 257 mg of Montmorency cherry anthocyanins per day in powder form. Times dropped 4.6% compared to placebo — from a mean of 1,580 to 1,506 seconds — and all eight participants improved without exception, though gains ranged from under 1% to nearly 9%. The mechanism the authors proposed wasn't primarily about reducing post-exercise soreness but about something more immediate: greater muscle oxygenation during the effort itself, consistent with a vasoactive effect of cherry polyphenols. For competitive cyclists where a 15 km time trial can decide overall standings, that number is hard to set aside.

The meta-analysis that best summarizes the state of the field is Kimble, Jones and Howatson (2023) in *Critical Reviews in Food Science and Nutrition* (PMID: 34402657), which analyzed 39 studies on the effect of anthocyanin-rich foods on post-exercise recovery. Results showed reduced creatine kinase at 48 hours (Hedges g = −0.31), increased antioxidant capacity immediately post-exercise (g = 0.56), strength improvements at all evaluated timepoints (g ranging from 0.45 to 0.67), lower delayed onset muscle soreness at 24 hours (g = −0.23), and better power output at both 24 and 48 hours post-exercise (g = 0.62 and 0.57, respectively). These are not enormous effect sizes, but they are consistent across nearly four decades of research and more than thirty controlled trials.

## What Anthocyanins Are and Why Montmorency Isn't Just Any Cherry

Anthocyanins are polyphenolic pigments from the flavonoid family responsible for the red, purple and blue colors in blueberries, blackberries, blackcurrants and sour cherries. Chemically, they are glycosides of anthocyanidins — the flavylium aglycone that becomes stable and bioavailable when bound to one or more sugars. Sour cherry (*Prunus cerasus*), and specifically the Montmorency variety cultivated primarily in Michigan and Ontario, concentrates the anthocyanins most studied in the sports context: cyanidin-3-glucoside and cyanidin-3-rutinoside, alongside chlorogenic acid, quercetin and melatonin in relevant proportions.

The advantage of Montmorency over other anthocyanin sources isn't solely about concentration — which ranges from approximately 26 to 40 mg of anthocyanins per 100 g of fresh fruit — but the body of clinical research specifically built around this variety over two decades. Blueberries, blackcurrants and açaí have equally promising anthocyanin profiles, but the vast majority of controlled trials in endurance cyclists use standardized Montmorency extract or juice concentrate as the intervention. Comparing studies across different anthocyanin sources introduces compositional variability that complicates any firm conclusions.

## The Mechanism: Oxidative Stress, Inflammation and Creatine Kinase as a Damage Marker

When a cyclist completes three consecutive days of high-intensity training — or a stage race with steep climbs every day — oxidative demand and mechanical muscle damage trigger an inflammatory cascade. Free radicals generated during intense exercise oxidize membrane lipids (lipid hydroperoxides, LOOH), damaged cells release creatine kinase (CK) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) into the bloodstream, and inflamed tissue produces cytokines including interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α). This response is necessary and useful — it signals the repair process and, in planned training blocks, is part of adaptation — but when it accumulates between stages without adequate recovery time, it becomes a brake on next-day performance.

Montmorency cherry anthocyanins intervene in this process through two primary pathways. The first is direct: their metabolites act as free radical scavengers, attenuating lipid peroxidation and reducing circulating LOOH levels. The second is more specific at the cellular level: cherry polyphenols inhibit COX-1 and COX-2 enzymes — the same targets as ibuprofen and aspirin, though in a much milder and more selective manner — and modulate the transcription factor NF-κB, which governs the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines. The practical outcome is lower IL-6, lower hsCRP and, consequently, faster functional recovery of damaged muscle.

## The Cycling Evidence: Four Trials With Verified PMIDs

Bell, Walshe, Davison, Stevenson and Howatson published in 2014 in *Nutrients* (PMID: 24566440) the first controlled trial specifically designed around multi-day cycling. Sixteen trained cyclists consumed 30 mL of Montmorency cherry concentrate twice daily for seven consecutive days. On days 5, 6 and 7, they completed a 109-minute stochastic cycling test designed to replicate road race demands. Results showed significantly lower responses across three key markers: lipid hydroperoxides (oxidative stress), IL-6 (inflammation) and high-sensitivity CRP (systemic inflammation), with reductions especially pronounced the day after the third bout of exercise. The authors concluded that cherry concentrate can "combat post-exercise oxidative and inflammatory cascades" that accumulate when high-effort days follow one another without adequate recovery.

A year later, the same group published in *Applied Physiology, Nutrition, and Metabolism* (PMID: 25794236) a second trial with 16 cyclists and a slightly different protocol — 30 mL twice daily for eight days, with the cycling trial on day five. The findings added a functional dimension the first study hadn't measured: maximum voluntary isometric contraction did not decline in the cherry group across the 72-hour post-trial period (where the placebo group showed significant decreases), and cycling economy improved in the supplemented group at 24 hours. IL-6 and hsCRP were again significantly lower in the cherry group. That the cherry maintained functional power output and mechanical efficiency the following day — not just moved biomarkers — is the number a professional cyclist cares about.

Morgan, Barton and Bowtell (2019, PMID: 30617467) took the next step: measuring direct performance rather than recovery markers. Their result — 4.6% improvement in a 15 km time trial with seven days of 257 mg anthocyanins in powder form — is the most cited finding in the field and also the one that warrants the most caution, because the sample was small (n = 8) and inter-individual variability high (improvement range: <1% to ~9%). The authors noted that increased muscle oxygenation during the effort suggests that a vasoactive mechanism — not just antioxidant action — may be contributing.

Wangdi, O'Leary, Kelly, Tang and Bowtell published in 2024 in the *European Journal of Sport Science* (PMID: 39213288) the most comprehensive timing trial to date in cycling. Twenty participants (10 trained, 10 recreationally active) each completed a 15 km time trial on four separate occasions: unsupplemented, and with an acute dose of Montmorency cherry concentrate at 30, 90 or 150 minutes pre-exercise. The 90-minute timing produced the greatest performance improvement — 2.83% or 48.3 seconds — and the highest levels of phenolic metabolites in plasma during the trial. Only the trained cyclists showed statistically significant improvement; recreationally active participants did not reach significance. The timing finding is directly actionable: if you're using cherry concentrate on race day, 90 minutes before the start appears to be the optimal window.

## The Protocol That Works: Dose, Duration and Format

Most of the evidence in cyclists uses cherry concentrate at 30 mL twice daily for a minimum of seven days, with the supplementation period beginning four or five days before the event or race block and extending two or three days afterward. Rojano Ortega and colleagues (2021) in *Biology of Sport* (PMID: 33795919) analyzed 25 studies and concluded that a minimum of 8-10 total days of supplementation is needed for consistent benefits on functional recovery — starting supplementation the night before a race does not replicate the results seen in the trials. A comparison of formats shows that juice concentrate, standardized powder capsules and freeze-dried fruit powder produce similar effects when matched for anthocyanin dose; fresh or pasteurized cherry juice from grocery stores does not contain a standardized or sufficient anthocyanin concentration to replicate these protocols.

| Format | Standard dose | Approximate equivalent | Typical protocol |
|--------|--------------|------------------------|-----------------|
| Concentrate (30 mL/serving) | 60 mL/day (2 servings) | ~50–60 cherries per serving | 4–5 days pre + race day + 2 days post |
| Encapsulated powder | 480–600 mg/day | Varies by brand | Same structure |
| Freeze-dried powder | 257–480 mg anthocyanins | Equivalent to concentrate | Minimum 7 days total |
| Commercial cherry juice (non-concentrate) | Not standardized | Variable | No verified protocol |

## The Elephant in the Room: Does It Block Adaptations?

The comparison with NSAIDs is the most contested point in the field. Ibuprofen and naproxen suppress prostaglandin synthesis via COX-2 inhibition, reducing post-exercise inflammation but potentially blunting the signals that drive muscle remodeling and protein synthesis upregulation. The concern with high-dose antioxidants — particularly vitamins C and E — is similar: Paulsen and colleagues (2014) in the *Journal of Physiology* demonstrated that supplementing 1,000 mg/day of vitamin C plus 235 mg/day of vitamin E for 11 weeks attenuated the endurance training-induced increase in mitochondrial proteins. The mechanism is that free radicals produced during exercise function as molecular signals required for mitochondrial biogenesis and vascular adaptation; suppressing them excessively blocks those signals.

Montmorency cherry anthocyanins, however, operate differently from vitamins C and E. They are not direct, non-specific free radical scavengers but rather modulators of specific signaling pathways — primarily NF-κB and COX-2 — without the sweeping ROS suppression that characterizes high-dose vitamin E. Jackman and colleagues (2018) in a trial with older men (PMID: 29890270) found that 60 mL/day of Montmorency cherry concentrate for two weeks did not affect myofibrillar protein synthesis following resistance exercise, though it did reduce the expression of proteins involved in the inflammatory response. Direct evidence of adaptation blunting in trained endurance cyclists with cherry Montmorency does not exist in the current literature — but neither does definitive evidence that it doesn't occur during longer training blocks.

The practical guidance that emerges from the literature is the following: during the competitive season — week-long stage races, spring classics, race blocks with consecutive high-effort days — Montmorency cherry has solid evidence for accelerating functional recovery and reducing accumulated inflammation. During preparation and adaptation blocks — high-altitude training camps of three weeks, pre-season with deliberate volume, strength-focus mesocycles — there are physiological arguments for not systematically suppressing post-exercise inflammation. This is not a restriction unique to cherry; it follows the same logic that leads elite coaches to limit NSAID use during adaptation blocks.

## When to Use It and When to Step Back

The cyclist profile that benefits most from the Montmorency cherry protocol is the one facing consecutive days of high intensity without sufficient recovery windows: racers in 5-to-9-day stage races, cyclists in two-week training camps with integrated competition, or athletes who must repeat maximal efforts within 24-48 hour windows. Howatson and colleagues (2010, PMID: 19883392) showed with marathon runners — 5 days of pre-supplementation, race day, and 48 hours post — that the same model applies across endurance sports and that the reduction in inflammatory markers and delayed onset muscle soreness has measurable functional consequences.

The context where cherry supplementation has less justification — or where prudence suggests pausing it — is during training blocks specifically designed to generate adaptation through controlled stress: altitude camps, VO₂max overshooting protocols, progressive strength loading. No evidence directly shows cherry blocks adaptations in trained cyclists, but the physiological coherence of the argument — and the parallel with vitamins C and E — warrants caution. Levers and colleagues (2016) in the *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition* (PMID: 27231439) documented that endurance athletes who used cherry powder for 10 days surrounding a competition finished a half-marathon 13% faster than the placebo group — but that study did not evaluate what would happen with chronic supplementation during a 6-to-8-week adaptation block.

| Context | Recommendation | Evidence in cycling |
|---------|---------------|---------------------|
| Stage race (5–9 days) | Use: 5 days pre + race duration | Bell 2014, Bell 2015 (direct) |
| Classics / single-day events | Use: 7 days pre + 90 min pre-event | Wangdi 2024 (timing), Morgan 2019 |
| Altitude training block | Caution: pause or limit to recovery days | No direct evidence; physiological rationale |
| Strength-focus mesocycle | Caution: may attenuate inflammatory signaling | No data in cyclists |
| Urgent recovery (≤48h between events) | Use: immediate post-event loading protocol | Bowtell 2011, Bell 2015 |

## What Montmorency Cherry Is Not

The evidence does not support using Montmorency cherry as a replacement for a complete recovery strategy. Sleep, carbohydrate availability in the post-exercise window, hydration and protein intake are the pillars that cherry supplementation complements, not substitutes. It is also not equivalent to NSAIDs in anti-inflammatory potency: ibuprofen reduces inflammatory markers with a pharmacological force that polyphenols do not reach — with its corresponding benefits and risks. And not all cherries are equal: the pasteurized commercial cherry juice sold in grocery stores contains a fraction of the anthocyanin concentration found in the standardized concentrate used in research; treating them interchangeably is a false economy that no study supports.

Montmorency cherry is, with the current data, the recovery supplement with the best combination of direct evidence in cyclists, mechanistic coherence and safety profile. Four controlled studies specific to cycling, a meta-analysis spanning 39 trials and functional outcomes measured under real cycling conditions place Montmorency anthocyanins at an evidence level that few supplements on the market can match. The question is no longer whether it works — it is whether a given cyclist's training calendar justifies using it now or saving it for the racing season.
