## The Peloton's Supplement That Doesn't Work Where You Think It Does

Brooks et al. published in 2022 in the *International Journal of Sport Nutrition and Exercise Metabolism* a meta-analysis of 8 randomized controlled trials involving 80 athletes that evaluated the acute effect of ketone esters on endurance performance (PMID: 35042186). The effect size was Hedges g = 0.136, with a confidence interval that includes zero (95% CI: −0.195, 0.467; p = .419). In practical terms: acute ingestion of ketone esters does not improve performance in endurance tests, whether time trials or time-to-exhaustion protocols. The fact that Visma|Lease-a-Bike and INEOS Grenadiers continue using ketones at the Tour de France does not contradict this finding, because their use is not aimed at in-stage performance.

## Ester or Salt: The Distinction That Defines All the Evidence

β-hydroxybutyrate (BHB) is the ketone body the body produces during prolonged fasting or severe carbohydrate restriction. Commercial formulations for athletes fall into two categories with very different absorption profiles and efficacy. Ketone salts bind BHB with minerals such as sodium, potassium, or calcium: they are inexpensive and widely available, but only raise plasma BHB to 0.5–1 mmol/L, a range insufficient for the physiological effects documented in performance research. Ketone esters — specifically the compound (R)-3-hydroxybutyl (R)-3-hydroxybutyrate, marketed under names such as HVMN and KetoneIQ — raise BHB to 3–5 mmol/L within 30 minutes at a 65-gram dose. All relevant research on cycling performance uses esters. Salts do not reproduce those outcomes.

## The 2016 Paper That Started Everything

Cox et al. published in 2016 in *Cell Metabolism* (PMID: 27475046) five experiments with 39 high-performance athletes in which co-ingestion of ketone ester and carbohydrates improved 30-minute time trial performance by approximately 2% compared to carbohydrates alone. The proposed mechanism: nutritional ketosis shifts metabolism toward BHB oxidation, reducing muscle glycogen breakdown and plasma lactate levels. The work, from Kieran Clarke's laboratory at the University of Oxford, generated enormous interest among WorldTour teams and marked the beginning of a race to secure the ketone ester as a potential secret weapon for performance.

One year later, Leckey et al. (PMID: 29109686) replicated the experiment with 10 professional cyclists under conditions simulating the final 31 kilometers of the 2017 World Road Cycling Championships. Using the same class of compound at a dose of 250 mg/kg body mass, performance deteriorated by 2% (p < 0.05), accompanied by significant gastrointestinal discomfort and elevated perceived exertion. The divergence between two rigorous studies using the same type of supplement opened a debate that remains unresolved.

## Mild Ketoacidosis: The Mechanism Behind the Contradiction

Poffé et al. described in 2020 the most plausible mechanism for the conflicting results (PMID: 32407242). Ingesting 65 grams of ketone ester reduces blood pH to ~7.35 and depletes plasma bicarbonate during the first hour of exercise. This transient acidosis compromises exactly the buffering system the muscle needs to sustain high-intensity efforts. The same group published in 2021 (PMID: 32735112) that co-ingesting sodium bicarbonate (300 mg/kg) with the ketone ester neutralized that acidosis and produced performance improvements in the final 15 minutes of a simulated three-hour cycling race. An independent trial that same year with 12 cyclists (PMID: 33196605) found, however, that exogenous ketosis still impaired a 30-minute time trial even with additional bicarbonate, suggesting that acidosis is not the only factor at play.

## Where the Signal Appears: Post-Exercise Recovery

The picture changes when the focus shifts from in-race performance to recovery. Holdsworth et al. (PMID: 28398950) demonstrated in 2017 in *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise* that co-ingestion of ketone ester with glucose after exercise increases muscle glycogen synthesis by 50% compared to control (246 vs. 164 mmol/kg dry weight; p < 0.05). The mechanism: BHB doubles endogenous insulin, accelerating glucose uptake and glycogen resynthesis independently of the caloric contribution of the ketone. In parallel, Vandoorne et al. (PMID: 28588499) documented that ketone ester during recovery activates mTORC1 signaling, the primary anabolic pathway for muscle protein synthesis, without additional protein intake.

The most relevant finding for competitive cycling came with Poffé et al. in 2019 (PMID: 31039280). Eighteen trained cyclists completed three weeks of deliberate overload — six days per week, two sessions per day — designed to induce non-functional overreaching. The group that received 25 grams of ketone ester immediately after each session sustained a training load 15% greater in week three (p < 0.05) and maintained power output in control performance tests. The placebo group, meanwhile, developed the classic markers of overreaching: reduced maximum heart rate, elevated nocturnal catecholamine excretion, and a spontaneously negative energy balance of −2,135 kJ/day compared to +198 kJ/day in the ketone group.

## EPO and New Blood Vessels: The 2023 Finding

Four years after the overreaching study, Poffé et al. published in *Journal of Physiology* (PMID: 37062892) the effects of ketone ester on vascular adaptation during high-load training blocks. Cyclists in the supplemented group developed 40% more muscle capillaries than the control group over the same training protocol, an increase accompanied by elevations in circulating erythropoietin (EPO) and the pro-angiogenic factors VEGF and eNOS in skeletal muscle. The finding repositions exogenous ketones beyond the energy substrate paradigm: ketone bodies act as molecular signals that amplify training adaptation, not merely as an alternative fuel.

## Why Visma and INEOS Use Them Anyway

The rationale behind WorldTour teams using ketones is not about producing more watts on a given stage. The goal is arriving at the third week of the Tour de France less deteriorated than the opposing peloton. A ketone ester taken immediately after a Pyrenean stage, combined with carbohydrate recovery (1–1.2 g/kg in the first hour), accelerates glycogen resynthesis and reduces muscle damage markers for the following day's stage. Across a three-week block where every recovery night counts, that cumulative difference may prove more decisive than any marginal power improvement during the stage itself.

Teams like Visma|Lease-a-Bike have described this protocol publicly: ketone post-stage, not pre-stage. INEOS Grenadiers uses a similar approach and cites Poffé 2019 as the foundation of their recovery program. The distinction is crucial for correctly interpreting the available literature: acute performance trials in a laboratory and adaptation studies in training blocks are scientifically distinct questions about the same compound.

## Adverse Effects, Cost, and WADA Status

Gastrointestinal effects are the most consistent obstacle in clinical trials with ketone esters. At doses of 65 grams, the majority of studies report nausea, epigastric discomfort, and an intensely bitter taste that many participants describe as intolerable. Dearlove et al. (PMID: 32868580) documented that exogenous BHB oxidation drops sharply above 70% of VO₂max: at high intensity, muscle prioritizes glucose and barely uses available BHB, explaining why the benefit during time trial efforts is limited while adverse effects persist. Cost represents the second barrier: a 65-gram dose of KetoneIQ costs approximately $30 on the US market. In a Grand Tour with a daily post-stage protocol over 21 stages, the per-rider budget exceeds $600 in ketones alone. The WADA has not included ketone esters or BHB salts on its prohibited substances list and there is no indication that will change in the near term.

| Scenario | Available evidence | Documented protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Time trial / acute performance | Mostly null or negative | Not recommended as a standalone intervention |
| Post-stage recovery with CHO | Moderate: +50% glycogen (Holdsworth 2017) | 25–65 g KE + 1 g/kg CHO immediately post-exercise |
| Overload training block ≥3 weeks | Moderate: 15% greater sustained load (Poffé 2019) | 25 g KE post-session throughout the block |
| Long-term vascular adaptation | Preliminary: +40% capillarization (Poffé 2023) | Ester only, not salts; research ongoing |
| Ketone salts (any scenario) | No evidence of efficacy | No evidence-based recommendation |

Exogenous ketones are not the high-performance ergogenic that 2016 headlines promised. They are a recovery and training adaptation modulator with concrete effects in specific contexts, a real cost, and low gastrointestinal tolerance in a relevant proportion of users. Understanding that distinction is what separates rational use from marketing.
