## The intensity that doesn't feel like training

When I left the professional peloton and started coaching amateur cyclists, the most common complaint was always the same: "I get bored riding this easy." It's understandable. Pedaling at 170 watts when your FTP sits at 260 feels like wasted time. Yet that intensity band — between 55 and 75% of FTP, what exercise physiology calls zone 2 — is precisely where the adaptations that determine your ultimate performance ceiling take place. This is not a coaching fad or a recent trend. It's basic biochemistry, backed by decades of evidence.

Physiologist Iñigo San Millán, researcher at the University of Colorado and the man behind Tadej Pogačar's performance program, has repeated it in every conference and publication: zone 2 is the intensity that most stimulates mitochondrial function, fat oxidation, and lactate clearance. Pogačar devotes roughly 80% of his training days to this zone. Not because it's easy to prescribe, but because laboratory data show that this is where the engine gets built.

## What happens inside the muscle cell

Understanding why zone 2 works requires looking inside the type I muscle fiber. These slow-twitch fibers are the protagonists of sustained aerobic effort and contain the highest mitochondrial density in skeletal muscle. Mitochondria are the structures where both fats and lactate are oxidized to produce energy. When we ride in zone 2, we preferentially recruit these fibers and subject them to a metabolic stimulus that promotes their proliferation and efficiency.

San Millán and George Brooks published a study in *Sports Medicine* (2018) comparing metabolic responses to exercise in professional endurance athletes, moderately active individuals, and people with metabolic syndrome. Elite athletes showed substantially greater fat oxidation rates and significantly lower blood lactate concentrations during submaximal exercise. The correlation was inverse and consistent: the greater the mitochondrial capacity, the higher the fat oxidation and the lower the lactate accumulation. Measuring lactate during exercise, they concluded, amounts to an indirect measurement of mitochondrial function.

Type I fibers possess a transporter called MCT-1 that captures lactate produced by fast-twitch fibers and shuttles it to mitochondria, where it is reused as an energy substrate. This clearance mechanism is what allows blood lactate levels to stay below 2 mmol/L during zone 2 exercise. San Millán has noted that one of the first things he observed when testing Pogačar was his exceptionally low lactate values relative to other riders in the peloton — a direct signal of his extraordinary mitochondrial machinery.

## The aerobic base is not an abstract concept

When coaches talk about "building the base," they're referring to a concrete set of physiological adaptations: increased mitochondrial density, proliferation of capillaries around muscle fibers, greater efficiency in fat utilization as fuel, and improved lactate recycling capacity. These adaptations don't happen explosively with high-intensity intervals. They're built through sustained volume at moderate intensities, accumulated over weeks and months.

John Holloszy demonstrated as early as 1967 that endurance training could double oxidative enzyme activity and mitochondrial capacity in skeletal muscle. That foundational finding still holds. What modern research has refined is the understanding of which intensity maximizes these adaptations in the context of cycling performance. Zone 2 emerges as the sweet spot because it generates sufficient metabolic demand to stimulate mitochondrial biogenesis without depleting glycogen reserves or producing fatigue that compromises the ability to train the following day.

Mølmen, Almquist, and Skattebo published in *Sports Medicine* (2025) a systematic review with meta-regression comparing the effects of different training types on mitochondrial content and muscle capillarization. Percentage increases in mitochondrial content were similar across continuous endurance training (23%), high-intensity intervals (27%), and sprint intervals (27%). However, context matters: sprint intervals were 3.9 times more time-efficient per exercise hour, but continuous training produced more consistent capillary adaptations and allowed a much greater total training volume without overtraining risk.

## The gray zone: amateur cycling's most expensive mistake

The most common problem I see in recreational cyclists isn't that they train too little — it's that they train at the wrong intensity. Stephen Seiler coined the term "black hole training" to describe the phenomenon: amateurs tend to ride too hard on easy days and not hard enough on hard days. The result is an accumulation of hours in what Seiler calls the gray zone — an intensity between the aerobic and anaerobic thresholds that produces considerable fatigue without proportional adaptations.

Seiler published in the *International Journal of Sports Physiology and Performance* (2010) that successful endurance athletes consistently devoted about 80% of their sessions to low intensities, with blood lactate below 2 mmol/L. Only the remaining 20% concentrated on high-intensity work. The middle zone appeared at minimal frequencies. This distribution was no accident: it reflected a strategy that allowed accumulating high volume without compromising recovery, maximizing the aerobic stimulus of easy sessions and the central stimulus of intense ones.

I remember my early years as a professional, when I arrived at a continental team and the directeur sportif told me something it took months to accept: "If you can talk to your teammate without effort, you're fine. If not, slow down." I'd been training alone, at race pace every ride. When I started respecting the zones and genuinely riding easy for four or five hours, my FTP climbed 18 watts in three months without doing a single interval more than the previous season.

## How to know if you're in zone 2

Defining zone 2 precisely requires understanding which zone system is being used, and here lies a frequent source of confusion. In the five-zone system based on FTP (the most widely used in cycling with power meters), zone 2 corresponds to 55-75% of FTP. In Seiler's three-zone physiological system, it equates to zone 1, below the first lactate threshold. San Millán defines it as the intensity where blood lactate remains stable around 1.5-2.0 mmol/L.

In practical terms, if you don't have access to a lactate test, zone 2 meets several simultaneous criteria: you can hold a full conversation without gasping, breathing is nasal or very gentle through the mouth, heart rate sits between 60 and 75% of your maximum, and perceived exertion is between 2 and 3 on a scale of 10. If you're on the trainer with a power meter, the reference is clear: between 55 and 75% of your FTP. The temptation to push to 78 or 80% "because the difference is barely noticeable" is exactly the mechanism that drags you into the gray zone.

## Weekly plan: zone 2 for three levels

Implementation varies by available time and experience level. San Millán recommends a minimum of three weekly zone 2 sessions to stimulate and improve mitochondrial function, noting that one or two sessions may be insufficient to generate meaningful adaptations.

## Signs that zone 2 is working

Zone 2 adaptations aren't immediate, but they are measurable. After six to eight weeks of consistent work, a cyclist should observe several indicators: heart rate for the same zone 2 power drops by 3 to 8 beats per minute, the power sustainable with nasal breathing increases, and perceived exertion at the same load decreases. In a lactate test, values at the same wattage decline, confirming that mitochondria are processing substrate more efficiently.

There are subtler signs that also matter. Recovery between sessions improves. Sleep stabilizes. Resting heart rate variability tends to increase. And there's an indicator rarely mentioned in textbooks but recognized by any experienced cyclist: you start feeling that the first two hours of a long ride "cost nothing." That sensation of the body finding a sustainable rhythm without conscious effort is the practical manifestation of a solid aerobic base.

## The counterargument: is zone 2 really what you need?

Not all of the scientific community agrees that zone 2 is the optimal intensity for maximizing mitochondrial adaptations. Storoschuk and colleagues published in *Sports Medicine* (2025) a narrative review questioning the generalized recommendations. Their central argument is that the evidence supporting zone 2 comes primarily from observations of elite athletes who already possess exceptional mitochondrial capacity, and that for the general population with low training volumes, higher intensities might be more efficient.

The nuance matters. For a cyclist who can only train four or five hours per week, incorporating two or three high-intensity interval sessions will likely generate faster improvements in VO2max and lactate threshold than devoting all that time to zone 2. But for someone who can accumulate eight, ten, or fifteen hours weekly, zone 2 serves a function that high intensity cannot replicate: it allows accumulating aerobic training volume without excessive fatigue, protects against overtraining, and builds the capillary and mitochondrial foundation upon which intense intervals produce their best results.

The answer isn't zone 2 or high intensity. It's zone 2 and high intensity, in the right proportions. San Millán recommends three to four days of zone 2 per week, complemented by two intense sessions. Seiler documents that this 80/20 distribution is the one followed by the world's most successful endurance athletes. Zone 2 doesn't replace intervals. It gives them a foundation on which to deliver results.

## Common mistakes and how to avoid them

Mistake number one is going too hard. It sounds obvious, but it's endemic. When you hit a rise on what should be an easy ride and decide to "push a little" to keep your pace, you've just left zone 2. When a group catches you and you raise the intensity to sit in, your aerobic session has become something else entirely. The discipline to maintain zone 2 is, paradoxically, harder than doing flat-out intervals.

The second mistake is not accumulating enough duration. Sessions of 30 or 40 minutes in zone 2 have limited effect for cyclists with some base fitness. Capillary and mitochondrial adaptations respond to sustained time under aerobic load. For intermediate cyclists, one hour should be the minimum per session, with the weekend long ride as the centerpiece of the week. For advanced riders, three- to four-hour zone 2 rides are where most base building happens.

The third mistake is abandoning zone 2 once race season begins. The aerobic base is not a tank that fills in winter and lasts all year. It requires constant maintenance. Even during competition periods, zone 2 sessions between races serve a dual purpose: they facilitate recovery and maintain the aerobic stimulus that sustains performance.

## What zone 2 can't do alone

It would be dishonest to present zone 2 as the complete solution. It isn't. A cyclist who only trains in zone 2 will improve aerobic efficiency but will see VO2max, anaerobic threshold power, and the ability to respond to attacks or pace changes plateau. Stöggl and Sperlich demonstrated in 2014 that the polarized model — combining high volume at low intensity with high-intensity blocks — produced superior improvements in VO2max and peak power compared to models based exclusively on moderate intensity.

Zone 2 is the foundation, not the building. It constructs the mitochondrial machinery, capillary network, and metabolic efficiency that allow you to tolerate and benefit from intense training. Without that base, VO2max intervals generate cumulative fatigue without producing complete adaptations. With it, every high-intensity session yields more because the body recovers faster and processes lactate more efficiently.

San Millán sums it up with an analogy I constantly use with my cyclists: zone 2 is like building a bigger engine. High-intensity intervals are the turbo. A turbo on a small engine doesn't produce great results. But a turbo on an engine that already has plenty of displacement transforms performance. That is what the aerobic base does: it gives you the engine on which everything else works better.
