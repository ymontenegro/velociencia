## The problem with the intervals everyone does

A block of 4×5 minutes at Z4-Z5 (91–120% FTP) leaves a cyclist exhausted, convinced they've done something great. The physiological reality is less flattering: in the first 60–90 seconds of each effort, oxygen consumption is still climbing from submaximal values. Only in the final two minutes of each repetition does the cardiorespiratory system actually operate near its ceiling, accumulating perhaps 8–10 minutes of total time at VO2max in a session that lasted 50 minutes of real work. There is an alternative that, with the same training load, produces superior adaptations.

Bent Rønnestad, researcher at the Inland Norway University of Applied Sciences, published data in 2020 in the *Scandinavian Journal of Medicine & Science in Sports* that changed the conversation about cycling intervals. Three weeks of short intervals — 13 repetitions of 30 seconds with 15-second recovery, in three blocks — improved maximal aerobic power by 3.7% versus -0.3% in the long-interval group, and performance on a 20-minute all-out test by 4.7% versus -1.4%. Power at lactate threshold (4 mmol/L) improved 2.0% in the short-interval group versus a decline of 2.8% in the long-interval group. Both groups trained exactly the same volume and total intensity.

## The physics of oxygen during recovery

Véronique Billat was the first to document the mechanism behind these results. In 2000, her team published in the *European Journal of Applied Physiology* (PMID 10638376) that runners could spend far more time at VO2max values using 30-second repetitions at VO2max velocity alternated with 30-second recoveries at 50% of that speed, compared to continuous effort. The key finding: subjects accumulated an average of 7 minutes and 51 seconds at VO2max across 19 repetitions. That is physiologically impossible with a continuous effort at the same power output.

The mechanism is counterintuitive but solid. When a cyclist stops the hard 30-second effort, blood lactate begins to fall because active muscle reduces its production rate. But oxygen consumption — VO2 — doesn't drop at the same speed. Cardiorespiratory kinetics are slow: there is an inertia that keeps oxygen consumption elevated for the first 20–25 seconds of the easy interval. That overlap, repeated 13 times per set, is the mechanical secret of 30/30 intervals.

Rønnestad and Hansen quantified this effect specifically in elite cyclists in a 2016 study in the *Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research* (PMID 23942167). They compared protocols with 30-second work intervals, 50% of Tmax, and 80% of Tmax, all at maximal aerobic power (MAP). The 30-second intervals produced more time ≥90% VO2peak and greater total work duration at MAP than the longer intervals. The authors' conclusion was straightforward: 30-second intervals are the optimal time unit for maximizing time at VO2max when training at that intensity.

## The specific protocols and their zones

The core protocol developed by Rønnestad has a well-defined structure: 3 sets of 13 repetitions of 30 seconds at Z6 (≥120% FTP) interspersed with 15 seconds of active recovery at Z1 (&lt;55% FTP), with 3 minutes of rest between sets. This format allows a cyclist to accumulate 9 minutes and 45 seconds of high-intensity work in a session that, with warm-up and cool-down, stays under 75 minutes. The power target during the 30-second efforts corresponds to individual MAP, which in most well-trained cyclists falls between 120% and 135% of FTP.

There is a more accessible variant for those new to this format: 3 sets of 9 repetitions with the same intensity distribution. Rønnestad uses this as the entry point before scaling to 13 repetitions. The between-set recovery interval can be extended to 4–5 minutes during the first weeks without compromising the stimulus, as long as Z6 intensity is maintained during efforts. Dropping to Z5 (106–120% FTP) to complete more repetitions is a common mistake: it reduces actual time at VO2max and turns the session into something closer to a prolonged sweet-spot effort.

Billat's original 30/30 format — 30 seconds at 100% of VO2max power and 30 seconds at 50% — uses longer recovery intervals than Rønnestad's (30 s vs 15 s), which allows genuinely harder efforts during the work phases. This format is useful when the goal is to also stimulate the glycolytic pathway and peak anaerobic power. For pure VO2max development and maximal aerobic capacity, Rønnestad's 15-second recovery variant accumulates more total time in the critical zone per session.

## Weekly progression: six weeks of VO2max construction

The most common mistake when starting 30/30 intervals is overestimating initial fitness. A cyclist who has never done this format should begin with 3 sets of 9 repetitions in week one, even if their VO2max is high. The reason is neuromuscular: sustaining Z6 power across 13 consecutive repetitions requires a specific recruitment pattern that the body learns over two or three sessions. Attempting 13 repetitions from day one typically results in power dropping to Z5 by the eighth repeat, emptying the protocol of its primary effect.

The maximum recommended frequency is two sessions per week of 30/30 intervals, never on consecutive days. Between each interval session there should be at least 36 hours of recovery or Z1–Z2 work (55–75% FTP). This VO2max development block should be positioned 4–8 weeks before the main competitive target, with a deload week at the end.

## 30/30 vs Norwegian 4×4 vs classic 5×5: which to choose

Rønnestad's research directly compared his short interval protocols with effort-matched long-interval formats (4–5 min). The 2015 study (PMID 24382021) showed that 10 weeks with two weekly sessions produced an 8.7% VO2max improvement for the short-interval group versus 2.6% for the long-interval group. The 2021 follow-up (PMID 33735833) found that even a one-week shock microcycle of five short-interval sessions improved VO2max by an additional 5.7% compared to marginal gains in the long-interval group. The evidence consistently points in one direction.

That doesn't mean Norwegian 4×4 or 5×5 long intervals are obsolete. Long intervals are superior for developing efficiency at lactate threshold and training the body to sustain high power over extended periods — exactly what happens in a time trial or a long breakaway. The 30/30 format trains the central aerobic machinery (heart, lungs, mitochondria) with great efficiency; 4–5 minute intervals also train the capacity to buffer lactate at sustained high intensity. A cyclist targeting short climbs and 30–60 minute events gains more from 30/30 work; one racing over 2–4 hours needs a combination of both formats.

The classic 5×5 at Z4 (91–105% FTP) is the least efficient of the three for VO2max stimulus. It works well for cyclists in late base phases looking to raise functional threshold before incorporating more specific work, but it should not be confused with a VO2max session: the power is too low for oxygen consumption to reach and sustain maximal values throughout the effort.

## When to schedule them and how often

The optimal window for a 30/30 block is 4–8 weeks before the competitive peak. Before that window, the aerobic base needs to be in place: at least 8–12 weeks of Z1–Z2 dominance (below 75% FTP) and some Z3–Z4 sessions to prime the system. Launching a 30/30 block on an insufficient aerobic base generates fatigue without the corresponding adaptation, because muscle mitochondria aren't yet positioned to respond to the stimulus with effective protein synthesis.

The maximum dosage studied in the literature is two sessions per week for four to six weeks. Three weekly sessions produce diminishing returns and increase overtraining risk — the autonomic nervous system doesn't fully recover between sessions, which shows up as elevated morning heart rate and power drops during efforts despite high perceived exertion. The warning signs are clear: if power in the fourth set drops more than 10% from the first with identical RPE, the athlete is accumulating fatigue that isn't dissipating.

## Common mistakes that neutralize the protocol

The most documented error is going out too hard in the first intervals. The temptation to hit 150% FTP in the opening four or five efforts leads to an abrupt drop where the final repetitions of each set are completed at Z4 or even Z3, undermining the protocol's purpose. The target power must be achievable on the tenth repetition of the third set, not just the first repetition of the first. Rønnestad recommends starting at the lower end of the Z6 range and adjusting upward only after completing two consecutive sessions without power decay.

Low cadence is another frequent problem. Pedaling at 70–75 rpm during the 30-second efforts increases the maximal strength component and reduces the time the heart can work at maximal cardiac output, shifting the stimulus from the central cardiovascular system toward peripheral musculature. The effective cadence range for maximizing time at VO2max during 30/30 intervals sits between 90 and 110 rpm, depending on the cyclist's profile. Finally, shortening the warm-up due to time constraints raises injury risk and reduces performance in the early sets: a minimum of 15 minutes in Z1–Z2 with one or two short activation sprints is part of the protocol, not optional.

## The marker that confirms it's working

After four well-executed weeks, a cyclist should be able to sustain a power output 3–6% higher on 5-minute all-out tests. The peak heart rate reached in the final intervals of each set should creep slightly upward between week one and week four — a sign that cardiac output is improving. If neither signal appears, the cause is almost always the same: effort intensity didn't consistently reach Z6, or Z1–Z2 volume in the rest of the week was insufficient to support recovery. The 30/30 format is unforgiving of poor execution — but done correctly, it is arguably the most efficient VO2max stimulus in cycling training.
