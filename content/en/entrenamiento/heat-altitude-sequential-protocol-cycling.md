## The "Two Is Better Than One" Trap

Heat and altitude are the two most potent environmental training stimuli in endurance cycling. Both increase oxygen-carrying capacity — though through distinct mechanisms — and both have solid evidence supporting time-trial performance improvements. The logical conclusion that combining them produces greater adaptations than either alone took hold in professional teams and amateur programs before the science could test it. That assumption turns out to be wrong.

In 2017, Erin McCleave and colleagues from Western Sydney University published the first controlled study to challenge this logic in *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise* (DOI: 10.1249/MSS.0000000000001138). Twenty-six well-trained runners trained for three weeks under one of three conditions: heat only (33 °C, below 600 m), heat combined with *Live High-Train Low* (LHTL) hypoxia at a simulated 3,000 meters for 13 hours per day, or temperate control conditions. A 3-km time trial in temperate conditions improved by 3.3% in the heat-only group at three weeks post-exposure. In the group that combined heat with LHTL: no statistically significant improvement over control. Two years later, the same group showed in the *International Journal of Sports Physiology and Performance* (PMID: 30427243) that the simultaneous combination not only failed to outperform isolated heat training but actually blunted the thermoregulatory adaptations that heat alone produced.

## Why the Mechanisms Interfere

The conflict is not accidental — it arises from the basic physiology of each adaptation. Heat acclimation depends on plasma volume expansion to function. That increase in plasma — occurring within the first five days of exposure and reaching 6–10% — activates renal oxygen sensors, which interpret the resulting hemodilution as a drop in renal oxygen tension and trigger erythropoietin synthesis. Heat uses plasma volume expansion as the signal to build more hemoglobin over time.

Altitude exposure produces the opposite effect. During the first days at elevation, the body reduces plasma volume through hemoconcentration: the kidneys excrete more fluid, and hematocrit rises acutely. That transient increase helps maintain arterial oxygen saturation with less circulating hemoglobin, but it destroys exactly the signal heat requires to trigger its erythropoietic cascade. Girard, Peeling, Racinais and Périard put it plainly in their 2024 review in the *International Journal of Sports Physiology and Performance* (DOI: 10.1123/ijspp.2023-0250): when both stimuli are applied simultaneously — or concurrently, training in heat and sleeping at altitude — improvements in temperate sea-level performance are generally similar in magnitude to those from heat or altitude training alone. There is no additive effect. For thermoregulatory adaptations specifically, the result may be inferior to heat alone.

## What Each Stimulus Does in Isolation

Altitude acts primarily on total haemoglobin mass (tHbmass): the hypoxic stimulus activates the HIF-1α pathway, raises circulating EPO within hours, and after three to four weeks of exposure at 2,000–2,500 metres produces tHbmass increases of 3.5–5%. Cubel, Klaris, Larsen, Faiss, Nybo and Lundby documented in *Experimental Physiology* (DOI: 10.1113/EP092968) that 12 elite cyclists who completed a 21-day LHTH camp at 3,000 metres increased their tHbmass significantly — but with a critical limitation: upon descent, the gained hemoglobin was lost quickly, and there was no effect on sea-level time-trial performance.

Heat training operates in two distinct phases. The first (days 1–5) expands plasma volume and improves sudomotor and cardiovascular adaptations. With longer protocols, heat also increases tHbmass. Rønnestad and colleagues showed in *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise* in 2022 (PMID: 35394464) that five weeks of heat suit training in elite cyclists raised tHbmass by 3% and maintained temperate time-trial performance. Critically, heat provides what altitude cannot: genuine thermoregulatory adaptations — earlier sweat onset, lower core temperature during exercise, reduced submaximal heart rate in hot conditions — that have direct competitive value in the hot stages of July and August grand tours.

## The Case for Heat First, Altitude After

The heat → altitude sequence has coherent physiological rationale, though no controlled trial has directly compared it head-to-head against the reverse order. Nybo, Rønnestad and Lundby proposed it in their 2024 review in the *Scandinavian Journal of Medicine & Science in Sports* (PMID: 36350277): a prior heat acclimation block expands plasma volume and primes the hematopoietic system before ascending. When the cyclist climbs, the hemoconcentration induced by altitude has to contract an already-expanded plasma volume — a larger buffer before the erythropoietic signal is extinguished. In theory, this could support a more robust EPO response during the early days of the altitude camp.

The practical limitation is that most of the plasma volume expansion induced by heat disappears within the first week at altitude as the kidneys recalibrate fluid balance. What the heat block does preserve are the thermoregulatory adaptations, which decay more slowly. For a cyclist preparing the Tour de France — where transition stages through Provence can exceed 38 °C on the road surface — those thermoregulatory adaptations offer competitive value that altitude cannot replicate. The practical protocol: 10–14 days heat acclimation (trainer without fan, 38–40 °C, 60–90 minutes at Z2) → 21–28 days LHTL camp (2,000–2,500 m) → return to sea level 5–7 days before racing.

## The Case for Altitude First, Heat to Preserve the Gains

The strongest empirical evidence supports a different sequence: altitude first, heat as a maintenance tool at sea level. The study by Rønnestad and colleagues published in *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise* in 2025 (PMID: 39160765, DOI: 10.1249/MSS.0000000000003542) is the clearest demonstration of this mechanism. Elite cyclists with a VO₂max of 76 ± 5 mL·min⁻¹·kg⁻¹ completed a three-week altitude camp around 2,100 metres. At the end, tHbmass had increased by 4.1% relative to baseline.

The classic problem on return is that the gains dissipate. Without any additional intervention, the body reduces red blood cell production because the hypoxic stimulus has disappeared. Rønnestad split cyclists into two groups upon descent: a control group continued normal training for 3.5 weeks; a heat group incorporated three weekly heat suit sessions into their normal routine. Results were unambiguous: the heat group maintained tHbmass essentially intact (+0.2%), while the control group lost 3.3 percentage points of their gains. The heat group also registered a plasma volume increase of 11.6% and a total blood volume increase of 5.8%.

The mechanism is straightforward: heat generates a transient hemodilution signal at sea level that mimics a reduction in renal oxygen tension, keeping erythropoiesis active enough to offset the natural loss of red blood cells when hypoxic drive has been removed. Without that signal, the body sees no reason to maintain a tHbmass above its habitual set point.

## Additive or Redundant?

The answer depends on sequence and objective. Applied simultaneously, the McCleave data are unambiguous: no additive effect. Applied sequentially, the combination can offer advantages neither stimulus provides alone — altitude builds hemoglobin more powerfully than heat alone, and post-altitude heat preserves it while adding the thermoregulatory adaptations altitude does not produce. Lundby and Robach noted in their 2025 review in *The Journal of Physiology* (PMID: 41139221) that heat training could serve as an alternative to altitude for athletes without mountain access, or as a sequential complement — but they emphasized the enormous individual variability in hypoxic response: the same athlete can increase tHbmass by 5% in one camp and barely 1% in the next with similar protocols, while heat produces more predictable responses.

## Minimum Effective Dose and Practical Protocol

For the heat block, the evidence converges on 9–14 consecutive sessions as the minimum to produce meaningful hematological and cardiovascular adaptations. Each session should last 60–90 minutes at Z2 intensity (55–75% FTP) in an environment of 38–42 °C — achievable on a trainer in a closed room wearing extra layers or a heat suit. Post-altitude maintenance sessions — three per week at 45–60 minutes in Z2, as in Rønnestad's 2025 protocol — are sufficient to retain the gains without adding excessive physiological load during the taper phase.

For the altitude block, requirements are stricter: a minimum of 21–28 days in the 2,000–2,500 metre range, with more than 14 hours of daily hypoxic exposure. The LHTL model — living at altitude and descending to train at intensity — remains the best-supported approach for maximising training quality without sacrificing the erythropoietic stimulus. Altitudes above 2,700–2,800 metres increase physiological stress, degrade sleep and reduce training quality without necessarily producing more hemoglobin.

## Risks That Don't Show Up in Lab Protocols

Chronic dehydration is the most underestimated risk when chaining both blocks. Both stimuli increase fluid losses — heat through elevated sweating, altitude through faster breathing in a dry environment — and a sustained hydration deficit reduces the very plasma volume both protocols aim to build. Fluid intake during and after every heat session is not optional.

Iron depletion is specific to the altitude block. Accelerated erythropoiesis consumes iron at a rate that can exceed normal dietary absorption, particularly in athletes with already-depleted stores. Starting a camp with ferritin below 30–40 ng/mL without supplementation effectively wastes the investment: the body has the signal to build hemoglobin but lacks the substrate. A blood test measuring ferritin and transferrin saturation four weeks before the camp is the cheapest step in the entire preparation. The transition between the heat block and the altitude camp also requires two to three days of easy active recovery — Z1, no thermal load — to manage the change in stimulus without accumulating fatigue.

## Practical Application for the Tour or Vuelta

The July grand tour (Tour de France) and the August edition (Vuelta a España) expose riders to two simultaneous demands: mountain stages require maximum oxygen-carrying capacity, while the transition stages through Provence, Andalusia or Murcia impose temperatures that can exceed 40 °C on the road surface. Optimal preparation needs both layers: the hematological layer for the summits and the thermoregulatory layer for surviving the hot days.

Nybo, Rønnestad and Lundby, in their 2024 review in the *Scandinavian Journal of Medicine & Science in Sports* (PMID: 36350277), propose an 8–12-week arc before the first stage that integrates both stimuli with sequential logic. Heat first (two weeks) builds the thermoregulatory adaptations with the longest decay timeline and primes the base for the altitude block. The altitude camp (3–4 weeks at 2,000–2,500 m) maximizes tHbmass. Post-altitude heat sessions preserve that tHbmass while the cyclist enters the taper phase. The optimal performance window — peak tHbmass, functional heat adaptations, rested organism — typically opens 7 to 14 days after returning from altitude when the thermal maintenance protocol has been followed.

For an amateur cyclist preparing a summer gran fondo or multi-day event, the protocol simplifies without losing its sequential logic: two weeks on the trainer in heat (no fan, 60–75 minutes at Z2, five sessions per week), followed by a mountain vacation at 2,000–2,500 metres for two to three weeks — with whatever physical activity is possible — and then two to three weekly thermal maintenance sessions in the final phase. It is not a WorldTour team protocol, but it follows the same logic the science supports.

## What the Research Has Not Yet Resolved

The most obvious gap in the literature is the absence of a controlled trial directly comparing the heat → altitude sequence against the altitude → heat sequence within the same group of cyclists, with matched performance outcomes. Girard, Peeling, Racinais and Périard flagged this explicitly in their 2024 review: sequential exposure in either order "warrants further investigation." The existing data come from studies that did not design that direct comparison but suggest it as an inference from their findings.

What is established is the guiding principle: it is not possible to extract the best of both stimuli simultaneously. The physiology of heat adaptation and the physiology of hypoxic adaptation act on the same systems — blood volume, erythropoiesis, oxygen transport — with signals that compete when applied in parallel. Sequence, dose and temporal separation are not logistical details. They are the protocol itself.
