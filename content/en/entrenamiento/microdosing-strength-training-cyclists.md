## The Most Common In-Season Mistake: Choosing Between the Bike and the Gym

A 45-minute strength session on Tuesday, two days before Saturday's race, generates 36 to 72 hours of measurable neuromuscular disruption. That cost is not a problem during the off-season, when the goal is exactly that deep stimulus and recovery has sufficient time. But in the middle of a competitive season, that toll interferes directly with the quality of key sessions on the bike. Most cyclists resolve this contradiction in the worst possible way: they abandon the gym from the first spring race until the end of the autumn season.

The evidence does not support that decision. Bent Rønnestad and Íñigo Mujika reviewed in 2014 in *Scandinavian Journal of Medicine & Science in Sports* (PMID: 23914932) the literature on optimizing strength training for endurance performance and concluded that stimulus frequency — more than session volume — is the primary modulator of adaptation retention during the competitive season. Dropping strength training entirely reverses a portion of the pedaling economy gains built in preseason within 4 to 6 weeks. The right question is not whether to do strength in-season, but how to do it without competing with the cycling sessions that actually determine race results.

## What a Strength Microdose Actually Means

A strength microdose is an 8 to 15-minute session of real work — not commuting to the gym, a 10-minute warmup, or isolation machines — repeated four to six days a week. The load sits between 65% and 80% of 1RM for compound movements: enough to generate a relevant neuromuscular stimulus, not enough to accumulate the residual fatigue that lasts 48 hours. Volume per session is 2 to 3 sets per exercise, with one or two main movements. What changes compared to the traditional model is not the total weekly volume but its distribution: instead of 16 sets of muscular work concentrated in two days, the cyclist does 3 sets of goblet squats on Monday, 3 sets of hip thrusts on Tuesday, 3 sets of Romanian deadlifts on Wednesday, and so on throughout the week.

Afonso, Nakamura, Baptista and colleagues analyzed the current state of exercise microdosing in 2022 in the *International Journal of Sports Physiology and Performance* (PMID: 36202386) and stated the premise precisely: distributing the same weekly strength volume in smaller, higher-frequency doses produces equivalent or superior adaptations compared to concentrated sessions, with a drastically lower individual recovery cost per session. Iversen, Norum, Schoenfeld and Fimland reinforced this in 2021 in *Sports Medicine* (PMID: 34125411): total weekly volume is the primary predictor of muscular adaptation, and that volume can be distributed across multiple formats without loss of efficacy. Five 10-minute sessions are equivalent in cumulative muscle protein synthesis to one 50-minute weekly session — and are far more compatible with a competitive training load on the bike.

## The Interference Effect: Better Than It Looks for Cyclists

The interference effect — the mutual attenuation of adaptations when strength and endurance training are combined — is real but context-dependent. Wilson and colleagues analyzed 21 studies in a 2012 meta-analysis in the *Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research* (PMID: 22002517) and found a critical pattern: interference is significant when endurance training involves running, but not when it involves cycling. Pedaling generates neither the mechanical impact nor the eccentric demands of running, which drastically reduces interference on strength adaptations. For cyclists, concurrent training is far less problematic than the general interference literature would suggest.

The practical implication: a cyclist can do 10 minutes of goblet squats before getting on the trainer without significantly compromising either session. What does generate interference is excessive volume — a 90-minute Z4-Z5 interval session followed by 40 minutes of heavy lifting saturates the molecular signaling mechanisms that regulate muscle protein synthesis. The microdose operates precisely below that saturation threshold: each session activates protein synthesis receptors without depleting glycogen stores or elevating muscle damage markers to levels that compromise recovery over the next 24 to 36 hours.

## Rønnestad and the Minimum That Maintains Everything

Bent Rønnestad demonstrated in a controlled 2010 study in the *European Journal of Applied Physiology* (PMID: 20799042) that one weekly heavy strength session during the first 13 weeks of the competitive season — following a 12-week off-season strength block — was sufficient to fully maintain gains in lactate threshold power, pedaling economy, and mean time trial power. The group that abandoned strength training at the start of the season lost a significant portion of those gains within 8 weeks. A single weekly maintenance session was enough to preserve them for 13 full weeks of competition.

The microdose approach not only reaches that minimum — it exceeds it comfortably. Five 10-minute sessions accumulate, in total weekly muscular work, roughly double what a traditional maintenance session produces, but without the fatigue spike of an isolated 40-minute session. The logic mirrors Rønnestad's 30/30 intervals in cycling: the same total load distributed in smaller pulses generates more time in the effective stimulus zone with less residual fatigue accumulation. Rønnestad and Mujika noted in their 2014 review that stimulus frequency — not the magnitude of each isolated session — is the key modulator of adaptation retention during periods of high competitive load.

## The Four Exercises That Work in Microdose Format

In a 10-minute window, exercise selection must be ruthlessly efficient. The goblet squat — with a kettlebell or dumbbell of 10 to 30 kg depending on level, full depth, 3 sets of 8 to 10 repetitions — engages quadriceps, glutes, and core in an integrated pattern requiring no barbell or rack. The hip thrust with a barbell or dumbbells resting on a bench is the most pedaling-specific exercise for the gluteus maximus, the primary engine of hip extension in the pedal stroke: 3 sets of 10 repetitions at 60 to 120 kg depending on level, with 60-second recovery between sets. The Romanian deadlift with dumbbells targets hamstrings and glutes in the eccentric range most weakened in cyclists who log many hours in the saddle: 3 sets of 8 repetitions with 15 to 30 kg per hand.

The fourth component is cycling-specific core work: weighted plank with lateral weight transfer (3×30 seconds), pallof press with resistance band (3×12 repetitions per side), or loaded dead bug (3×10 per side). Core training does not produce direct propulsive force — it transfers the force generated by legs and glutes to the pedal. A cyclist with a weak trunk loses power through the kinetic chain even with strong quadriceps, particularly during out-of-saddle efforts. Rønnestad, Hansen and colleagues in *Scandinavian Journal of Medicine & Science in Sports* (PMID: 24862305) found that programs combining posterior chain exercises with core stability work produced greater improvements in preferred cadence and per-pedal power distribution than leg-only protocols.

## How to Schedule It in a Race Week

The fundamental rule: no leg microdoses within 18 to 24 hours before a Z4-Z5 interval session or a race. Core work carries no such restriction and can be trained virtually any day with controlled volume. Leg microdoses go on Z2 base days, active recovery days, or in the first minutes of the morning on easy days. In a typical competition week with a Saturday race:

| Sunday | Z1 recovery | Core | 8 min | Plank 3×30s + Pallof 3×12 | Z1 (<55% FTP) |

## How to Progress and What Success Looks Like

The microdose is not static. In the first week, 2 sets per exercise per session establish the pattern without generating accumulated fatigue. By week three, add the third set. By week five, add a second exercise to the longest session of the week — typically Tuesday's, which coincides with the longest Z2 day and the furthest temporal distance from the next hard session. Load should increase when all prescribed repetitions are completed with more than 2 reps in reserve; for compound leg exercises, a 2.5 to 5 kg increase every two weeks during the season is realistic with a daily stimulus.

The signal that the system is working is not post-session muscular soreness — which should not exist with this format — but FTP stability across the entire season. The cyclist who holds threshold power in July at the same level as in April, without loss of pedaling economy or chronic heavy-leg sensation, is preserving the neuromuscular adaptations built in winter. The warning signal is an FTP drop greater than 3 to 5% between June and August without clear aerobic volume cause: it almost always means microdose frequency was insufficient for the weekly aerobic training load, or that the load was too low to generate a real stimulus. A power meter and a systematic log of strength sessions — even 10-minute ones — are the only instruments needed to catch that problem before it becomes irreversible in-season performance loss.
