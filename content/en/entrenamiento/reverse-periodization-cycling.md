In 2022, a systematic review published in *Sports Medicine Open* pulled together 11 studies, 200 endurance athletes and one direct question: does reverse periodization produce better outcomes than conventional training models? The answer, documented by González-Ravé and colleagues (PMID 35445953), was no — at least not consistently or with statistical significance across any of the measured variables: swimming performance, running performance, muscular endurance, or VO2max. That puts the debate about this model in honest territory: there's no evidence elevating it above traditional or block periodization, but no evidence ruling it out either as a workable approach for specific types of cyclists.

Traditional periodization, formalized by Soviet scientist Lev Matveyev in the 1960s, follows a sequence most coaches still recognize: first build aerobic volume at low intensity, then gradually introduce intensity, then peak for competition. During autumn and winter, the classic model fills the training week with Zone 1 and Zone 2 riding (55-75% of FTP), where the body develops mitochondrial density, fat oxidation efficiency and the capillary network needed to sustain hard efforts weeks later. The recurring metaphor in training literature — laying foundations before raising walls — has real physiological support, though Kiely (2012, PMID 22356774) noted in *International Journal of Sports Physiology and Performance* that much of periodization's internal logic rests on tradition and empirical observation rather than randomized controlled trials.

## What exactly gets reversed

Reverse periodization takes that framework and flips it. Instead of starting autumn with long, easy rides, the model proposes beginning with high intensity and low volume: Zone 4 and Zone 5 intervals (91-120% of FTP), structured sessions of 60-90 minutes, and a weekly volume of 6-10 hours that in the traditional model would correspond to peaking mesocycles, not base-building ones. As the season progresses and spring brings longer days and better weather, volume is added progressively until the athlete carries the load needed to perform at race level.

The practical argument behind this model's growing popularity has less to do with physiology than with the reality of winter training. A cyclist riding on a smart trainer or Zwift during the cold months can complete a 75-minute session of 4×8 minutes at Zone 4 without much trouble, but rarely finishes the four-hour easy ride that the base block of a classic model demands. Mujika et al. (2018, PMID 29848161) argued in *International Journal of Sports Physiology and Performance* that effective periodization must integrate the athlete's real conditions — available time, environment, equipment access — before selecting a theoretical model. A plan that never gets executed produces no adaptation, no matter how physiologically sound it looks on paper.

## The evidence: no clear winner

The González-Ravé et al. (2022) review is the most comprehensive reference currently available, and its limitations matter as much as its findings. Of the 11 studies analyzed, most used swimmers or runners as subjects; direct evidence in cyclists is sparse and methodologically heterogeneous. The study closest to the amateur cyclist profile comes from Clemente-Suárez and Ramos-Campo (2019, PMID 31390818), who compared reverse versus traditional linear periodization in amateur triathletes over a 10-week training program: both groups improved swimming and running performance to a similar degree, with no statistically significant differences on the physiological variables measured.

Issurin (2016, PMID 26573916) showed in *Sports Medicine* that block periodization — a different model from reverse periodization, though both concentrate stimuli — can offer advantages over traditional training in experienced athletes by generating sharper adaptation peaks. Stöggl and Sperlich (2014, DOI 10.3389/fphys.2014.00033) demonstrated in 41 well-trained endurance athletes who completed the 9-week protocol that polarized training — a high proportion of Zone 1 combined with Zone 5 work, without excessive time in Zone 3 — improved VO2max and peak power more than threshold training, high-intensity training, or high-volume uniform training. This evidence is relevant to reverse periodization because both approaches work at the intensity extremes, but they are different strategies: polarized describes a weekly intensity distribution, while reverse periodization describes an annual sequence.

The honest bottom line: reverse periodization has not been shown to be superior to traditional periodization in cycling, but it has not been shown to be inferior either. Its usefulness depends on the cyclist's context, not on any intrinsic physiological advantage the available evidence has failed to establish.

## Who this model actually fits

Reverse periodization makes its strongest case for time-crunched cyclists during winter: those who work full-time, have family commitments and can only train 60-90 minutes on weekdays, but can't carve out the 3-4 hour rides that classic base-building requires. In that context, 75 minutes of structured high-quality intervals on a trainer extracts far more value from the available time than a 90-minute easy ride that never accumulates enough volume to drive meaningful mitochondrial adaptations. The cyclist who has access to long outdoor weekend rides in winter — with favorable weather, safe roads and enough free time — gains nothing from flipping the order.

It also makes sense for cyclists targeting criteriums, short time trials (30-45 minutes) or circuit races with high power demands and repeated accelerations. These events require the ability to produce high power repeatedly — something Zone 4 and Zone 5 work in winter develops directly — rather than the sustained multi-hour endurance that a gran fondo or long mountain stage demands. A cyclist preparing for any event longer than three hours in mountainous terrain needs the aerobic base that the traditional or polarized model builds during winter; applying reverse periodization to that objective is a diagnostic error, not a stylistic preference.

The third profile that can benefit is the cyclist in environments where winter physically limits long outdoor rides: heavy urban traffic, extreme cold, or lack of safe cycling infrastructure. Those riding exclusively on a smart trainer for three to four winter months have an excellent tool for structured intensity but a psychologically demanding one for sessions beyond 90-120 minutes. Adapting periodization to the tools actually available is smarter than planning as though unlimited clear road access exists.

## Designing a reverse macrocycle: six-month example

The following plan assumes an amateur cyclist with an FTP of 230 W, 7-10 weekly hours available during winter and a goal of peaking for criteriums between May and July.

| Month | Weekly Volume | Dominant Zones | Block Objective |
|-------|--------------|----------------|-----------------|
| October | 6-8h | Zone 4-5 (60% of time) | Activate high-intensity capacity; calibrate load |
| November | 7-9h | Zone 4 sustained + Zone 5 once/week | Consolidate anaerobic power in short sessions |
| December | 8-11h | Zone 3-4 + Zone 5 once/week | Introduce threshold; maintain intensity ceiling |
| January | 10-13h | Zone 2-3 growing + Zone 4 twice/week | Aerobic volume growth without dropping quality |
| February | 12-15h | Wide Zone 2 + Zone 4-5 blocks | Aerobic base with intensity preservation |
| March | 13-17h | Zone 2 + competition-specific | Race form; opener criteriums |

A typical November week in this macrocycle:

| Day | Session | Duration | Zone/Intensity | Description |
|-----|---------|----------|----------------|-------------|
| Monday | Rest | — | — | Optional active recovery (mobility, walking) |
| Tuesday | Zone 4 intervals | 75 min | Z4 (91-105% FTP) | 4×8 min; 4 min Zone 1 recovery between efforts |
| Wednesday | Recovery | 45 min | Z1 (<55% FTP) | Easy spin; full rest day if residual fatigue is high |
| Thursday | Over-unders | 90 min | Z3-Z4 (85-105% FTP) | 3×10 min alternating 2 min Z4 / 3 min Z3 |
| Friday | Rest | — | — | — |
| Saturday | Tempo | 100 min | Z3 (76-90% FTP) | 2×20 min Zone 3; remainder in Zone 2 |
| Sunday | Aerobic | 90 min | Z2 (55-75% FTP) | Steady ride; do not exceed 75% FTP |

## Real limitations and warning signs

Reverse periodization has a concrete physiological problem the literature has not resolved: if the cyclist doesn't incorporate enough low-intensity aerobic work during the model's early months, the capacity to oxidize fat and sustain prolonged efforts can decline. High-intensity Zone 4 and Zone 5 work is metabolically demanding and requires prior mitochondrial capacity; if that capacity is low because it was never developed or allowed to atrophy, the efficiency of that high-intensity work diminishes in a compounding way. The evidence reviewed by González-Ravé et al. (2022) suggests that the reverse model may underestimate the importance of aerobic base work, which improves exercise economy through adaptations that high-intensity training does not replicate in the same manner.

The overreaching risk at the start of the cycle is meaningfully higher in the reverse model than in traditional periodization. Beginning the training year with weeks of intense intervals without prior aerobic base can stress the musculoskeletal system and increase the probability of overuse injuries, particularly at the knee and patellar tendon. Warning signs to monitor closely include: unexplained drop in power output on sessions that previously felt manageable, resting heart rate elevated above habitual baseline by five or more beats per minute across several consecutive days, muscle fatigue that doesn't clear after 48 hours of rest, and sustained irritability or sleep disruption.

This model is not compatible with preparing for long-distance events. A cyclist aiming to complete a 180- or 200-km gran fondo, or any event lasting more than four hours in demanding terrain, needs the aerobic base that the traditional model — or the polarized approach covered in a separate analysis — builds across the winter. The ability to maintain moderate power output for many hours depends on adaptations that only sustained Zone 1 and Zone 2 volume can develop robustly, and that is precisely the quality reverse periodization trades away in its early phases to develop others.

One final limitation that no controlled study measures but every experienced coach knows: doing high-intensity intervals alone on a trainer in a garage or spare room through the dark months of November and December — without the energy of a group ride or the landscape that offsets effort — is psychologically more demanding than long easy riding. A technically sound model that fails to sustain week-to-week execution has no practical value. Adherence to training — continuing to execute the plan when motivation drops — carries as much weight as any physiological variable, and it's the factor controlled studies consistently fail to capture.
