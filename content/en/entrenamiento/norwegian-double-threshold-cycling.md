## The moment triathlon entered the peloton

On November 1, 2024, Olav Aleksander Bu signed on as performance director of Uno-X Mobility, the Norwegian World Tour team. Bu is the coach who turned Kristian Blummenfelt into an Olympic champion in Tokyo 2021 and Gustav Iden into a long-distance world champion. The central methodological tool he used in both cases is called double threshold, and it now has its first real test on 700c road wheels inside the professional peloton.

Bu's arrival was neither subtle nor quiet. Thor Hushovd, the team's general manager and former world cycling champion, announced it directly: "The idea is that he comes in with new and fresh eyes and sees what can be done differently. He has a different kind of experience and is an extremely smart person." Bu set the tone from the start: "A double threshold session is only a tool in the coach's toolbox. What I do here is look at what tools we can use to take the next step. That might be double threshold for some riders in some applications, but not for others."

## Marius Bakken: the physician who measured his own lactate 5,500 times

Double threshold did not originate in an academic laboratory. It was built by Marius Bakken, a former Norwegian runner who holds the national 5,000-meter record at 13:06, out of a personal crisis: between 1996 and 1998, training under Peter Coe — father of Sebastian Coe — his performance plateaued. Bakken began pricking his own earlobe every few minutes during training sessions, accumulating more than 5,500 lactate measurements across his career. The finding was so counterintuitive that it took years to convince other coaches.

The Norwegian Athletics Federation and the Olympiatoppen institute launched a lactate monitoring program for elite runners in 1998 that confirmed what Bakken was observing in himself: the athletes who performed best in competition were not those who accumulated the most work at very high intensities, but those who accumulated the most work in a moderate band without ever exceeding it. The anaerobic threshold — the frontier between what the body can sustain and what destabilizes it — was the boundary that needed to be repeatedly approached without crossing.

## What double threshold actually is

The structure is as simple to describe as it is demanding to execute. Twice a week, the athlete trains twice a day with six to eight hours between sessions: one morning session and one afternoon session. Each session consists of intervals whose intensity is controlled through lactate tests taken every one to three repetitions. The target is to keep blood lactate between 2.0 and 4.5 mmol/L — never above that range — throughout the entire work block. In cycling terms, that corresponds roughly to Z3 (76-90% FTP) at the lower end and the onset of Z4 (91-105% FTP) as the absolute ceiling, although precise values depend on each athlete's individual biochemistry.

The morning session uses longer intervals, typically six to ten minutes, which prime the muscle tissue with a moderate stimulus before deep acidosis sets in. The afternoon session uses shorter intervals — between 45 seconds and three minutes — faster within the same lactate band. Bakken describes the whole as "one long session with a functional rest break in the middle": the gap between sessions is not complete recovery, but a pause that allows lactate to drop and the central nervous system to rebalance without letting the muscular adaptation stimulus go cold.

## Three models, three distinct philosophies

To understand double threshold, it must be placed alongside the two paradigms that dominate professional endurance training. The polarized model — formulated by physiologist Stephen Seiler after analyzing the training diaries of hundreds of elite athletes (Seiler, 2010, PMID 20861519) — proposes that around 80% of total volume is performed at low intensity (Z1-Z2, below 2 mmol/L lactate) and the remaining 20% at high intensity (Z5-Z6, above the anaerobic threshold). The middle zone — between 2 and 4 mmol/L — is deliberately minimized because it accumulates fatigue without delivering the adaptation benefits of the extreme zones. Stöggl and Sperlich (2014, PMID 24550842) experimentally confirmed that the polarized model produces superior improvements in VO2max and movement economy compared to pure threshold or pure high-volume training.

Sweet-spot — the second dominant model in amateur and semi-professional cycling — targets a power equivalent to 88-93% FTP, with the rationale of accumulating a sufficient stimulus without reaching the exhaustion of high-zone intervals. The problem is that FTP as a reference point does not anchor real metabolic intensity: a cyclist with an unfavorable metabolic profile can exceed 5-6 mmol/L working at 90% FTP, which technically places them in high-intensity territory with all its recovery consequences.

Double threshold abandons power-based zones as the primary reference. The control variable is lactate measured in real time. If concentration rises above 4.5 mmol/L, the athlete reduces effort or ends the block. This precision comes at a cost: it requires a portable lactate meter, reactive test strips, and the discipline to stop mid-interval for a finger-prick.

## The available scientific evidence

Casado, Foster, Bakken, and Tjelta published in 2023 in the *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health* (PMID 36900796) the most comprehensive review of the method's physiological basis. The authors propose that lactate-guided sub-threshold training simultaneously activates the AMPK and intracellular calcium signaling pathways — the two main triggers of mitochondrial biogenesis. During high-intensity training, the AMPK pathway saturates rapidly and the cell enters a stress state that inhibits protein synthesis for several hours. In double threshold, the intensity is high enough to activate both pathways, but moderate enough that the adaptation process is not interrupted by acidosis. The result, the authors argue, is that the athlete can repeat the stimulus twice in the same day and four to six times per week without the fatigue accumulation that would accompany an equivalent volume of high-intensity work.

Tønnessen, Sandbakk, Seiler, and Haugen (2024, PMID 39012575), in a review published in *Sports Medicine*, documented that half of elite endurance coaches in Nordic sports have already adopted double threshold sessions in their weekly programs. The figure is significant because a decade ago the method was considered an eccentricity of Bakken's; today it is standard practice in Norwegian distance running and is expanding into cross-country skiing, biathlon, and now cycling.

What the scientific literature has not yet resolved is whether the benefits of double threshold measurably surpass those of the polarized model in high-performance cyclists. Available studies are primarily descriptive — documenting what champions do — or apply to distance running, where the mechanics of muscular fatigue differ from cycling. Bu himself has been careful on this point: "We are analyzing cycling as it is now and looking at where the most obvious next steps are."

## Uno-X as a real-world laboratory

Uno-X Mobility has spent several seasons building a methodologically distinct identity within the World Tour. The team already had an active performance department before Bu arrived, and the Norwegian performance director has said publicly that he does not intend to dismantle what was working. What he is integrating, according to reports from Velo and TRI247, is a systematic analysis of which tools from the Norwegian method have direct transfer to road cycling performance. Double threshold, with its requirement of daily double sessions, collides with the reality of professional cycling: a World Tour cyclist races between 70 and 85 competition days per year, fragmenting the training calendar in ways that distance running never encounters.

Uno-X's bet makes sense from another angle. The Norwegian team competes with a smaller budget than the top World Tour giants — UAE Team Emirates, Visma-Lease a Bike, Ineos — and needs to extract performance from its riders through methodology rather than through marquee signings. If double threshold raises a rider's physiological ceiling without increasing high-risk training load, the cost-benefit argument is solid.

## Practical application for amateur cyclists: real requirements and real limits

Textbook double threshold requires at least three resources that most amateur cyclists do not have: a portable lactate meter with reactive strips (the most commonly used models in sport, such as the Lactate Scout or Lactate Pro 2, cost between 300 and 500 euros plus consumables), enough time to train twice a day, and an aerobic base already consolidated over at least eight to twelve months of predominantly Z1-Z2 work. Without that base, the mitochondrial system is not prepared to process the repeated double threshold stimulus without accumulating chronic fatigue.

For the amateur cyclist without a lactate meter, an approximate version of the method exists based on breathing control. The target intensity of double threshold corresponds to a state in which short phrases — four or five words — can be spoken but a flowing conversation cannot be sustained. Once breathing prevents completing those phrases, intensity has exceeded the method's boundary. It is an imprecise proxy, but in practice it places the cyclist in a lactate range far closer to the Norwegian target than riding at 90% FTP without any measurement.

The warning signs of overtraining with double threshold are quieter than with high-intensity training. The first to appear is typically lactate drift: the cyclist maintains the same power as the previous week, but the meter reads 0.5-1.0 mmol/L higher than usual. This is followed by an inability to hold power through the final repetitions of each set, then by resting morning heart rate elevated one to two beats above the weekly average. When two of these signals appear simultaneously, the correct response is not one rest day — it is a full week of unloading, with volume cut to 50% and no double sessions.

## Questions that remain open

Double threshold has been refined over thirty years in Norwegian athletics and has had formal presence in competitive cycling for barely twelve months. It is still unknown whether the adaptations it produces — primarily more efficient mitochondria and greater capacity to buffer lactate at sub-maximal intensities — have the same magnitude in cyclists as in distance runners, given that cycling demands repeated explosive efforts and pace changes that endurance running does not encounter in the same way. The zone where double threshold accumulates its work is precisely the zone where race attacks and hard climbs do not occur.

Bu has made clear that he intends to resolve those questions with data, not dogma. "There are still low-hanging fruits that are out there to be grabbed," he said when announcing his move to Uno-X. If Bakken needed nearly a decade to convince Norwegian athletics, it is reasonable that cycling will need at least two or three seasons to know whether the method travels well on two wheels.
