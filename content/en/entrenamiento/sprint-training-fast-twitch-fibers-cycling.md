## The Variable Everyone Ignores Until They Need It

Six-second peak power is the parameter that endurance cyclists most systematically neglect when designing their training. Two cyclists with 320 watts of FTP can have peak powers of 900 and 1,200 watts respectively — they climb the same mountains, hold the same pace in Z3-Z4 (76-105% FTP), weigh nearly the same. But the first cannot answer the 8-second attack with which the second opens 30 meters at the decisive moment. The difference doesn't appear on a trainer with threshold metrics: it shows in the final 3 kilometers of a race, in covering a split before it closes, in the sprint after a short cobblestone wall.

Peak sprint power can be trained systematically. Evidence from the Rønnestad group demonstrates that doing so with the right protocols does not destroy VO2max, does not compromise pedaling economy, and does not reduce the capacity to sustain power in Z3-Z4. The key is effort duration and recovery amplitude between repetitions.

## The Fibers That Produce and Limit Sprint Power

Type IIx fibers are the fastest in human skeletal muscle: they produce force at a rate up to four times greater than type I fibers and contain high concentrations of phosphocreatine, the substrate that fuels the first 6 to 10 seconds of any maximal effort. During a 6-second all-out sprint, ATP resynthesis depends 85 to 90% on the phosphocreatine system and 10 to 15% on rapid anaerobic glycolysis. Type IIx fibers generate 80 to 120 watts per kilogram of active muscle during those seconds, but become completely depleted in 10 to 15 seconds and require 3 to 5 minutes of passive or Z1 recovery to replenish 85 to 95% of phosphocreatine stores.

Type IIa fibers are the functional compromise: faster and more powerful than type I, more fatigue-resistant than type IIx, capable of sustaining high power for 30 to 90 seconds. In a trained endurance cyclist, the typical vastus lateralis distribution is 55 to 65% type I fibers, 25 to 35% type IIa, and less than 10% type IIx. Plotkin, Roberts, Haun and Schoenfeld reviewed fiber type transition mechanisms in *Sports (Basel)* in 2021 (PMID: 34564332) and confirmed that sprint training can partially reverse the endurance training trend toward IIx→IIa→I transition, increasing the functional proportion of IIa fibers by 8 to 17 percentage points after 6 to 8 weeks of specific protocol.

## The Two Protocols with Experimental Support in Cyclists

The short maximal sprint protocol is the most studied and most transferable for endurance cyclists: 5 to 10-second efforts at absolute maximum — the power meter reading 800 to 1,800 watts depending on level — with active recovery of 3 to 5 minutes at Z1 (&lt;55% FTP). Between 6 and 10 repetitions per session. This format maximizes the stimulus on IIa and IIx fibers without generating substantial lactate accumulation, because the effort duration is insufficient to activate glycolysis in significant volume. The ample recovery allows near-complete phosphocreatine resynthesis before the next sprint.

The supramaximal medium-duration protocol — 20 to 30-second efforts at 130 to 150% of FTP — adds a glycolytic component that raises blood lactate to 8 to 14 mmol/L per repetition and requires longer recoveries of 4 to 6 minutes. It produces a more complete stimulus for total anaerobic capacity, working both the phosphocreatine system and lactate production and buffering capacity. The drawback is greater residual fatigue per session and the requirement for at least 48 hours before any quality aerobic session. For most endurance cyclists, the 6 to 10-second protocol is the more applicable: direct peak power benefit with minimal recovery cost.

## Rønnestad's Transition Period Experiment

Almquist, Rønnestad and colleagues published in 2020 in *Frontiers in Physiology* (PMID: 33041839) results of including 30-second sprints in one weekly low-intensity session during a 3-week transition period in elite cyclists with VO2max of 72 ± 5 ml·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹. The sprint group protocol: 3 sets of 3 × 30-second maximal repetitions embedded in a Z1-Z2 session of identical total duration to the control group. The result was a 4% improvement in mean 30-second sprint power in the sprint group, versus a performance decline in the low-intensity-only group.

The follow-up study, published in *International Journal of Sports Physiology and Performance* in 2021 (PMID: 33819914), measured both groups 6 weeks into the subsequent preparatory period, with no differences in training plan between groups during those 6 weeks. The group that had included sprints during the transition improved mean power in a 20-minute all-out test by 7.3%, versus a −1.3% decline in the control group. That 8.6-percentage-point difference without any subsequent training difference indicates that transition-period sprints build a neuromuscular base that amplifies the response to subsequent aerobic training.

## Short Sprints vs. Heavy Strength: What Each Protocol Develops

In a controlled study published in *Frontiers in Physiology* in 2019 (PMID: 31555153), Kristoffersen, Sandbakk, Rønnestad and Gundersen compared 6 weeks of sprint training (SST) versus heavy strength training (HST) in 28 competitive cyclists following a common 4-week adaptation period. The SST group improved 6-second peak power in fresh state by 4.7% and in fatigued state after 185 minutes of cycling by 6.1%, versus 1.1% and 1.8% for the HST group. Mean 30-second power improved 3.7% in SST versus 1.3% in HST, and the 55-meter seated sprint from standstill improved 4.3% in SST versus a non-significant 0.2% in HST. The heavy strength group was superior in half-squat 1RM (+9.3% vs −3.9%), but that maximal strength gain did not translate into greater sprint-specific power than sprint training itself.

## Combining Sprints with an Aerobic Base Without Losing Either

Wilson and colleagues demonstrated in their 2012 meta-analysis (PMID: 22002517) that concurrent training interference on power is significantly lower when the endurance modality is cycling rather than running. This means cyclists can add sprint sessions without the level of interference that a triathlete with the same goals would experience. The mechanism is the lower muscle damage per unit of time in cycling versus running, which preserves the signaling mechanisms for power adaptations.

The practical rule is to place sprint sessions on days of moderate or low aerobic volume, never the day before or on the same day as a Z4-Z5 session. Six-second sprints with 4 minutes of Z1 recovery do not accumulate enough lactate to compromise the aerobic adaptations of the week's remaining Z2 volume: post-sprint lactate from a 6-second effort does not exceed 4 to 5 mmol/L and is cleared completely in the first 10 to 12 minutes of easy pedaling. A 6-week block with two weekly sessions of 6 to 8 sprints of 6 to 10 seconds does not reduce VO2max or threshold power when the aerobic base volume in Z2 is held constant.

## Six-Week Protocol and Expected Gains

| Day | Session | Duration | Zone/Intensity | Description |
|-----|---------|----------|----------------|-------------|
| Monday | Rest | — | — | Full recovery |
| Tuesday | Sprint + Z2 | 75 min | Z1-Z6 | 20 min warmup Z1 + 6×6s maximal (4 min Z1 rec) + 35 min Z2 |
| Wednesday | Z2 base | 90-120 min | Z2 (55-75% FTP) | Aerobic endurance, free cadence |
| Thursday | Z3-Z4 threshold | 75 min | Z3-Z4 (76-105% FTP) | 3×12 min Z3-Z4, 5 min Z1 rec between sets |
| Friday | Rest or Z1 | 45 min | Z1 (&lt;55% FTP) | Easy active recovery pedaling |
| Saturday | Sprint + Z2 | 85 min | Z1-Z6 | 20 min warmup + 8×6s maximal (4 min Z1 rec) + 40 min Z2 |
| Sunday | Long Z2 | 120-180 min | Z2 (55-75% FTP) | Long aerobic endurance, no intensity, free cadence |

A well-executed 6-week block produces 3 to 7% improvements in 5 to 6-second peak power in recreational and competitive cyclists. In elite cyclists the margin is 1 to 4%, because the starting baseline is already high. Total anaerobic capacity, measured with the 30-second Wingate test, improves 4 to 8% over the same period. The follow-up case report by Rønnestad published in *Frontiers in Sports and Active Living* in 2022 (PMID: 35548458) documented two elite cyclists who incorporated systematic sprint training across two seasons, increasing sprint power by 16% in the first season and fully maintaining those gains through the competitive period — indicating that type IIa fiber adaptations are durable if the stimulus is not interrupted.

## The Signal That Confirms the Block Is Working

The power drop between the first and last sprint of each session is the most reliable control marker. If sprint 8's power is more than 8% below sprint 1's power with the same perceived effort, either the 4-minute recovery was insufficient for phosphocreatine resynthesis or preceding sprint intensity was too high. The correction is to extend recovery to 5 minutes or reduce repetitions before gradually scaling up in subsequent weeks. Morning resting heart rate elevated more than 5 beats above baseline for three consecutive days signals systemic fatigue accumulation: that week's sprint session should be replaced with 60 minutes of pure Z2 and reintroduced the following week at reduced volume. Fast-twitch fiber adaptations build over weeks; the overtraining that erases them takes only days.
